# "Comfort ye my people": a study guide to Isaiah 40–49

Come, Follow Me, September 28–October 4, 2026

## How to use this guide

This guide is meant for adults who want to read Isaiah slowly. Each chapter section has the same five parts: the chapter's setting in the sequence, close readings of specific verses, connections to Jesus Christ with the textual reasons for them, cross-references, and questions. Most of the work is reading the King James text closely. Secondary sources are used sparingly and named when they appear.

The guide tries to keep three kinds of statement separate:

- **What the text states.** Isaiah's words, quoted from the King James Version and cited by verse.
- **What a commentator argues.** Each of these is attributed in the sentence and linked.
- **Proposed devotional application.** These are marked as application. They are offered for reflection, not as the meaning the text requires.

## Historical orientation

Isaiah 39 ends with a warning. After King Hezekiah shows his treasures to envoys from Babylon, Isaiah tells him that "all that is in thine house... shall be carried to Babylon" and that some of his descendants will serve in the Babylonian palace ([Isaiah 39:6–7](https://www.churchofjesuschrist.org/study/scriptures/ot/isa/39?lang=eng)). Then the voice changes. Chapter 40 opens with "Comfort ye, comfort ye my people." Judgment has dominated much of the book up to this point. Now the speech is addressed to a people living after the catastrophe.

The historical frame is approximately the following:

- **Late 600s BC.** Babylon replaced Assyria as the dominant power.
- **597 and 586 BC.** Babylonian armies deported Judah's leaders. In 586 they destroyed Jerusalem and Solomon's temple.
- **539 BC.** Cyrus the Persian took Babylon.
- **Soon after.** His decree allowed exiles to return and rebuild the temple ([Ezra 1:1–4](https://www.churchofjesuschrist.org/study/scriptures/ot/ezra/1?lang=eng); compare 2 Chronicles 36:22–23).

Isaiah 40–49 speaks to people facing that reality. Their city is in ruins. They live under a foreign empire with impressive gods and processions. Many of them suspect their own God has forgotten them (40:27; 49:14).

Readers handle authorship differently. Many biblical scholars attribute chapters 40–55 to a later prophet writing during the exile. Latter-day Saints have generally read these chapters as Isaiah's prophecy. One reason is that Nephi, around 600 BC, quotes Isaiah 48–49 from the brass plates ([1 Nephi 20–21](https://www.churchofjesuschrist.org/study/scriptures/bofm/1-ne/20?lang=eng)). Readers on either view can agree on the setting the chapters address: exile in Babylon and the hope of return. This guide reads the chapters within that setting first, then follows how later scripture applies them.

The official lesson, [Come, Follow Me—For Home and Church: Old Testament 2026](https://www.churchofjesuschrist.org/study/manual/come-follow-me-for-home-and-church-old-testament-2026/40?lang=eng), frames these chapters as a turn toward comfort for Jews facing the loss of Jerusalem and captivity, and it extends that hope to present readers. The lesson also acknowledges that "servant" passages can refer to Jesus Christ, to Israel, or to Cyrus. It points readers to 1 Nephi 22 to see how Nephi and Jacob likened Isaiah 48–49, and it invites readers to ponder 48:10 alongside 49:13–16.

Taylor Halverson's Interpreter Foundation study, ["'Besides Me There is No Saviour.' Isaiah 40–49"](https://interpreterfoundation.org/res-besides-me-there-is-no-saviour-isaiah-40-49/), reads the block by asking what each portrait teaches about God's character. His examples include God as unique Savior, refiner, shepherd, teacher, and covenant-keeper. That question helps here. These chapters argue about God. The argument centers on whether Israel's God can be trusted when Babylon appears to have won.

### The word "servant" in these chapters

The word "servant" does not have one fixed referent in Isaiah 40–49. Reading the occurrences together prevents flattening them:

| Passage | Who is called servant or commissioned | What the text says |
|---|---|---|
| 41:8–9 | Israel/Jacob | "But thou, Israel, art my servant, Jacob whom I have chosen" |
| 42:1–4 | Unnamed servant | Bears the Spirit and brings judgment to the Gentiles. Matthew applies this passage to Jesus. |
| 42:19 | The blind and deaf servant | "Who is blind, but my servant?" |
| 43:10 | Israel as witnesses | "Ye are my witnesses... and my servant whom I have chosen" |
| 44:1–2, 21 | Jacob/Israel ("Jesurun") | Formed and not forgotten |
| 44:26 | A prophetic servant | God "confirmeth the word of his servant" |
| 44:28–45:1 | Cyrus | "My shepherd" and "his anointed," commissioned "for Jacob my servant's sake" (45:4) |
| 48:20 | Jacob | "The LORD hath redeemed his servant Jacob" |
| 49:1–6 | Servant named "Israel" | Also sent to restore Israel |

Christian reading finds Jesus Christ most clearly in the servant poems, especially 42:1–7 and 49:1–7, because the New Testament reads them that way. That reading does not erase Israel's own vocation. It also does not make Cyrus a type of Christ in any simple sense.

## Isaiah 40: The voice that ends the warfare

### Setting in the sequence

Chapter 40 works as an overture. It introduces the themes the next nine chapters develop:

- comfort after judgment
- a highway home through the wilderness
- the frailty of human power
- the incomparability of God
- God's tenderness as shepherd
- strength for the weary

There is little narrative. The chapter is mostly voices: an unnamed command to comfort (40:1–2), a voice crying in the wilderness (40:3–5), a voice commanding "Cry" (40:6–8), and Zion herself told to proclaim good tidings (40:9–11).

### Close readings

**40:1–2, comfort spoken "to the heart."** The KJV's "Speak ye comfortably to Jerusalem" introduces three declarations. Her "warfare is accomplished," her "iniquity is pardoned," and she "hath received of the LORD's hand double for all her sins." The text frames the exile as covenant judgment. That framing belongs to Israel's prophetic interpretation of a national catastrophe.

The word "double" should not be turned into a precise heavenly ledger in which Jerusalem paid twice what she owed. The text gives no arithmetic. Its point is that the time of judgment is complete and nothing more is outstanding. Isaiah later uses "double" in the opposite direction: "For your shame ye shall have double... everlasting joy shall be unto them" ([Isaiah 61:7](https://www.churchofjesuschrist.org/study/scriptures/ot/isa/61?lang=eng)). The word works as poetic fullness in both places.

*Application:* Some readers may hear 40:2 as permission to treat every personal suffering as payment for sin. The chapter does not ask for that. Here Israel's prophets interpret one specific national exile. Other scripture refuses to make every affliction a sign of guilt (John 9:2–3).

**40:3–5, a highway for God.** The voice does not say "prepare a way for yourselves." It says "Prepare ye the way of the LORD... a highway for our God." The return from exile is pictured as God's own procession. Valleys are lifted and mountains lowered so "the glory of the LORD shall be revealed, and all flesh shall see it together." Exiles who once walked east in chains now see a road leveled for their God to lead them home. The image echoes the Exodus without repeating it: once the way led through the sea, and now it runs across the desert.

**40:6–8, grass and the word.** The second voice says "Cry," and the prophet asks, "What shall I cry?" The answer is unflattering: "All flesh is grass." Babylon is grass. So is Israel ("surely the people is grass"). Only "the word of our God shall stand for ever." This is the chapter's first argument. Empires are seasonal, and God's promise is not.

**40:9–11, strong arm and gentle arm.** Zion becomes the herald: "Behold your God!" The next two verses put two pictures of God's arm side by side.

- In 40:10, "his arm shall rule for him," with strength and reward.
- In 40:11, "he shall gather the lambs with his arm, and carry them in his bosom, and shall gently lead those that are with young."

The arm that rules is the same arm that carries lambs. The text holds power and gentleness together without ranking them.

**40:12–26, the measure of God.** A series of questions follows. "Who hath measured the waters in the hollow of his hand?" The nations are "as a drop of a bucket" (40:15). The inhabitants of the earth are "as grasshoppers" (40:22). In the middle sits a satire of idol-making (40:18–20), in which a craftsman picks wood "that will not rot" so his god "shall not be moved." Isaiah expands that satire in chapter 44.

Against a stable idol stands a God who "bringeth out their host by number: he calleth them all by names" (40:26). In Babylon the stars were treated as divine powers. Here they are an army that answers when God calls the roll.

**40:27–31, the complaint answered.** Now the real problem surfaces: "My way is hid from the LORD, and my judgment is passed over from my God." Everything before this verse has been preparation for answering it. The God who names stars "fainteth not, neither is weary." He "giveth power to the faint."

The final verse presents three movements in a surprising order: mount up "as eagles," "run, and not be weary," "walk, and not faint." One plain reading is that the sequence descends toward the ordinary. Soaring may come seldom. Walking without fainting is the daily form of strength. The text does not say the youths will be spared exhaustion. It says that those who "wait upon the LORD" will "renew their strength."

### Seeing Jesus Christ in the chapter

The New Testament explicitly applies 40:3 to John the Baptist preparing the way for Jesus (Matthew 3:3; Mark 1:3; John 1:23). Luke extends the quotation through "all flesh shall see the salvation of God" ([Luke 3:4–6](https://www.churchofjesuschrist.org/study/scriptures/nt/luke/3?lang=eng)). In Isaiah the way is prepared for "the LORD... our God." So when the Gospels apply it to Jesus, they identify Jesus with the coming of Israel's God.

The shepherd of 40:11 anticipates Jesus's self-description as the good shepherd ([John 10:11](https://www.churchofjesuschrist.org/study/scriptures/nt/john/10?lang=eng)). Peter quotes 40:6–8 and then adds, "this is the word which by the gospel is preached unto you" (1 Peter 1:24–25).

### Cross-references

- **Matthew 3:3, Luke 3:4–6, John 1:23.** These show how early Christians read 40:3–5. They also show John himself accepting the role of the "voice."
- **1 Peter 1:24–25.** This applies the frailty of flesh and the permanence of the word to the gospel message.
- **Psalm 23.** The shepherd imagery is a useful companion to 40:11.
- **Isaiah 61:7.** It shows that "double" can mean abundant blessing as well as fullness of judgment.

### Questions for study and discussion

1. Verse 2 makes three declarations about Jerusalem. Which of them does the chapter develop most, and why might the order matter?
2. Verses 10 and 11 both describe God's arm. What does each picture contribute, and what would be lost if you kept only one?
3. Verse 7 says "surely the people is grass." How does including Israel among the grass change the message from "Babylon will fall" into something more searching?
4. The complaint in 40:27 is answered mostly with statements about who God is, not with an explanation of events. Is that a satisfying answer? Why or why not?
5. In what kinds of experiences is walking without fainting harder than mounting up as eagles?

## Isaiah 41: The courtroom of the nations and the servant who is held

### Setting in the sequence

Chapter 41 turns the argument of chapter 40 into a trial scene. The islands and peoples are summoned to "come near together to judgment" (41:1). Two linked questions run through the chapter. Who controls history? And who can explain it beforehand? In the middle of the trial God turns to Israel with some of the most tender promises in these chapters.

### Close readings

**41:2–4, the one raised from the east.** "Who raised up the righteous man from the east... gave the nations before him, and made him rule over kings?" The figure is not named here. Most readers connect him with Cyrus, who is named in 44:28 and 45:1. The question is not "who is this conqueror?" It is "who raised him?" The answer comes immediately: "I the LORD, the first, and with the last; I am he" (41:4). Cyrus's victories, which might look like proof of Persian power or Persian gods, are claimed as Israel's God working in history.

**41:5–7, the nations' response.** Frightened nations encourage one another and build idols: "he fastened it with nails, that it should not be moved." The irony is sharp. A god that must be nailed down to keep from toppling is being asked to steady its makers.

**41:8–10, "thou, Israel, art my servant."** The contrast begins with "But thou." Israel is called "my servant," "Jacob whom I have chosen," and "the seed of Abraham my friend." Verse 9 adds what exiles most needed to hear: "I have chosen thee, and not cast thee away." Then comes 41:10: "Fear thou not; for I am with thee: be not dismayed; for I am thy God: I will strengthen thee; yea, I will help thee; yea, I will uphold thee with the right hand of my righteousness."

The verbs pile up: with, strengthen, help, uphold. Notice which verse the servant title belongs to. Here the servant is explicitly the people Israel.

**41:13–16, the worm becomes a threshing sledge.** "I the LORD thy God will hold thy right hand." In 45:1 God will hold Cyrus's right hand. Here he holds Israel's first. "Fear not, thou worm Jacob" (41:14) is not an insult. It is an honest description of how small the exiles feel. The God who calls himself "thy redeemer, the Holy One of Israel" makes that worm into "a new sharp threshing instrument having teeth" (41:15). The image reverses scale: the smallest creature levels mountains.

**41:17–20, water for the needy.** "When the poor and needy seek water, and there is none, and their tongue faileth for thirst, I the LORD will hear them." The text admits there are times when there is no water. The promise does not deny thirst. It answers it. Rivers in high places, fountains in valleys, and trees planted in the wilderness have a stated purpose: "that they may see, and know... that the hand of the LORD hath done this" (41:20).

**41:21–29, the idols' cross-examination.** "Produce your cause... bring forth your strong reasons" (41:21). The idols are challenged to explain "the former things" or announce "things for to come." Failing that, they are invited simply to "do good, or do evil" (41:23), meaning do anything at all. The verdict is "ye are of nothing" (41:24), and "their molten images are wind and confusion" (41:29). The test is moral and historical, not aesthetic. A god who cannot speak truthfully about history, or act within it, is no god.

Halverson's lens is helpful at this point. He names both God's presence and his mercy among the attributes these chapters display, and the chapter supports both at 41:10 and 41:17 ([Interpreter Foundation](https://interpreterfoundation.org/res-besides-me-there-is-no-saviour-isaiah-40-49/)).

### Seeing Jesus Christ in the chapter

The connection to Christ here runs through titles more than through prophecy. The God who speaks in 41:14 is "thy redeemer, the Holy One of Israel." Latter-day Saint scripture identifies the Holy One of Israel with Jesus Christ. For example, 2 Nephi 25:29 calls him "the Holy One of Israel," and the surrounding chapter makes clear that it means Christ. The promise "I am with thee" also anticipates the name Emmanuel (Matthew 1:23) and the risen Lord's "I am with you alway" (Matthew 28:20).

### Cross-references

- **2 Chronicles 20:7 and James 2:23.** Both call Abraham God's friend, which shows that 41:8 draws on a covenant tradition.
- **Isaiah 45:1.** God holds Cyrus's right hand there, just as he holds Israel's here. Comparing the two shows that the same gesture serves different purposes.
- **John 4:14 and 7:37–38.** Jesus offers living water, recasting the promise of 41:17–18.

### Questions for study and discussion

1. Verse 4 answers "Who raised up the righteous man from the east?" with a statement about God, not about the man. What does that suggest about how these chapters want readers to understand political events?
2. Verse 9 pairs "chosen" with "not cast thee away." Why would exiles need both statements?
3. What is the effect of calling Israel a "worm" (41:14) and then a "threshing instrument" (41:15)?
4. The challenge in 41:21–24 tests the gods by speech and action. What would a comparable test look like for the modern things people trust?
5. Verse 17 begins with real thirst: "there is none." How should a promise like this be read by someone still waiting for water?

## Isaiah 42: The faithful servant and the blind servant

### Setting in the sequence

Chapter 42 introduces the first passage usually called a "servant song" (42:1–4, often read through 42:7–9). It then ends with a startling turn. The servant Israel is described as blind and deaf. A careful reader has to keep both halves of the chapter in view.

### Close readings

**42:1–4, the gentle servant.** "Behold my servant, whom I uphold; mine elect, in whom my soul delighteth; I have put my spirit upon him: he shall bring forth judgment to the Gentiles." The verb "uphold" echoes 41:10. Unlike 41:8, this servant is not named.

His manner is described almost entirely by what he will not do. He "shall not cry, nor lift up, nor cause his voice to be heard in the street" (42:2). "A bruised reed shall he not break, and the smoking flax shall he not quench" (42:3). He "shall not fail nor be discouraged" (42:4).

A bruised reed is nearly useless, and smoking flax is a lamp wick about to go out. The servant does not discard either one. Yet he is not passive. "Judgment," meaning justice set right, appears three times in four verses. Gentleness and justice are not opposites in this portrait.

**42:5–7, covenant and light.** God, "he that created the heavens," addresses the servant: "I the LORD have called thee in righteousness, and will hold thine hand... and give thee for a covenant of the people, for a light of the Gentiles" (42:6). The task follows: "To open the blind eyes, to bring out the prisoners from the prison, and them that sit in darkness out of the prison house" (42:7).

In the exile setting, prisoners and darkness describe Israel's captivity. The servant's work therefore includes Israel's liberation as well as light for the nations.

**42:8–9, former things and new things.** "My glory will I not give to another, neither my praise to graven images." Then: "the former things are come to pass, and new things do I declare: before they spring forth I tell you of them." This continues the courtroom argument of chapter 41. Israel's God announces history before it happens.

**42:13–16, the travailing God.** God, who has "long time holden my peace," now cries out "like a travailing woman" (42:14). It is a striking image. God's long silence during the exile is not indifference. It is restraint before a birth. Then comes a promise that matters for what follows: "I will bring the blind by a way that they knew not... I will make darkness light before them... and not forsake them" (42:16).

**42:18–25, the blind servant.** "Hear, ye deaf; and look, ye blind... Who is blind, but my servant? or deaf, as my messenger that I sent?" (42:18–19). The faithful servant of 42:1–7 opens blind eyes. The servant here is blind. Israel is "seeing many things, but thou observest not" (42:20). They are "a people robbed and spoiled... for a prey, and none delivereth" (42:22).

The prophet asks who gave Jacob to the robbers and answers, "did not the LORD, he against whom we have sinned?" (42:24). The fire of judgment "burned him, yet he laid it not to heart" (42:25).

The tension is deliberate. The same chapter presents a servant who fulfills God's purposes and a servant who does not. Israel bears the title "servant" in both its calling and its failure. That leaves an open question that later chapters, and later readers, take up: who will do what Israel was called to do, and how will blind Israel be healed? Verse 16 has already said that God will lead the blind.

### Seeing Jesus Christ in the chapter

Matthew 12:15–21 applies 42:1–4 directly to Jesus. Matthew introduces the quotation right after Jesus heals the multitude and charges them "that they should not make him known." Matthew's own wording ends "till he send forth judgment unto victory. And in his name shall the Gentiles trust" ([Matthew 12:15–21](https://www.churchofjesuschrist.org/study/scriptures/nt/matt/12?lang=eng)).

The textual reasons for the connection are specific. The voice from heaven at Jesus's baptism, "in whom I am well pleased" (Matthew 3:17), echoes the language of delight in 42:1. The Spirit descends on Jesus as the servant has the Spirit put upon him. And Jesus's ministry to the sick, the excluded, and the sinner fits the refusal to break the bruised reed.

Christian readers also hear 42:7 in Jesus's announcement at Nazareth (Luke 4:18), although that sermon formally quotes Isaiah 61.

Reading 42:1–4 as fulfilled in Christ does not erase 42:19. The Christian claim is not that Israel's vocation was canceled. It is that Jesus fulfills it, as one who is fully Israel's servant and also Israel's Lord.

### Cross-references

- **Matthew 12:15–21.** This is the clearest New Testament use of the first servant song. Notice its context of quiet healing.
- **Matthew 3:16–17.** The Spirit and the Father's pleasure at the baptism echo 42:1.
- **Luke 4:16–21.** Its themes of opening blindness and releasing captives parallel 42:7.
- **John 9.** A man born blind receives sight while religious leaders who "see" remain blind (John 9:39–41). This reworks the reversal at the heart of Isaiah 42.

### Questions for study and discussion

1. Isaiah 42:2–3 describes the servant largely by what he refrains from doing. Why might restraint be central to this servant's identity?
2. Find the three uses of "judgment" in 42:1–4. What kind of justice comes from someone who will not break a bruised reed?
3. Matthew places this prophecy right after Jesus asks the healed not to publicize him. What does that context add to Isaiah's words?
4. How do 42:7 and 42:18–19 relate? Can a servant who is himself blind be among those whose eyes are opened?
5. Verse 25 says Israel was burned "yet he laid it not to heart." What is the difference between suffering and learning, and does the text claim the two always go together?
6. Where in your congregation or community might the bruised reed and smoking flax be found? What would it mean in practice not to break or quench them?

## Isaiah 43: Through the waters, beside me no saviour

### Setting in the sequence

Chapter 43 continues directly from the blind servant of 42:18–25. It opens with "But now," and it makes one of the greatest reversals in the book. The people just described as blind, deaf, and burned are told, "Fear not: for I have redeemed thee." The chapter then returns to the courtroom. This time Israel is called as God's witness.

### Close readings

**43:1, the identity before the promise.** God speaks as "the LORD that created thee, O Jacob, and he that formed thee, O Israel." Four claims follow: "Fear not: for I have redeemed thee, I have called thee by thy name; thou art mine." Redemption, naming, and belonging all come before any promise about what will happen next.

**43:2, presence in the waters and the fire.** "When thou passest through the waters, I will be with thee; and through the rivers, they shall not overflow thee: when thou walkest through the fire, thou shalt not be burned; neither shall the flame kindle upon thee."

Notice the word "when." The text does not say "if." It assumes the waters and the fire will come. In its historical frame, the waters recall the Red Sea and the Jordan, and the fire recalls the judgment just described in 42:25. Israel has already been burned. The promise looks forward to a passage through, with God present.

This verse needs careful handling. Scripture records physical deliverance from fire. It also records faithful people who died. John Hilton III's slides for [Finding Christ in the Old Testament, Class 39, "Walking Through the Fire"](https://johnhiltoniii.com/findingchrist/class-39-walking-through-the-fire/) ([slides](https://www.dropbox.com/scl/fi/hioqm9s166o80fr7hh4pq/39-Isaiah-40-49.pptx?rlkey=jm8gaki39kkggr5qqqbfie07s&dl=1)) put 43:1–3 next to two stories. The first is Daniel 3, including the three men's "but if not" and the fourth figure in the furnace. The second is Abinadi's testimony in Mosiah about the resurrection overcoming the grave. The slides then return to 43:2.

Read in the KJV, the pairing is instructive. Shadrach, Meshach, and Abed-nego say: "our God whom we serve is able to deliver us... But if not, be it known unto thee, O king, that we will not serve thy gods" ([Daniel 3:17–18](https://www.churchofjesuschrist.org/study/scriptures/ot/dan/3?lang=eng)). They are delivered, and Nebuchadnezzar sees "the form of the fourth... like the Son of God" walking with them (3:25).

Abinadi says, "it matters not whither I go, if it so be that I am saved" ([Mosiah 13:9](https://www.churchofjesuschrist.org/study/scriptures/bofm/mosiah/13?lang=eng)). He later suffers "death by fire" (Mosiah 17:20). Before that he testified: "there is a resurrection, therefore the grave hath no victory, and the sting of death is swallowed up in Christ. He is the light and the life of the world" (Mosiah 16:8–9).

Between them, these stories show two ways Isaiah 43:2 has been fulfilled for the faithful. One is rescue within history. The other is resurrection beyond it.

*Application:* The verse cannot honestly be read as a promise that faithful people avoid drowning grief, illness, abuse, or death. It promises God's presence and an outcome that the flame does not finally determine. For some, God's presence comes as rescue now. For others, it comes as companionship in the fire and redemption after it.

**43:3–7, ransom and gathering.** "I am the LORD thy God, the Holy One of Israel, thy Saviour: I gave Egypt for thy ransom, Ethiopia and Seba for thee." Historically, this probably reflects the Persian expansion. Other nations fall under Persia while Israel is released. The language is unsettling. Nations are "given" for Israel's sake, so it should be read as a statement of Israel's value to God, not as a principle that others are expendable.

The motive is stated plainly in 43:4: "thou wast precious in my sight... and I have loved thee." Gathering follows (43:5–7), and it is pointedly inclusive: "bring my sons from far, and my daughters from the ends of the earth."

**43:8–13, witnesses who are blind.** "Bring forth the blind people that have eyes, and the deaf that have ears" (43:8). These blind and deaf people, the same ones from 42:18–20, are then called to testify: "Ye are my witnesses, saith the LORD, and my servant whom I have chosen" (43:10). Their purpose is "that ye may know and believe me." God does not wait for perfect witnesses. Testifying is one of the ways Israel's sight returns.

The testimony itself is "I, even I, am the LORD; and beside me there is no saviour" (43:11). Halverson takes his title from this verse, and his lens makes the point well. The chapter's claim is about the uniqueness of God's saving power, not only about the falseness of other gods ([Interpreter Foundation](https://interpreterfoundation.org/res-besides-me-there-is-no-saviour-isaiah-40-49/)).

**43:16–21, the new thing.** God "maketh a way in the sea" (43:16), which is Exodus language. Then: "Remember ye not the former things... Behold, I will do a new thing; now it shall spring forth; shall ye not know it? I will even make a way in the wilderness, and rivers in the desert" (43:18–19). Elsewhere these chapters urge Israel to remember (46:9). Here the point is that the new deliverance should not be judged only by the old pattern.

**43:22–25, the wearied God.** The chapter's ending turns the burden around. Israel has been "weary of me" (43:22). Then: "thou hast made me to serve with thy sins, thou hast wearied me with thine iniquities" (43:24). God says he has been made to serve, burdened by his people. The response is "I, even I, am he that blotteth out thy transgressions for mine own sake, and will not remember thy sins" (43:25).

### Seeing Jesus Christ in the chapter

The key textual link is 43:11: "beside me there is no saviour." In the Book of Mormon, the one who saves is Jesus Christ: "there shall be no other name given nor any other way nor means whereby salvation can come... only in and through the name of Christ" (Mosiah 3:17). Acts 4:12 makes a similar claim.

Latter-day Saints identify Jehovah of the Old Testament with the premortal Christ. On that view, 43:11 is the Savior speaking about himself. The image of God burdened by Israel's sins (43:24) points readers toward Isaiah 53:4–6 and toward Gethsemane. That is a Christian reading. The verse itself describes God wearied by covenant unfaithfulness.

### Cross-references

- **Daniel 3:16–28 and Mosiah 13:9; 16:8–9; 17:20.** These prevent a prosperity reading of 43:2.
- **Exodus 14:21–22 and Joshua 3:14–17.** The literal waters 43:2 and 43:16 draw on.
- **Mosiah 3:17 and Acts 4:12.** The uniqueness of salvation in Christ.
- **Jeremiah 31:34.** God remembering sins no more, in the context of the new covenant.

### Questions for study and discussion

1. What changes when you read 43:1 directly after 42:25? What does "But now" accomplish?
2. Isaiah 43:2 says "when," not "if." How does that word shape the promise?
3. Compare the three men in Daniel 3 with Abinadi. Both kept faith. What does the difference in outcomes teach about how to read 43:2?
4. Why might God call people described as blind and deaf to be his witnesses (43:8–10)?
5. In 43:18–19 Israel is told not to dwell on "former things," yet elsewhere it is told to remember. When does remembering the past help faith, and when does it get in the way of recognizing something new?
6. Verse 25 says God blots out sins "for mine own sake." What might that phrase reveal about God's motives in forgiving?

## Isaiah 44: Water poured, wood burned, and a shepherd named Cyrus

### Setting in the sequence

Chapter 44 contains the longest idol satire in Isaiah (44:9–20). It is framed by two passages about Israel as God's formed servant (44:1–8, 44:21–23). It ends by naming Cyrus for the first time (44:28). This arrangement places the most concrete mockery of idols right next to the most concrete political prediction.

### Close readings

**44:1–5, Spirit on the offspring.** "Yet now hear, O Jacob my servant." Israel is again the servant, and in 44:2 also "Jesurun," an affectionate name for Israel found in Deuteronomy (Deuteronomy 32:15; 33:5, 26). The promise is "I will pour water upon him that is thirsty... I will pour my spirit upon thy seed, and my blessing upon thine offspring" (44:3).

Verse 5 describes people choosing their identity: "One shall say, I am the LORD's; and another shall call himself by the name of Jacob; and another shall subscribe with his hand unto the LORD." Covenant belonging is pictured as something spoken and signed, possibly by people not born into it.

**44:6–8, first and last.** "I am the first, and I am the last; and beside me there is no God." Then, almost wryly: "Is there a God beside me? yea, there is no God; I know not any" (44:8). The courtroom theme continues, since God is the one who appointed "the ancient people" and announces "the things that are coming."

**44:9–20, the carpenter's breakfast.** The satire works through concrete detail.

- The smith works until "he is hungry, and his strength faileth" (44:12). The god-maker gets tired, while the true God "fainteth not" (40:28).
- The carpenter plants a tree that "the rain doth nourish" (44:14). God provided the raw material for the idol.
- The carpenter uses half the wood to warm himself and bake bread, "and the residue thereof he maketh a god... and prayeth unto it, and saith, Deliver me; for thou art my god" (44:17).
- The verdict: "He feedeth on ashes: a deceived heart hath turned him aside, that he cannot deliver his soul, nor say, Is there not a lie in my right hand?" (44:20).

The prophet's diagnosis is not stupidity. It is a deceived heart. The idolater cannot make the simple inventory that would expose the absurdity. The same block of wood cannot be fuel and savior at once.

*Application:* The modern equivalent is seldom carved wood. It is anything we make or acquire with our own hands and then ask to deliver us, such as money, reputation, or a political cause. Each has ordinary uses, like the carpenter's wood. The trouble starts when the "residue" becomes something prayed to. The question in 44:20 is a useful self-examination: what is in my right hand?

**44:21–23, not forgotten, blotted out.** "O Israel, thou shalt not be forgotten of me" (44:21). "I have blotted out, as a thick cloud, thy transgressions... return unto me; for I have redeemed thee" (44:22). The order matters. Redemption is stated first, and the call to return follows. Creation joins in the response: "Sing, O ye heavens" (44:23).

**44:24–28, the God who confirms his servant's word and names Cyrus.** One long sentence of participles describes the LORD. He "maketh all things," stretches out the heavens "alone," frustrates diviners, "confirmeth the word of his servant" (a prophetic servant here), tells Jerusalem "Thou shalt be inhabited," tells the deep "Be dry," and finally "saith of Cyrus, He is my shepherd, and shall perform all my pleasure: even saying to Jerusalem, Thou shalt be built; and to the temple, Thy foundation shall be laid" (44:28).

Calling Cyrus a "shepherd" is a royal title, since kings were shepherds of their people. Here a foreign king shepherds Israel's restoration. This matches the historical record in Ezra 1:1–4, where Cyrus authorizes the rebuilding of the temple.

### Seeing Jesus Christ in the chapter

The title "the first, and... the last" (44:6) is taken up by the risen Christ in Revelation 1:17 and 22:13. That is a clear New Testament identification of Jesus with the God who speaks in Isaiah.

The promise of the Spirit poured on Israel's offspring (44:3) finds Christian fulfillment in the outpouring of the Spirit after Christ's ascension (Acts 2:17–18, quoting Joel).

Cyrus as "shepherd" is a contrasting case, not a symbol of Christ. Cyrus shepherds Israel politically, toward rebuilding. Jesus calls himself the good shepherd who "giveth his life for the sheep" (John 10:11). The difference is the point. God can use a foreign ruler to accomplish his purposes in history. Only one Shepherd lays down his life.

### Cross-references

- **Deuteronomy 32:15; 33:26.** Uses of "Jesurun."
- **Revelation 1:17; 22:13.** "The first and the last" applied to Christ.
- **Ezra 1:1–4 and 2 Chronicles 36:22–23.** Cyrus's decree in Israel's historical books.
- **Acts 2:17–18.** The Spirit poured out on sons and daughters.

### Questions for study and discussion

1. What details in 44:12–17 make the satire effective? Which detail do you find most pointed?
2. Verse 20 describes "a deceived heart." Why can't the idol-maker see "a lie in my right hand"? What would help someone see it?
3. In 44:22, redemption is announced before the invitation to "return." What does that order suggest about repentance?
4. Verse 5 imagines people choosing to "subscribe with [their] hand unto the LORD." What does it mean to belong to God by choice and by covenant?
5. Why might the prophet place the naming of Cyrus right after a satire on idols? What argument does that arrangement make?

## Isaiah 45: Cyrus the anointed, and God who forms light and darkness

### Setting in the sequence

Chapter 45 speaks directly to Cyrus (45:1–7), then broadens out to creation, to Israel, and finally to "all the ends of the earth" (45:22). It is the most theologically daring chapter in the block. A pagan king is called God's anointed. God claims both light and darkness. And salvation is offered to every nation.

### Close readings

**45:1–4, the anointed king who does not know God.** "Thus saith the LORD to his anointed, to Cyrus, whose right hand I have holden." The word "anointed" was used for Israel's kings and priests. Calling a Persian ruler "anointed" means he is commissioned by God for a specific task. God will "go before thee," "break in pieces the gates of brass," and give "the treasures of darkness" (45:2–3).

Two purposes are given. The first is "that thou mayest know that I, the LORD... am the God of Israel" (45:3). The second, and more basic, is "For Jacob my servant's sake, and Israel mine elect" (45:4). Cyrus is not the servant here. Jacob is the servant, and Cyrus is commissioned for Jacob's sake.

The phrase "though thou hast not known me" appears twice (45:4–5). God works through Cyrus whether or not Cyrus acknowledges him.

Cyrus is not Jesus. He is a historical ruler whose victories served God's purposes for Israel. The text never presents him as a redeemer from sin, a sufferer, or a covenant figure. It does show that God's sovereignty reaches beyond his covenant people.

*Application:* That idea brings comfort and also humility. God can work through institutions and people who do not acknowledge him. The text does not make every powerful person God's chosen instrument, and it does not ask readers to endorse every ruler.

**45:5–7, light and darkness, peace and "evil."** "I form the light, and create darkness: I make peace, and create evil: I the LORD do all these things" (45:7).

The structure of the verse guides how to read it. Light pairs with darkness, and peace pairs with "evil." In this pairing "evil" is the opposite of peace and well-being, so it means calamity or disaster, not moral wickedness. The verse is addressed to Cyrus, whose conquests brought peace to some and ruin to others, including Babylon.

The claim is that no rival power controls the dark side of history. A dualist might attribute darkness to a competing god. Isaiah says there is "none beside me" (45:6).

The verse does not say that God commits moral evil, approves of cruelty, or causes every particular tragedy. Scripture elsewhere insists that God is just (45:21) and that "God cannot be tempted with evil, neither tempteth he any man" (James 1:13). Amos 3:6 uses "evil" in a similar sense of a disaster that strikes a city.

*Application:* Readers in grief should not be told that 45:7 means God personally designed their loss. The verse makes a claim about sovereignty over history. It is not a cause-and-effect explanation of individual suffering.

**45:8–13, rain of righteousness and the potter.** "Drop down, ye heavens, from above, and let the skies pour down righteousness" (45:8). Right after this comes a rebuke: "Woe unto him that striveth with his Maker!... Shall the clay say to him that fashioneth it, What makest thou?" (45:9). In context, the objection seems to be against God's choice of Cyrus. The rebuke concerns contending with God's purposes. It is not a ban on lament, which Israel's psalms and prophets practice openly. God's reply restates Cyrus's commission: "he shall build my city, and he shall let go my captives, not for price nor reward" (45:13).

**45:15–19, the hidden God who did not speak in secret.** "Verily thou art a God that hidest thyself, O God of Israel, the Saviour" (45:15). That is an honest admission inside the chapter itself. Then God answers: "I have not spoken in secret, in a dark place of the earth: I said not unto the seed of Jacob, Seek ye me in vain" (45:19).

The two statements stand side by side. God is experienced as hidden, and yet he has spoken openly. Between them comes 45:18: God "created it not in vain, he formed it to be inhabited." Compare Nephi: "the Lord hath created the earth that it should be inhabited; and he hath created his children that they should possess it" (1 Nephi 17:36).

**45:20–25, look unto me.** The invitation becomes universal: "Look unto me, and be ye saved, all the ends of the earth: for I am God, and there is none else" (45:22). God calls himself "a just God and a Saviour" (45:21). He swears that "unto me every knee shall bow, every tongue shall swear" (45:23).

### Seeing Jesus Christ in the chapter

Paul applies 45:23 to Jesus Christ: "every knee should bow... and that every tongue should confess that Jesus Christ is Lord, to the glory of God the Father" (Philippians 2:10–11). He also applies it in Romans 14:11 to judgment. Paul takes Isaiah's strongest monotheistic oath and places Jesus within it. That is one of the New Testament's clearest claims about Christ's divine identity.

The title "a just God and a Saviour" (45:21) is fulfilled in the Christian teaching that in Christ, justice and mercy meet (compare Alma 42:15).

The contrast with Cyrus is instructive. Cyrus is called "anointed" for a political task. "Messiah" and "Christ" both mean "anointed one," and Jesus is the Anointed for the redemption of all.

### Cross-references

- **Ezra 1:1–4.** Cyrus's actual decree.
- **Amos 3:6.** "Evil" in the sense of calamity on a city.
- **James 1:13.** God does not tempt with evil, which guards against misreading 45:7.
- **Philippians 2:9–11 and Romans 14:11.** Paul's Christ-centered use of 45:23.
- **1 Nephi 17:36.** An inhabited creation, parallel to 45:18.

### Questions for study and discussion

1. What does it mean that Cyrus is anointed "for Jacob my servant's sake" (45:4)? How does that phrase keep Cyrus in his proper place?
2. Read 45:7 again with its pairs: light/darkness, peace/evil. How does the structure guide your interpretation? What would be lost or distorted by reading "evil" as moral wickedness?
3. How do 45:15 and 45:19 relate? Can God be experienced as hidden and still have spoken openly?
4. In 45:9, the clay questions the potter. How is that different from the honest laments of scripture, such as Psalm 13 or Job?
5. Paul applies 45:23 to Jesus. What does that tell you about how early Christians understood who Jesus is?

## Isaiah 46: Gods who are carried and the God who carries

### Setting in the sequence

Chapter 46 is short and tightly built. It begins with Babylon's gods being hauled away on animals. It contrasts them with the God who has carried Israel from birth. Then it returns to the argument from foreknowledge and the "ravenous bird from the east."

### Close readings

**46:1–2, idols on pack animals.** "Bel boweth down, Nebo stoopeth, their idols were upon the beasts, and upon the cattle: your carriages were heavy loaden; they are a burden to the weary beast." Bel was a title of Marduk, Babylon's chief god, and Nebo (Nabu) was another major Babylonian deity.

The picture shows Babylon's gods being loaded onto animals, whether in procession or in flight. Either way they cannot walk. They "could not deliver the burden, but themselves are gone into captivity" (46:2). The gods meant to deliver Babylon need to be carried, and they fail.

**46:3–4, carried from the womb to old age.** "Hearken unto me, O house of Jacob... which are borne by me from the belly, which are carried from the womb: And even to your old age I am he; and even to hoar hairs will I carry you: I have made, and I will bear; even I will carry, and will deliver you."

The contrast is exact. Babylonians carry their gods. Israel's God carries his people. The verbs repeat (borne, carried, carry, bear, carry) with the insistence of a promise. The span runs from birth to gray hair, a whole life. The final verb, "deliver," is the one thing the idols could not do (46:2).

*Application:* People often ask what they are carrying, such as obligations, images, or reputations, that ought instead to be carrying them. There is also an application for aging. Isaiah's promise reaches specifically to "hoar hairs." It does not end when a person's usefulness seems to end.

**46:5–7, the expensive god who cannot answer.** "They lavish gold out of the bag... and hire a goldsmith; and he maketh it a god." Once made, "they bear him upon the shoulder... and set him in his place, and he standeth; from his place shall he not remove: yea, one shall cry unto him, yet can he not answer, nor save him out of his trouble" (46:6–7). Two failures stand out: it cannot move, and it cannot answer.

**46:8–11, remember and see.** "Remember this, and shew yourselves men... Remember the former things of old" (46:8–9). Compare 43:18, where Israel is told not to dwell on "former things." The two passages serve different purposes. Here remembering former acts of God is evidence against idolatry. There, clinging to the old pattern could keep Israel from recognizing a new deliverance.

God is the one "declaring the end from the beginning" (46:10). He calls "a ravenous bird from the east, the man that executeth my counsel" (46:11), which in context is Cyrus again. The image of a bird of prey is not flattering. Cyrus is a tool for God's purpose, not a model of virtue.

**46:12–13, salvation brought near to the stubborn.** "Hearken unto me, ye stouthearted, that are far from righteousness: I bring near my righteousness... and my salvation shall not tarry." The people who are "far from righteousness" are not told to close the gap themselves. God brings his righteousness near to them.

### Seeing Jesus Christ in the chapter

In English, the verbs of 46:4 ("I will bear... I will carry") anticipate Isaiah 53:4: "Surely he hath borne our griefs, and carried our sorrows." The servant who bears and carries in Isaiah 53 is read in Christian tradition as Jesus. Alma uses similar language when he says Christ will "take upon him the pains and the sicknesses of his people" (Alma 7:11–12).

The connection here is a Christian theological reading that the English wording invites. It is not a claim about shared Hebrew vocabulary. The God who carries Israel in 46:4 is, in Christian reading, the God who in Christ takes up his people's burdens.

### Cross-references

- **Deuteronomy 1:31.** "The LORD thy God bare thee, as a man doth bear his son."
- **Exodus 19:4.** Bearing Israel on eagles' wings.
- **Isaiah 53:4 and Alma 7:11–12.** The Savior bearing griefs and pains.
- **Matthew 11:28–30.** Jesus offers rest to the "heavy laden." Compare the "heavy loaden" carts of 46:1.

### Questions for study and discussion

1. List every word for carrying in 46:1–7. Who carries whom? What argument does the pattern make?
2. Isaiah 46:4 names "old age" and "hoar hairs." Why might that matter to a people in long exile, and to you?
3. The idol in 46:7 cannot move and cannot answer. What does Isaiah assume a real God should be able to do?
4. How do you reconcile "Remember the former things" (46:9) with "Remember ye not the former things" (43:18)?
5. Verse 12 addresses the "stouthearted, that are far from righteousness," and 46:13 says God brings righteousness near. What does that order suggest about how God approaches resistant people?

## Isaiah 47: The fall of the lady of kingdoms

### Setting in the sequence

Chapter 47 is a taunt song over Babylon, the empire that destroyed Jerusalem. Babylon is personified as a pampered royal woman forced off her throne. The chapter is the counterpart to chapter 49, where desolate Zion, also personified as a woman, is restored. These two women frame the end of the block.

### Reading with care

This chapter uses the imagery of conquest: a woman stripped, humiliated, and widowed. In the ancient world, conquered cities and especially their women actually suffered such things. The text personifies an empire. It does not command anyone to humiliate real people, and it should never be used to justify degrading anyone. The chapter is prophetic judgment against an imperial power, voiced for a people that power had crushed.

Readers can acknowledge that the imagery is painful. They can also recognize that for exiles, hearing the oppressor's fall announced was an act of hope.

### Close readings

**47:1–3, from throne to dust.** "Come down, and sit in the dust, O virgin daughter of Babylon, sit on the ground: there is no throne." Babylon will no longer be "tender and delicate." She must "take the millstones, and grind meal" (47:2), the labor of a servant or captive. Then she must cross rivers exposed. Verse 3 ends in judgment: "I will take vengeance."

In chapter 52 Zion will be told to rise from the dust (52:2). Here Babylon goes down into it. The reversal answers what Babylon did to Jerusalem.

**47:5–7, judgment on the instrument that showed no mercy.** "Thou shalt no more be called, The lady of kingdoms" (47:5). Verse 6 is the moral center of the chapter: "I was wroth with my people... and given them into thine hand: thou didst shew them no mercy; upon the ancient hast thou very heavily laid thy yoke."

The text holds two things together. God says Israel's fall was judgment. Babylon is still accountable for its cruelty, especially toward the old. Being an instrument of judgment did not entitle Babylon to show no mercy. The text gives no excuse for oppression, even oppression that appears within a larger divine story.

*Application:* No one can justify cruelty by claiming the victim deserved it, or that events were part of God's plan.

**47:7–10, "I am, and none else beside me."** Babylon said, "I shall be a lady for ever" (47:7). Twice she says in her heart, "I am, and none else beside me" (47:8, 10). These are God's own words about himself (45:5, 6, 21; 46:9), now spoken by the empire.

This is the deepest charge in the chapter. Babylon has claimed divine status. She expects never to "sit as a widow, neither... know the loss of children" (47:8). Both will come "in a moment in one day" (47:9).

Verse 10 traces the pathology: "Thou hast trusted in thy wickedness: thou hast said, None seeth me. Thy wisdom and thy knowledge, it hath perverted thee."

**47:11–15, the failure of experts.** Babylon is invited to rely on "enchantments" and "sorceries," and on "the astrologers, the stargazers, the monthly prognosticators" (47:12–13). In the courtroom terms of chapters 41–46, these are the professional forecasters who cannot see what is coming.

They will be "as stubble" in a fire so consuming that "there shall not be a coal to warm at" (47:14). This contrasts with the comfortable fire of the carpenter in 44:16. The chapter ends with the bleakest line in the block: "none shall save thee" (47:15). Compare 43:11. Beside God there is no savior, and when Babylon has set herself in God's place, there is no one to save her.

### Seeing Jesus Christ in the chapter

This chapter is about God's judgment on imperial pride, not about the Messiah. The connection to Christ is thematic and should be stated modestly. Mary's song praises God who "hath put down the mighty from their seats, and exalted them of low degree" (Luke 1:52). In the Christian reading, the God who brings down Babylon is the God who in Christ chooses the humble road.

Revelation takes up Babylon's boast, "I sit a queen, and am no widow, and shall see no sorrow" (Revelation 18:7). In Latter-day Saint scripture, "Babylon" also names worldly power and pride (D&C 1:16). Readers can see the faithful martyrs and the Lamb set against this kind of self-deifying power.

### Cross-references

- **Isaiah 52:1–2.** Zion rising from the dust, the mirror image of 47:1.
- **Revelation 18:7–8.** The new Babylon repeating the old boast.
- **Luke 1:51–53.** The reversal of the proud and the lowly.
- **2 Nephi 9:28–29.** Learning that becomes self-deception when it does not listen to God's counsel. Compare 47:10.

### Questions for study and discussion

1. How does 47:6 hold together God's judgment on Israel and God's judgment on Babylon's cruelty? What does that teach about accountability?
2. Babylon says "I am, and none else beside me" twice. Where has the reader heard those words before? What is the chapter saying about pride?
3. Verse 10 says Babylon's "wisdom and... knowledge... perverted" her. How can knowledge mislead? What would distinguish wisdom that does not?
4. How should a reader today handle the humiliation imagery of 47:1–3, taking it seriously as judgment poetry while caring for real victims of conquest and abuse?
5. Where do you see the attitude "None seeth me" (47:10) in individuals or institutions today, and what does Isaiah suggest about where it leads?

## Isaiah 48: Refined in the furnace of affliction

### Setting in the sequence

After the fall of Babylon in chapter 47, chapter 48 turns sharply to Israel's own stubbornness. Before announcing the exodus from Babylon (48:20), God addresses a people who name his name but do not truly trust him. This chapter and the next were copied by Nephi into his record as [1 Nephi 20–21](https://www.churchofjesuschrist.org/study/scriptures/bofm/1-ne/20?lang=eng), with some textual differences.

### Close readings

**48:1–2, worship without trust.** The house of Jacob "swear by the name of the LORD... but not in truth, nor in righteousness" (48:1). The KJV of 48:2 says they "call themselves of the holy city, and stay themselves upon the God of Israel." 1 Nephi 20:2 reads differently: they call themselves of the holy city, "but they do not stay themselves upon the God of Israel." That sharpens the charge into identity without reliance.

1 Nephi 20:1 also adds to 48:1 that they come "out of the waters of Judah, or out of the waters of baptism." This invites Nephi's readers, and Latter-day Saints, to read covenant identity with ordinances in mind.

**48:3–8, why God foretold.** God explains why he announced events beforehand: "Because I knew that thou art obstinate, and thy neck is an iron sinew, and thy brow brass" (48:4). He foretold them "lest thou shouldest say, Mine idol hath done them" (48:5). Prophecy here is not mainly about satisfying curiosity. It is evidence that answers stubbornness. God even withheld some revelations until "now" so that Israel could not say, "Behold, I knew them" (48:7). Israel was "called a transgressor from the womb" (48:8).

**48:9–11, deferred anger and the furnace.** "For my name's sake will I defer mine anger... that I cut thee not off" (48:9). Then: "Behold, I have refined thee, but not with silver; I have chosen thee in the furnace of affliction" (48:10). The same motive is stated twice: "For mine own sake, even for mine own sake, will I do it" (48:11).

Notice what 48:10 says and what it does not. It addresses Israel's exile, which these chapters treat as judgment. Its claim is that God was present and choosing within affliction, not that God takes pleasure in it. "Not with silver" may mean that the refining did not proceed as a silversmith's would, perhaps not to the point of total consumption (compare 48:9, "that I cut thee not off").

The official lesson invites readers to ponder 48:10 alongside 49:13–16. That pairing keeps refinement and compassion together. The God who refines is the God who cannot forget ([Come, Follow Me](https://www.churchofjesuschrist.org/study/manual/come-follow-me-for-home-and-church-old-testament-2026/40?lang=eng)).

*Application:* Many faithful people find meaning in affliction and come to see refining in it, as Joseph Smith was taught in Liberty Jail (D&C 122:7). The verse does not require every sufferer to identify a purpose. It does not make every hardship deserved. It does not justify anyone who causes affliction. It promises that the furnace is not a place where God stops choosing.

**48:12–16, the first and last who sends.** "I am he; I am the first, I also am the last" (48:12). His hand "laid the foundation of the earth" (48:13). Of Cyrus: "The LORD hath loved him: he will do his pleasure on Babylon" (48:14).

Verse 16 then shifts voice: "Come ye near unto me... I have not spoken in secret from the beginning... and now the Lord GOD, and his Spirit, hath sent me." In context the speaker may be the prophet, who has been sent. Christian readers have also heard in it the voice of the Son, sent by the Father and the Spirit. Both readings take seriously the shift from God speaking to someone sent by God.

**48:17–19, the river that might have been.** "I am the LORD thy God which teacheth thee to profit, which leadeth thee by the way that thou shouldest go. O that thou hadst hearkened to my commandments! then had thy peace been as a river, and thy righteousness as the waves of the sea" (48:17–18).

This is a lament, not a threat. God grieves over what might have been. The images are of continuous, inexhaustible water. A river keeps flowing, and waves keep arriving.

**48:20–22, the call out of Babylon.** "Go ye forth of Babylon, flee ye from the Chaldeans, with a voice of singing... say ye, The LORD hath redeemed his servant Jacob" (48:20). The new exodus recalls the old: "he caused the waters to flow out of the rock for them" (48:21). The chapter ends with a warning that reappears at 57:21: "There is no peace, saith the LORD, unto the wicked" (48:22). This sets 48:18 alongside 48:22: peace like a river for those who hearken, and no peace for the wicked.

### Nephi's likening

Scripture Central's [KnoWhy 23](https://scripturecentral.org/knowhy/how-might-isaiah-48-49-be-likened-to-lehis-family) argues that Nephi's use of these chapters was deliberate, not filler. It proposes parallels with his family's experience. Some in Lehi's family claimed holy-city identity without depending on God. They showed stiff-necked resistance. Revelation was heard and declared among them. The KnoWhy places the river and waves of 48:18 beside Lehi's words to Laman and Lemuel: "O Laman, that thou mightest be like unto this river, continually running into the fountain of all righteousness! ... O Lemuel, that thou mightest be like unto this valley, firm and steadfast, and immovable" ([1 Nephi 2:9–10](https://www.churchofjesuschrist.org/study/scriptures/bofm/1-ne/2?lang=eng)).

These are likenings and proposed parallels. They do not prove that Isaiah's words originally predicted Lehi's family. That fits Nephi's own method: "I did liken all scriptures unto us" (1 Nephi 19:23).

### Seeing Jesus Christ in the chapter

There are three textual points of contact.

- The title "the first... the last" (48:12) is claimed by Christ in Revelation 1:17.
- The sent one of 48:16 can be read, in Christian terms, as the Son sent by the Father with the Spirit.
- The water from the rock (48:21) is explicitly connected to Christ by Paul: "they drank of that spiritual Rock that followed them: and that Rock was Christ" (1 Corinthians 10:4).

The God who says "I have refined thee" is also, in the Book of Mormon, the Savior who "suffereth the pains of all men" (2 Nephi 9:21). The refiner has entered the furnace himself.

### Cross-references

- **1 Nephi 20:1–2.** The Book of Mormon readings that sharpen 48:1–2.
- **1 Nephi 2:9–10.** Lehi's river and valley, a proposed parallel to 48:18.
- **Malachi 3:2–3.** The refiner and purifier of silver.
- **D&C 122:7–8.** Affliction and experience, with the reminder that "the Son of Man hath descended below them all."
- **1 Corinthians 10:1–4.** Christ as the rock in the wilderness.

### Questions for study and discussion

1. Compare the KJV of 48:2 with 1 Nephi 20:2. How does the Book of Mormon reading change the meaning? How might someone "call themselves of the holy city" without leaning on God?
2. According to 48:3–8, why did God announce events beforehand? What does that suggest about the purpose of prophecy?
3. What does 48:10 claim about God's role in affliction, and what does it leave unsaid? How would you discuss this verse with someone whose suffering has no visible purpose?
4. "O that thou hadst hearkened" (48:18) is a lament. What does it reveal about God that he grieves over what might have been?
5. Lehi spoke of a river and a valley. Isaiah speaks of a river and waves. What do the water images suggest about the kind of righteousness God hopes for?
6. Nephi likened these chapters to his family. Which verse in 48 seems most likely to fit your own circumstances, and why?

## Isaiah 49: Light to the Gentiles, graven on the palms

### Setting in the sequence

Chapter 49 begins a new section. The servant speaks in the first person (49:1–6). God answers him (49:7–12). Then Zion, the abandoned woman whose counterpart was Babylon in chapter 47, receives an extended answer to her complaint that she has been forgotten (49:14–26). Nephi included it as 1 Nephi 21, and Nephi's commentary follows in [1 Nephi 22](https://www.churchofjesuschrist.org/study/scriptures/bofm/1-ne/22?lang=eng).

### Close readings

**49:1–3, the hidden arrow called Israel.** "The LORD hath called me from the womb... he hath made my mouth like a sharp sword; in the shadow of his hand hath he hid me, and made me a polished shaft; in his quiver hath he hid me" (49:1–2). The servant is a weapon kept in reserve, prepared and hidden until the right time. Then comes the explicit naming: "Thou art my servant, O Israel, in whom I will be glorified" (49:3).

**49:4, the confession of vain labor.** "Then I said, I have laboured in vain, I have spent my strength for nought, and in vain: yet surely my judgment is with the LORD, and my work with my God." The servant openly admits apparent failure. Faith in this verse does not deny futility. It entrusts the outcome to God. Before any success is promised, the servant states that his vindication rests with God.

**49:5–6, the productive tension.** The servant was formed "to bring Jacob again to him" (49:5), and then: "It is a light thing that thou shouldest be my servant to raise up the tribes of Jacob, and to restore the preserved of Israel: I will also give thee for a light to the Gentiles, that thou mayest be my salvation unto the end of the earth" (49:6).

The tension is easy to see. In 49:3 the servant is named Israel. In 49:5–6 the servant's mission is to restore Israel. How can Israel restore Israel? Several readings are possible:

- A faithful remnant restores the larger people.
- A prophet who embodies Israel's calling restores the nation.
- In Christian reading, Jesus is the true Israel who fulfills Israel's vocation and restores Israel.

These readings need not cancel one another. The tension is part of the text's power.

Scripture Central's [KnoWhy 24](https://scripturecentral.org/knowhy/who-is-the-servant-of-isaiah-491-nephi-21) reports that interpreters debate the servant's identity. Some read the servant as collective Israel, and Christian readers see Christ. It notes that Nephi invites readers to liken the text in a way that gives hope. It also presents Andrew C. Skinner's proposal that the passage can be applied in the latter days to Joseph Smith, alongside the Messiah. That is Skinner's proposed likening for the Restoration. It is not the passage's sole historical meaning, and it does not put any prophet in Christ's unique redemptive role.

**49:7–12, the despised servant and the highway.** God speaks "to him whom man despiseth, to him whom the nation abhorreth, to a servant of rulers": "Kings shall see and arise" (49:7). The despised servant becomes the one before whom kings rise. This anticipates 52:13–53:3.

The servant is given "for a covenant of the people" (49:8, echoing 42:6). He is to say "to the prisoners, Go forth" (49:9). The returning people "shall not hunger nor thirst... for he that hath mercy on them shall lead them, even by the springs of water" (49:10). God makes "all my mountains a way" (49:11), returning to the highway of 40:3–4. The block circles back to its opening image.

**49:13–15, Zion's complaint and the mother's memory.** Heaven and earth sing "for the LORD hath comforted his people" (49:13). That is the comfort promised in 40:1, now announced as accomplished. Yet immediately: "But Zion said, The LORD hath forsaken me, and my Lord hath forgotten me" (49:14). Celebration and despair sit side by side. The text does not mock the complaint. It answers it with one of the most daring images in scripture: "Can a woman forget her sucking child...? yea, they may forget, yet will I not forget thee" (49:15).

The text also frankly admits that mothers may forget. Human love, even the strongest kind, can fail. God's memory is measured against the best human love and then said to exceed it.

**49:16, palms and walls.** "Behold, I have graven thee upon the palms of my hands; thy walls are continually before me."

In its first context, the addressee is Zion, the city, and the second half of the verse is about her walls. Jerusalem's walls lay in ruins. God says those walls are "continually before me," inscribed on his hands where he cannot help seeing them. It is like a builder's plan always in view, or a name written on the hand as a reminder. The promise is that God will rebuild the city. The following verses confirm this, as "thy children shall make haste" and "thy destroyers... shall go forth of thee" (49:17).

Christian readers then take a further step. They connect hands that bear Zion with the hands of the risen Christ. Isaiah does not mention wounds. But the risen Lord invited his disciples and the Nephites to feel "the prints of the nails in my hands and in my feet" ([3 Nephi 11:14](https://www.churchofjesuschrist.org/study/scriptures/bofm/3-ne/11?lang=eng); compare John 20:27; D&C 6:37). For Christian readers, God's remembering hands become crucified and risen hands. The marks he carries are the cost of not forgetting his people.

*Application:* Many people personally hear "I have graven thee" as addressed to them. That is a legitimate likening, in the way Nephi likened Isaiah. It is strongest when it remembers that Isaiah first spoke to a ruined community, so it also includes others in that community.

**49:17–23, children Zion never expected.** Zion is told, "Lift up thine eyes round about, and behold: all these gather themselves together" (49:18). The returning children complain, "The place is too strait for me" (49:20). Zion asks, "Who hath begotten me these, seeing I have lost my children, and am desolate... Behold, I was left alone; these, where had they been?" (49:21).

Grief and astonishment come together. The losses were real, and the new children do not erase them. God lifts his "hand to the Gentiles," and they carry Israel's sons and daughters home; "kings shall be thy nursing fathers, and their queens thy nursing mothers" (49:22–23). Nephi explains these verses as the Gentiles' role in the latter-day gathering, including "a marvelous work among the Gentiles" (1 Nephi 22:6–8).

**49:24–26, the captive of the mighty.** "Shall the prey be taken from the mighty, or the lawful captive delivered?" (49:24). The question is realistic. Captives usually stay captive. God's answer is "I will contend with him that contendeth with thee, and I will save thy children" (49:25).

Verse 26 contains violent imagery of oppressors turned against themselves. Like chapter 47, it is judgment on oppressive power, not a mandate for readers. The goal is stated: "all flesh shall know that I the LORD am thy Saviour and thy Redeemer, the mighty One of Jacob." This echoes "all flesh shall see it together" (40:5) and so closes the block where it began.

### Seeing Jesus Christ in the chapter

The New Testament uses 49:6 in two instructive ways.

- When Simeon holds the infant Jesus, he calls him "A light to lighten the Gentiles, and the glory of thy people Israel" ([Luke 2:32](https://www.churchofjesuschrist.org/study/scriptures/nt/luke/2?lang=eng)). The infant fills both halves of the servant's task, light to Gentiles and glory for Israel.
- Paul and Barnabas apply the same verse to their own mission: "I have set thee to be a light of the Gentiles, that thou shouldest be for salvation unto the ends of the earth" ([Acts 13:47](https://www.churchofjesuschrist.org/study/scriptures/nt/acts/13?lang=eng)).

Both uses are honest to the text. The servant's vocation is fulfilled in Christ, and his disciples share in it. Paul also quotes 49:8, "in the day of salvation have I succoured thee," and announces, "behold, now is the day of salvation" (2 Corinthians 6:2). Revelation 7:16–17 echoes 49:10 when it describes the Lamb leading the redeemed to "living fountains of waters."

Scripture Central's KnoWhy 23 also links the chapter's light to the Gentiles with Nephi's vision of his record going to the Gentiles ([KnoWhy 23](https://scripturecentral.org/knowhy/how-might-isaiah-48-49-be-likened-to-lehis-family)). It presents that as likening, not as a replacement for the Christ reading.

### Cross-references

- **Luke 2:29–32 and Acts 13:46–47.** Christ and his disciples as light to the Gentiles.
- **2 Corinthians 6:1–2.** The "acceptable time" of 49:8 made present.
- **1 Nephi 21 and 1 Nephi 22:1–12.** Nephi's text and commentary on gathering and the Gentiles.
- **3 Nephi 11:14–15, John 20:27, D&C 6:37.** The marks in the risen Lord's hands.
- **Revelation 7:16–17.** Fulfillment of 49:10.

### Questions for study and discussion

1. The servant says "I have laboured in vain" (49:4) and in the same verse "my judgment is with the LORD." How do you hold those statements together?
2. The servant is named "Israel" in 49:3 but restores Israel in 49:5–6. Which interpretations seem most persuasive, and what is gained by not resolving the tension too quickly?
3. Simeon and Paul both apply 49:6, one to Jesus and one to a missionary journey. What does that dual use teach about the relationship between Christ and his disciples?
4. Zion's complaint in 49:14 comes right after the song of 49:13. Why might the text place celebration and despair together?
5. Before applying 49:16 to yourself, read it as a promise about Zion's walls. How does that first meaning enrich the Christian reading of the Savior's hands?
6. In 49:21, Zion still remembers the children she lost. How does the text handle grief that remains alongside restoration?

## Synthesis

Isaiah 40–49 begins with a command to comfort and ends with Zion's children gathering and "all flesh" coming to know her Redeemer. Between those points, the chapters sustain one argument. Babylon looked like it had won. Its gods were carried in procession, its experts read the stars, and its queen said "I am, and none else." Israel was blind, deaf, burned, and afraid that its way was hidden from God.

The prophet answers with several claims:

- God is incomparable (chapters 40, 46).
- He alone foretells and directs history, even through a Persian king who does not know him (41, 44–45).
- He remains present in the waters and the fire (43).
- He refines rather than destroys (48).
- He remembers Zion more faithfully than a mother remembers her child (49).

Three cautions emerge from close reading.

1. **"Servant" names more than one figure.** The word applies to Israel in its calling and in its blindness, to a prophet, and to a figure who restores Israel and enlightens the nations. The New Testament recognizes that last figure in Jesus. Cyrus is a commissioned ruler, not a redeemer.
2. **The promises do not guarantee an easy life.** Isaiah 43:2 assumes waters and fire. Daniel's friends and Abinadi show that God's faithfulness can mean rescue or resurrection.
3. **Judgment language is not a license.** Babylon's fall is God's judgment on a merciless empire. It gives no permission to humiliate anyone. Israel's exile, read as judgment, does not make every sufferer guilty.

Within those cautions, the Christ-centered reading is well supported. The New Testament identifies Jesus with the coming LORD of 40:3, the gentle servant of 42:1–4, the "first and last" of 44:6 and 48:12, the one before whom every knee bows (45:23), and the light to the Gentiles of 49:6. Latter-day Saint scripture adds that the Holy One who says "beside me there is no saviour" is Jesus Christ. It also adds that the hands which remember Zion bear the prints of nails.

*Application:* The chapters do not ask readers to pretend that exile was not exile. They ask readers to wait upon the Lord, to bear witness while their own sight is still being healed, and to trust a God who carries his people from the womb to old age.
