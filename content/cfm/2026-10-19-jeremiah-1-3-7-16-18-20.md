# “Before I Formed Thee in the Belly I Knew Thee”

**Come, Follow Me study for October 19–25, 2026**
**Readings:** Jeremiah 1–3; 7; 16–18; 20 (204 verses)

This is a personal Latter-day Saint study guide, not an official publication of The Church of Jesus Christ of Latter-day Saints. It follows the week's assignment in the [official 2026 Come, Follow Me lesson](https://www.churchofjesuschrist.org/study/manual/come-follow-me-for-home-and-church-old-testament-2026/43?lang=eng) and quotes scripture from the King James Version. Throughout, I try to separate four kinds of statements: what a passage most likely meant in Jeremiah's world, what Latter-day Saints officially teach from it, what particular scholars have proposed, and my own devotional application. Those categories often support one another, but they are not the same thing.

## Historical and Literary Context

Jeremiah was "the son of Hilkiah, of the priests that were in Anathoth in the land of Benjamin" (Jeremiah 1:1). Anathoth was a village a few miles northeast of Jerusalem. Hilkiah was a common name. Some readers identify Jeremiah's father with the high priest Hilkiah who found the book of the law in Josiah's temple (2 Kings 22:8), but the text never makes that connection, and the phrase "priests that were in Anathoth" more naturally suggests a village priestly family.

The word of the Lord came to Jeremiah "in the thirteenth year" of King Josiah (1:2), about 627/626 BC. Josiah's reign brought a sweeping reform: local shrines were torn down and worship was centralized in Jerusalem (2 Kings 22–23). Josiah died at Megiddo, his son Jehoahaz reigned only briefly, and Pharaoh then placed Jehoiakim on the throne (2 Kings 23:29–35). Babylon soon became the dominant power. Under Jehoiachin, the city's treasures and many of its leaders were carried to Babylon (2 Kings 24:13–15). Zedekiah reigned until Jerusalem and its temple were destroyed about 587/586 BC. (Jeremiah 52:12–13.) Jeremiah lived through all of it, and later chapters show him carried to Egypt by survivors (Jeremiah 43:6–7). The [Bible Dictionary](https://www.churchofjesuschrist.org/study/scriptures/bd/jeremiah?lang=eng) reports a tradition that he was stoned there; it is a tradition, not a biblical record. We do not know his age at his call or the circumstances of his death.

Latter-day Saints remember that Jeremiah was Lehi's contemporary. [Jonn Claybaugh's study aid](https://interpreterfoundation.org/cfm-study-aids-old-testament-2022-lesson-42) gathers the Book of Mormon references: Jeremiah's prophecies were on the brass plates (1 Nephi 5:13), Nephi's brothers knew he had been imprisoned (1 Nephi 7:14), and Nephi the son of Helaman later cited him (Helaman 8:20).

The book of Jeremiah is not a diary arranged in order. It is a set of collections: poetic oracles, prose sermons, biographical stories, and personal laments. Jeremiah 36 describes how the prophet dictated his words to Baruch, how the king burned the scroll, and how it was rewritten "and there were added besides unto them many like words" (36:32). The Bible Dictionary groups chapters 1–6 under Josiah and 7–20 under Jehoiakim. That grouping is a helpful approximation, but many individual oracles in this week's chapters cannot be dated precisely.

Three features of the writing need care:

- **Several voices.** God speaks, Jeremiah speaks, a narrator speaks, and sometimes the people speak, quoted or imagined. Noticing who is talking prevents us from treating a prophet's anguished prayer as a divine command.
- **Corporate marriage imagery.** Chapters 2–3 picture the covenant as a marriage between the Lord and Israel and describe idolatry as adultery. The "wife" is the whole nation: kings, priests, and the men who ran its courts and shrines. The metaphor indicts covenant betrayal. It does not rank women as morally lower, justify violence, or model how human spouses should treat each other.
- **Interpreting disaster.** Jeremiah explains Judah's national catastrophe in covenant terms. That was a prophetic reading of a particular history. It is not a key for deciding that any suffering person today is being punished; Jesus explicitly refused that conclusion (John 9:2–3; Luke 13:1–5).

Jewish communities still read these chapters with deep seriousness. In a long-standing Jewish liturgical tradition, Jeremiah 1:1–2:3 is the first of three prophetic readings of affliction during the Three Weeks before the fast of Tisha B'Av, the Ninth of Av, which remembers the temple's destruction ([Chabad.org, "Matot Haftorah in a Nutshell"](https://www.chabad.org/parshah/article_cdo/aid/703054/jewish/Haftorah-in-a-Nutshell.htm)). Jeremiah 2:4–28 and 4:1–2 follow as the second ([Mendel Dubov, "Matot-Masei (Three Weeks Period) Haftarah Companion"](https://www.chabad.org/parshah/article_cdo/aid/3726251/jewish/Matot-Masei-Three-Weeks-Period-Haftarah-Companion.htm)). Custom and the calendar vary, so not every community reads these passages the same way every year. Christians and Latter-day Saints reading Jeremiah are reading the inheritance of a living people, not just an archive of ancient Israel's failures.

## Significant Insights for This Week

[John Hilton III's class slides](https://johnhiltoniii.com/findingchrist/class-42-fire-in-the-bones-jeremiah-1-3-7-16-18-20/) for this lesson organize the week around one question: how does God's word come to live deep in a person's heart, as it burned in Jeremiah's? The slides follow that question through four movements: being called, rejecting counterfeit devotion, choosing to turn back, and yielding to the potter. The question is worth keeping in mind through every chapter. Here are six threads I found especially illuminating.

**1. The God who forms.** In Jeremiah 1:5, "Before I formed thee" uses the Hebrew verb *yatsar*, the potter's word. The same root names the potter in Jeremiah 18:2–6 and describes God "framing" disaster in 18:11. It is also the verb of Genesis 2:7, where the Lord "formed man of the dust" (see the public-domain lexical entry, [Strong's 3335](https://biblehub.com/hebrew/3335.htm)). The lesson's title verse and its potter chapter share the vocabulary of shaping. Jeremiah was formed for a calling, and a nation was still on the wheel.

**2. The grammar of returning.** Hilton's slides note how Jeremiah plays on the Hebrew root *shuv* ("turn, return"): "backsliding" (*meshubah*) and "return" come from the same word. Jeremiah 3:22 compresses the whole gospel of repentance into that wordplay: "Return, ye backsliding children, and I will heal your backslidings." People who have turned away can turn again, and God heals the turning itself.

**3. Fountain or cistern.** Water runs through these chapters: the "fountain of living waters" and leaking cisterns (2:13), the Nile and Euphrates of foreign alliances (2:18), the tree planted by the waters (17:8), the Lord named again as the fountain (17:13), and the snow and cold streams of Lebanon (18:14). The underlying question is always where a people draws its life.

**4. Holy places require holy lives.** Jeremiah preaches at the temple gate (7:2), foresees a day without the ark (3:16), and stands at the city gates about the Sabbath (17:19). He does not despise any of these. He refuses to let them stand in for justice and loyalty. The Bible Dictionary identifies inwardness as one of the prophet's central themes: outward service is useless without devotion of heart and life.

**5. The womb at both ends.** The lesson title celebrates 1:5: "before thou camest forth out of the womb I sanctified thee." The week's last verse asks: "Wherefore came I forth out of the womb to see labour and sorrow?" (20:18). The same image frames a sacred call and a devastating lament. Being known by God before birth did not make Jeremiah's life easy, and scripture keeps both verses.

**6. Judgment is not the last word.** Some of the bleakest chapters in scripture contain gathering promises: one of a city and two of a family brought to Zion (3:14), and a new exodus that will outshine the old (16:14–15). Ancient readers saw return from exile. Latter-day Saints also see an ongoing latter-day gathering. In these chapters, the same God who announces scattering also promises to bring His people home.

## Jeremiah 1: A Prophet Formed, Called, and Strengthened

[Read Jeremiah 1](https://www.churchofjesuschrist.org/study/scriptures/ot/jer/1?lang=eng). The chapter has four movements: a heading (1–3), a call dialogue (4–10), two visions (11–16), and a renewed commission (17–19). It is framed by the same promise, "I am with thee... to deliver thee" (verses 8 and 19).

### Jeremiah 1:1–3 — The Words of a Man, the Word of the Lord

The book opens with "The words of Jeremiah" and then says at once that "the word of the Lord came" to him. Human words carry a divine word, and the book never pretends otherwise: Jeremiah's personality, protest, and grief stay audible. The heading names Josiah, Jehoiakim, and Zedekiah, and passes over the two three-month kings, Jehoahaz and Jehoiachin. It runs "unto the carrying away of Jerusalem captive in the fifth month," the month the city was burned (52:12). This marks the book's great turning point, not the end of Jeremiah's ministry, which continues in chapters 40–44.

Ancient meaning: God's word enters real history with dated reigns. Application: a calling may unfold across decades of unwelcome news, and faithfulness is measured over that whole span, not by a single season.

### Jeremiah 1:4–5 — Known, Sanctified, Ordained

The narration shifts to first person ("came unto me"), and God speaks with four verbs. *Formed* is the potter's word. *Knew* carries covenant weight: to know is to choose and commit, as in Amos 3:2. *Sanctified* means set apart. *Ordained* is literally "gave" or "appointed," and the appointment is broad: "a prophet unto the nations," which chapters 46–51 fulfill.

**Ancient meaning.** The verse speaks directly about Jeremiah's own prophetic vocation. Similar language describes the servant in Isaiah 49:1, 5, John the Baptist in Luke 1:15, and Paul in Galatians 1:15, who wrote that God "separated me from my mother's womb." Jewish interpreters and many Christians read it as God's purpose and foreknowledge for this prophet.

**Latter-day Saint teaching.** Latter-day Saints also read the verse as a witness of premortal life and foreordination. The official lesson pairs it with Abraham 3:22–23, Alma 13:1–4, and Doctrine and Covenants 138:53–56. Alma 13:3–4 grounds premortal callings in faith and choice. [Scripture Helps](https://www.churchofjesuschrist.org/study/manual/scripture-helps-old-testament/41-jeremiah-1-3-7-16-18-20?lang=eng) adds that foreordination does not guarantee any calling; opportunities still come through the righteous use of agency in mortality.

Two cautions follow. First, earthly circumstances such as health, wealth, nationality, or status are not a measure of premortal worth. Second, being known does not make every choice predetermined or life easy. Jeremiah's next forty years prove that.

**Christ connection.** The supreme case of foreordination is Christ, "who verily was foreordained before the foundation of the world" (1 Peter 1:20). Every other calling takes its meaning from His.

### Jeremiah 1:6 — "I Cannot Speak: For I Am a Child"

"Ah, Lord God! behold, I cannot speak: for I am a child." Jeremiah does not mean he is mute. He means he lacks the standing to speak with authority. Scripture Helps notes that the Hebrew word translated "child" can describe anyone from an infant to a young adult, so we should not invent an exact age. In a culture that honored elders, youth was a real disadvantage.

Jeremiah joins a line of hesitant prophets: Moses was "slow of speech" (Exodus 4:10), Enoch called himself "but a lad" (Moses 6:31), and Isaiah said he was "a man of unclean lips" (Isaiah 6:5). Application: the call narrative keeps the objection. Honest hesitation is not disqualifying in God's presence. It becomes the occasion for His reassurance.

### Jeremiah 1:7–8 — Go, Speak, Fear Not

"Say not, I am a child." God does not deny that Jeremiah is young. He denies that youth gets to decide the matter. Two commands follow, to go "to all that I shall send thee" and to speak "whatsoever I command thee," echoing the promise of a prophet like Moses (Deuteronomy 18:18).

"Be not afraid of their faces" anticipates hostile audiences. "I am with thee to deliver thee" recalls God's word to Moses, "Certainly I will be with thee" (Exodus 3:12). Deliverance here does not mean exemption from harm. Jeremiah will be beaten (20:2), jailed, and lowered into a muddy cistern (38:6). It means he will never be abandoned and his word will not be silenced. Christ makes the same promise to His disciples: "lo, I am with you alway" (Matthew 28:20).

### Jeremiah 1:9–10 — Words in the Mouth, Six Verbs

The Lord "touched my mouth." Isaiah's lips were purified with a coal (Isaiah 6:7). Here the touch simply places words: "I have put my words in thy mouth," almost verbatim from Deuteronomy 18:18. The New Testament applies that "prophet like Moses" promise to Jesus (Acts 3:22–23), who said He spoke only what the Father gave Him (John 12:49–50).

Verse 10 gives six verbs: "to root out, and to pull down, and to destroy, and to throw down, to build, and to plant." Four tear down and two build, which matches the proportion of Jeremiah's ministry, but the sequence ends in building. The verbs return across the book (18:7–9; 24:6; 31:28; 42:10; 45:4), and in 31:28 God promises to watch over Israel "to build, and to plant."

Ancient meaning: Jeremiah commands no army. He uproots and plants by announcing God's word. The verse authorizes proclamation, not violence. The official lesson invites readers to ask what living prophets "root out" and "plant" today. That is a fruitful application if we keep in mind that prophetic building is done with words, invitation, and covenant.

### Jeremiah 1:11–12 — The Almond Branch and the Watching God

"I see a rod of an almond tree." God answers, "I will hasten my word to perform it." Behind the English is a Hebrew pun: *shaqed* means almond ([Strong's 8247](https://biblehub.com/hebrew/8247.htm)), and *shoqed* means watching ([Strong's 8245](https://biblehub.com/hebrew/8245.htm)). The almond is traditionally explained as a "wakeful" tree because it blossoms early. "Hasten" is better understood as "I am watching over my word to perform it."

"Thou hast well seen" is God's affirmation of the young prophet's perception. The same verb returns in 31:28, where God watches first to pluck up and then to build. Application: God's watchfulness is not surveillance for its own sake. It is faithfulness to His promises, both warning and restoration.

### Jeremiah 1:13–16 — The Pot Tipping From the North

The second vision is "a seething pot; and the face thereof is toward the north," a boiling cauldron tilted so it will pour southward. "Evil" in verse 14 means disaster, not moral wickedness. Because of the desert to the east, invaders from Mesopotamia entered Israel's land from the north.

Over the course of the book, the northern threat is identified as Babylon (20:4; 25:9). Interpreters have proposed other identities for Jeremiah's earliest warnings, but we do not need a confident timeline to grasp the vision. Verse 15 imagines kings setting thrones "at the entering of the gates," and Jeremiah 39:3 later records Babylonian princes sitting "in the middle gate."

Verse 16 gives the legal reason: the people "have forsaken me, and have burned incense unto other gods, and worshipped the works of their own hands" (compare Isaiah 2:8). This is a prophet's covenant interpretation of a national crisis. It is not a formula for interpreting every invasion or hardship.

### Jeremiah 1:17–19 — A Fortified City Made of One Man

"Gird up thy loins" is the language of readying for work or battle. "Be not dismayed at their faces, lest I confound thee before them" is blunt: if fear of the people masters Jeremiah, he will be undone before them.

Jeremiah becomes "a defenced city, and an iron pillar, and brasen walls," the strong city Jerusalem was failing to be. The opposition includes everyone: "kings... princes... priests... and... the people of the land." The promise assumes conflict: "they shall fight against thee; but they shall not prevail." This is protection amid opposition, not freedom from injury.

Christ connection: "In the world ye shall have tribulation: but be of good cheer; I have overcome the world" (John 16:33).

**Questions to ponder**

- Which part of Jeremiah's call addresses my fears most directly: being known, being given words, or being promised companionship?
- How can a belief in foreordination encourage humility rather than a sense of being special?
- Where have I seen that God's presence does not remove opposition but keeps it from prevailing?

## Jeremiah 2: The Lord's Case Against His People

[Read Jeremiah 2](https://www.churchofjesuschrist.org/study/scriptures/ot/jer/2?lang=eng). Chapter 2 reads like a covenant lawsuit. The verb "plead" (2:9, 29, 35) belongs to the courtroom. God is plaintiff, Israel is defendant, and heaven is witness. Poetry, rhetorical questions, and vivid images replace legal prose.

### Jeremiah 2:1–3 — The Honeymoon Remembered

"Go and cry in the ears of Jerusalem." The charge opens with memory, not accusation: "I remember thee, the kindness of thy youth, the love of thine espousals." "Kindness" here is *hesed*, covenant loyalty. The wilderness, "a land that was not sown," is remembered tenderly. Hosea uses the same idea (Hosea 2:14–15), even though Numbers records plenty of complaining. God's memory dwells on the devotion.

"Israel was holiness unto the Lord" recalls the high priest's engraved plate (Exodus 28:36). "The firstfruits of his increase" belonged to God alone. Outsiders who ate holy things incurred guilt (Leviticus 22:10), so "all that devour him shall offend": nations that consume Israel answer for it.

In the Jewish liturgical tradition described above, the first reading ends here, on this note of tenderness. Christ connection: Paul calls the risen Christ "the firstfruits of them that slept" (1 Corinthians 15:20).

### Jeremiah 2:4–6 — What Did Your Fathers Find Wrong With Me?

The address widens to "O house of Jacob, and all the families of the house of Israel." Then comes a startling question: "What iniquity have your fathers found in me?" God invites the defendant to name His failure, as in Micah 6:3.

They "walked after vanity, and are become vain." "Vanity" (*hebel*) is breath or vapor. The same phrase appears in 2 Kings 17:15: people become as empty as what they worship (compare Psalm 115:8).

Verse 6 identifies the core failure as forgetfulness: they did not ask, "Where is the Lord that brought us up out of the land of Egypt?" The wilderness is described with piled-up terrors: deserts, pits, drought, "the shadow of death," and a land "where no man dwelt" (compare Deuteronomy 8:15). Seeking God begins with remembering what He has already done.

### Jeremiah 2:7–8 — A Good Land Defiled, a Leadership Gone Astray

"I brought you into a plentiful country" (literally, a garden land). "But when ye entered, ye defiled my land." The land belonged to God, and its purity depended on Israel's conduct (Leviticus 18:24–28; Numbers 35:33–34).

Verse 8 names four groups of leaders:
- priests, who never asked "Where is the Lord?";
- "they that handle the law," who "knew me not";
- "pastors" (shepherds, meaning rulers), who "transgressed";
- prophets, who "prophesied by Baal."

Handling the law without knowing its Giver is a sobering phrase. Jesus said something similar: one can "search the scriptures" and still refuse to "come to me" (John 5:39–40). Application: closeness to sacred things is not the same as knowing the Lord.

### Jeremiah 2:9–11 — Nations Keep Their Gods; Israel Traded Its Glory

"I will yet plead with you... and with your children's children" shows that the lawsuit spans generations. Then God sends Israel on an imaginary survey from west to east: "the isles of Chittim" (Cyprus and the Mediterranean coastlands) and "Kedar" (desert tribes of Arabia, Genesis 25:13). "See if there be such a thing."

Nations do not change their gods, "which are yet no gods." But Israel "changed their glory for that which doth not profit." Israel's glory was God Himself (compare Psalm 106:20). The irony is sharp: pagans are loyal to nothing, and Israel is disloyal to everything.

### Jeremiah 2:12–13 — Two Evils: Fountain and Cisterns

The heavens are summoned as witnesses and told to shudder, as in other covenant lawsuits (Deuteronomy 32:1; Isaiah 1:2). Then comes the chapter's central image: "my people have committed two evils; they have forsaken me the fountain of living waters, and hewed them out cisterns, broken cisterns, that can hold no water."

In a dry land, cisterns were plastered pits cut into rock to store rain. Cracked plaster meant a dry pit. "Living water" is flowing spring water. The second evil compounds the first: they exchanged free, flowing water for back-breaking labor that produces nothing.

**Christ connection.** Jesus offered water that "shall be in him a well of water springing up into everlasting life" (John 4:14) and invited the thirsty to come to Him (John 7:37–38). Latter-day Saints also recall Nephi's vision, where the fountain of living waters represents the love of God (1 Nephi 11:25). The official lesson suggests illustrating the verse with a cracked container.

**Application.** What am I hewing so hard to build, and does it hold anything?

### Jeremiah 2:14–17 — Why Is Israel Plundered?

"Is Israel a servant? is he a homeborn slave? why is he spoiled?" Israel was God's "son, even my firstborn" (Exodus 4:22), so how did a son end up as plunder? "Young lions" roared, cities burned "without inhabitant." This probably recalls the Assyrian devastation of the previous century.

"Noph and Tahapanes," that is, Memphis and an Egyptian frontier city, "have broken the crown of thy head," a picture of humiliation. Egypt's domination of Judah after Josiah's death (2 Kings 23:29–35) may be in view, though the oracle's exact date is uncertain.

Verse 17 answers its own question: "Hast thou not procured this unto thyself... when he led thee by the way?" This is a diagnosis of Judah's covenant history, not a rule that every misfortune is self-caused.

### Jeremiah 2:18–19 — Other Rivers, Bitter Correction

"What hast thou to do in the way of Egypt, to drink the waters of Sihor?" (an arm of the Nile) "or... in the way of Assyria, to drink the waters of the river?" (the Euphrates). Political alliances are pictured as drinking from foreign rivers, the cistern problem of verse 13 played out in diplomacy.

Verse 19: "Thine own wickedness shall correct thee, and thy backslidings shall reprove thee." The consequences are built into the choices themselves: "an evil thing and bitter... my fear is not in thee." Here "fear" means reverent awe. Application: sin often teaches through its own fruit. Yet compassion forbids assuming that someone else's pain is that kind of lesson.

### Jeremiah 2:20–22 — Broken Yoke, Degenerate Vine, Indelible Stain

"Of old time I have broken thy yoke." Translations differ on who breaks the yoke here and on the wording of Israel's reply, and I will not try to settle that question. In the KJV, God broke the yoke of bondage, and Israel answered, "I will not transgress," while wandering anyway. Either way, freedom given in the Exodus became freedom from God.

"Upon every high hill and under every green tree" describes the local shrines Deuteronomy 12:2 condemns. "Playing the harlot" is the covenant-adultery metaphor, applied to the whole nation and its leaders.

Verse 21: "I had planted thee a noble vine, wholly a right seed" (compare Isaiah 5:1–7; Psalm 80:8). How did it become "a strange vine"?

Verse 22: "Though thou wash thee with nitre, and take thee much soap, yet thine iniquity is marked before me." Self-scrubbing cannot remove covenant guilt. Christ connection: Jesus is "the true vine" (John 15:1), and cleansing comes as gift: "the blood of Jesus Christ his Son cleanseth us from all sin" (1 John 1:7).

### Jeremiah 2:23–25 — The Camel, the Wild Ass, and the Language of Craving

"How canst thou say, I am not polluted?" "See thy way in the valley" likely points to the valley of Hinnom (7:31). Two animal images follow. The first is "a swift dromedary traversing her ways," a young camel crisscrossing tracks without direction. The second is "a wild ass... that snuffeth up the wind at her pleasure"; in mating season, no one can restrain her, and pursuers need not tire themselves. The images portray the nation's compulsive chase after gods and allies. They are not commentary on women.

Verse 25 is almost pastoral: "Withhold thy foot from being unshod, and thy throat from thirst." In other words, stop running before your sandals wear through and your throat is parched. The reply is resignation: "There is no hope: no; for I have loved strangers."

Application: despair can become an excuse not to stop. Christ's invitation answers exactly that exhaustion: "Come unto me, all ye that labour" (Matthew 11:28).

### Jeremiah 2:26–28 — Shame, Wood, and Stone

"As the thief is ashamed when he is found" describes the shame of being caught, not the sorrow of repentance. It covers "their kings, their princes, and their priests, and their prophets."

They say "to a stock, Thou art my father; and to a stone, Thou hast brought me forth." The point is plain: they credit wood and stone with giving life. They turn "their back unto me, and not their face," yet in trouble they cry, "Arise, and save us."

God's reply is sardonic: "let them arise, if they can save thee... according to the number of thy cities are thy gods, O Judah." Application: crisis-only faith reveals what we actually trust the rest of the time.

### Jeremiah 2:29–30 — Correction Refused, Prophets Slain

"Wherefore will ye plead with me?" Israel files a countersuit, and God dismisses it: "ye all have transgressed." Verse 30: "In vain have I smitten your children; they received no correction." Hardships the prophet reads as corrective changed nothing.

"Your own sword hath devoured your prophets, like a destroying lion." This recalls Manasseh's "innocent blood" (2 Kings 21:16) and the prophet Urijah, executed under Jehoiakim (Jeremiah 26:20–23). Jesus grieved over the same pattern: "O Jerusalem, Jerusalem, which killest the prophets" (Luke 13:34).

### Jeremiah 2:31–33 — The Forgotten Bride

"Have I been a wilderness unto Israel? a land of darkness?" The God who led Israel through the wilderness asks whether He Himself has been barren to them. The people answer, "We are lords; we will come no more unto thee," meaning "we roam free."

Verse 32: "Can a maid forget her ornaments, or a bride her attire? yet my people have forgotten me days without number." This is the reverse of 2:2, where God remembers. Verse 33: "Why trimmest thou thy way to seek love?" Judah has become so skilled at chasing other loves that "thou hast also taught the wicked ones thy ways." Memory is the hinge of the chapter: God remembers, and Israel forgets.

### Jeremiah 2:34–35 — Innocent Blood on the Hem

"In thy skirts is found the blood of the souls of the poor innocents." This is not private ritual impurity. It is the blood of innocent poor people, stained on the nation's garments. "I have not found it by secret search" suggests the evidence is in plain sight, though the line is difficult and translations differ. Whatever its exact nuance, these deaths had no excuse.

Verse 35 adds the deeper charge: "Thou sayest, Because I am innocent... I will plead with thee, because thou sayest, I have not sinned" (compare 1 John 1:8). Idolatry and oppression go together in Jeremiah. Christ connection: what is done to "the least of these" is done to Him (Matthew 25:40).

### Jeremiah 2:36–37 — Hands on the Head

"Why gaddest thou about so much to change thy way?" Judah shifts alliances endlessly. "Thou also shalt be ashamed of Egypt, as thou wast ashamed of Assyria." Judah's earlier reliance on Assyria under Ahaz brought distress rather than help (2 Chronicles 28:20–21), and trust in Egypt will end the same way. "Yea, thou shalt go forth from him, and thine hands upon thine head": Judah will come away from that alliance in grief and disgrace, the gesture of Tamar's mourning (compare 2 Samuel 13:19). "The Lord hath rejected thy confidences."

**Questions to ponder**

- Which "broken cisterns" are culturally attractive today, and why do they seem easier than the fountain?
- Why does God begin the lawsuit with fond memory rather than accusation?
- What does verse 34 teach about the connection between worship and how a society treats the poor?

## Jeremiah 3: Can the Unfaithful Return?

[Read Jeremiah 3](https://www.churchofjesuschrist.org/study/scriptures/ot/jer/3?lang=eng). Chapter 3 continues the marriage imagery but bends it toward invitation. The verb *shuv*, turn or return, dominates. The chapter also shifts metaphors, from husband to father, as if no single picture can hold God's longing.

### Jeremiah 3:1 — A Legal Impossibility and an Invitation

The verse opens with the law of Deuteronomy 24:1–4: a divorced wife who marries another man may not return to her first husband. "Shall not that land be greatly polluted?" By that precedent, Israel's return to the Lord should be unthinkable, since she has "played the harlot with many lovers."

Then: "yet return again to me, saith the Lord." The Hebrew is compressed. The King James translators read it as an invitation, while some translators read an incredulous question: "and would you return to me?" That interpretive question is real. Yet the chapter's repeated calls in verses 12, 14, and 22 leave no doubt about the direction of God's heart. Grace exceeds legal precedent (compare Hosea 3:1). Again, this is a national metaphor, not instruction about human marriages.

### Jeremiah 3:2–3 — Bare Heights and Withheld Rain

"Lift up thine eyes unto the high places" points to the bare hilltops where shrines stood. The language is deliberately coarse, meant to shock a complacent audience. "As the Arabian in the wilderness" pictures a desert raider waiting by the road: Judah lies in wait for lovers.

"The showers have been withholden, and there hath been no latter rain," the spring rain crops needed. Deuteronomy 11:13–17 ties rain to covenant loyalty, and the prophet reads a particular drought in that light. "Thou hadst a whore's forehead, thou refusedst to be ashamed" describes a brazen face that will not blush.

### Jeremiah 3:4–5 — Pious Words, Unchanged Deeds

"Wilt thou not from this time cry unto me, My father, thou art the guide of my youth?" "Guide" means close companion; Proverbs 2:17 uses the same phrase of an unfaithful wife who forsakes "the guide of her youth." Verse 5 seems to quote the people's presumption: "Will he reserve his anger for ever?"

The verdict is this: "Behold, thou hast spoken and done evil things as thou couldest." Application: the right words, even "My father," cannot replace a changed life.

### Jeremiah 3:6–8 — Two Sisters

A prose section dated "in the days of Josiah the king" introduces two sisters. "Backsliding Israel" (almost a name, Apostasy-Israel) is the northern kingdom, which went "upon every high mountain and under every green tree." God said, "Turn thou unto me. But she returned not." Then "her treacherous sister Judah saw it."

The northern kingdom fell to Assyria, and verse 8 calls this a "bill of divorce" (compare Isaiah 50:1). Judah watched it happen and "feared not." Ezekiel 23 develops a similar parable.

### Jeremiah 3:9–11 — Feigned Return

"Through the lightness of her whoredom" means frivolity: Judah treated covenant betrayal as trivial and "committed adultery with stones and with stocks." Verse 10 is the chapter's sharpest line: "Judah hath not turned unto me with her whole heart, but feignedly." Josiah's reform purged shrines (2 Kings 23), but hearts did not follow.

Hilton's slides observe that "hypocrite" comes from a Greek word for a stage actor. Jesus warned against righteousness performed to be seen (Matthew 6:1) and against outward beauty over inward corruption (Matthew 23:27). Verse 11 concludes that Israel "justified herself more than treacherous Judah." Judah had more warning, so its pretense was worse (compare Luke 12:48).

### Jeremiah 3:12–13 — A Message to the Long-Exiled North

"Go and proclaim these words toward the north." This is an oracle of hope to northern Israelites scattered by Assyria a century earlier. "Return, thou backsliding Israel... I will not cause mine anger to fall upon you," literally, "I will not let my face fall upon you." "For I am merciful." Scripture Helps notes that "merciful" here is *hasid*, closely related to *hesed*, God's covenant loyalty.

The only condition: "Only acknowledge thine iniquity." Confession is the doorway (compare 1 John 1:9). Application: no exile is so old that God stops calling.

### Jeremiah 3:14–15 — Married to You; One of a City, Two of a Family

"Turn, O backsliding children... for I am married unto you." Hosea 2:16 shows how a prophet could set the language of husband against the name Baal: "thou shalt call me Ishi; and shalt call me no more Baali." Jeremiah may be making a similar point. Israel's true husband and Lord is the Lord.

"I will take you one of a city, and two of a family, and I will bring you to Zion." Anciently, this promised the return of a remnant. Latter-day Saints also read it as describing the latter-day gathering, which the official lesson describes as happening one by one, and the Bible Dictionary counts 3:12–19 among Jeremiah's gathering prophecies.

Verse 15 promises "pastors according to mine heart, which shall feed you with knowledge and understanding," in contrast to the failed shepherds of 2:8. Christ connection: Jesus is the "good shepherd" (John 10:11), and His under-shepherds are charged to feed the flock (1 Peter 5:2–4).

### Jeremiah 3:16–18 — When the Ark Is No Longer Missed

"When ye be multiplied and increased" echoes Genesis 1:28. Then a striking promise: "they shall say no more, The ark of the covenant... neither shall it come to mind: neither shall they remember it; neither shall they visit it." The ark was God's footstool (1 Chronicles 28:2), and its fate after Babylon is unknown.

The promise does not show disdain for Israel's worship. It expands it: "they shall call Jerusalem the throne of the Lord; and all the nations shall be gathered unto it" (compare Isaiah 2:2–3). God's presence will no longer be focused on one sacred box because it will fill a gathered city.

Verse 18 unites the divided nation: "the house of Judah shall walk with the house of Israel" (compare Ezekiel 37:15–22, which Latter-day Saints associate with the coming together of scriptural records and peoples). Christian readers find fulfillment in the Word who "dwelt among us" (John 1:14) and in the city where God dwells with His people (Revelation 21:3).

### Jeremiah 3:19–20 — A Father's Dream, a Husband's Grief

"But I said, How shall I put thee among the children, and give thee a pleasant land?" God thinks aloud. He longed to give this "daughter" a full heritage among sons. He expected her to say "My father; and shalt not turn away from me." The marriage image yields to a parent image.

Verse 20 returns to the marriage: "Surely as a wife treacherously departeth from her husband, so have ye dealt treacherously with me." The juxtaposition reveals divine disappointment that is personal, not bureaucratic. Christ connection: the father who runs to meet his returning son (Luke 15:20) expresses the same heart.

### Jeremiah 3:21–22 — Weeping on the Heights; the Return Begins

"A voice was heard upon the high places, weeping and supplications." The heights where sin was committed become the place of lament. God answers: "Return, ye backsliding children, and I will heal your backslidings." Repentance is paired with healing (compare Hosea 14:4).

Then a new voice: "Behold, we come unto thee; for thou art the Lord our God." Who speaks? Perhaps the people in an idealized future, perhaps Jeremiah modeling the prayer they should pray, perhaps a liturgical confession. The speaker is uncertain. What is certain is the contrast with verse 10: this is the whole-hearted turning feigned repentance lacked.

### Jeremiah 3:23–25 — A Penitent's Confession

"Truly in vain is salvation hoped for from the hills, and from the multitude of mountains: truly in the Lord our God is the salvation of Israel." The hill shrines delivered nothing.

"Shame hath devoured the labour of our fathers... their sons and their daughters." Several biblical names replace "Baal" with "shame" (*bosheth*), for example Ishbosheth for Eshbaal (compare 2 Samuel 2:8 with 1 Chronicles 8:33). Many interpreters therefore hear "the Shame" as Baal, devouring livestock and possibly children (compare 7:31).

The confession ends: "We lie down in our shame... for we have sinned against the Lord our God, we and our fathers." This is communal, generational honesty, like the prayers of Daniel 9 and Nehemiah 9. Christ connection: Jesus "endured the cross, despising the shame" (Hebrews 12:2), bearing shame so that penitents need not lie down in it forever.

**Questions to ponder**

- How does verse 10's "feignedly" help me recognize the difference between performed and real repentance?
- Why might God use several metaphors (husband, father, healer) for one relationship?
- What does the ark passage teach about holding sacred things reverently without confining God to them?

## Jeremiah 7: The Temple Sermon

[Read Jeremiah 7](https://www.churchofjesuschrist.org/study/scriptures/ot/jer/7?lang=eng). Jeremiah 26:1 dates a closely related temple sermon to "the beginning of the reign of Jehoiakim," and records that it nearly cost Jeremiah his life. Chapter 7 gives the sermon itself, followed by related oracles.

### Jeremiah 7:1–4 — Standing in the Gate

"Stand in the gate of the Lord's house" places Jeremiah where worshipers enter. The message is addressed to those who "enter in at these gates to worship the Lord": the devout, not the absent. "Amend your ways and your doings, and I will cause you to dwell in this place."

Then the warning: "Trust ye not in lying words, saying, The temple of the Lord, The temple of the Lord, The temple of the Lord, are these." The triple chant turns a holy name into a magic shield. Many interpreters connect this confidence to psalms celebrating Zion's security (Psalms 46; 48) and to Jerusalem's deliverance in Hezekiah's day (2 Kings 19:32–34). Scripture Helps explains that leaders taught the temple would protect them regardless of conduct. Notice that the sermon is preached at the temple, to defend its meaning, not to reject it.

### Jeremiah 7:5–7 — What Amending Means

"If ye throughly amend": the conditions follow.
- "Execute judgment between a man and his neighbour" means fair courts.
- "Oppress not the stranger, the fatherless, and the widow," the three groups Deuteronomy repeatedly protects (10:18; 24:17; 27:19).
- "Shed not innocent blood in this place."
- Do not "walk after other gods to your hurt."

Then "will I cause you to dwell in this place... for ever and ever." Justice and worship are not two separate religions. Christ connection: Jesus called justice and mercy "the weightier matters of the law" (Matthew 23:23), and James defines pure religion as caring for widows and orphans (James 1:27).

### Jeremiah 7:8–10 — Delivered to Do Abominations?

"Ye trust in lying words, that cannot profit." Verse 9 lists commandments from the Decalogue: steal, murder, commit adultery, swear falsely, and worship other gods (compare Exodus 20; Hosea 4:2). Verse 10 exposes the logic: people "come and stand before me in this house... and say, We are delivered to do all these abominations." Worship becomes a reset button that frees them to keep sinning.

Application: covenant worship should make me more just, not more comfortable.

### Jeremiah 7:11 — A Den of Robbers

"Is this house... become a den of robbers in your eyes? Behold, even I have seen it." A den is a cave where bandits hide between raids. The point is not that crimes happen inside the temple. The point is that criminals use it as a hideout.

Jesus quoted this verse when He cleansed the temple, combining it with Isaiah 56:7: "It is written, My house shall be called the house of prayer; but ye have made it a den of thieves" (Matthew 21:13). Mark adds "of all nations" (Mark 11:17). Like Jeremiah, Jesus loved the temple enough to confront its misuse (John 2:17), and like Jeremiah, He warned of its destruction (Mark 13:2).

### Jeremiah 7:12–15 — Remember Shiloh

"Go ye now unto my place which was in Shiloh, where I set my name at the first." The tabernacle once stood there (Joshua 18:1; 1 Samuel 1:3), and Psalm 78:60 says God "forsook the tabernacle of Shiloh." The Bible does not narrate Shiloh's destruction directly, and archaeologists debate its timing. The sermon needs only the remembered fact: sacred ground was no immunity.

"I spake unto you, rising up early and speaking" is a favorite idiom of Jeremiah for God's persistent effort. Verse 14 makes the comparison explicit: "this house... wherein ye trust... as I have done to Shiloh." Verse 15 adds "as I have cast out all your brethren, even the whole seed of Ephraim," the northern kingdom. Jeremiah 26:8–9 records the crowd seizing him for saying this.

### Jeremiah 7:16 — "Pray Not Thou for This People"

"Pray not thou for this people... for I will not hear thee." Moses (Exodus 32:11–14) and Samuel (1 Samuel 12:23) were famous intercessors, so this command signals that this particular judgment is settled. Similar commands appear in 11:14 and 14:11.

It is a specific oracle, not a universal ban on praying for sinners. Jeremiah himself later says he "stood before thee to speak good for them" (18:20) and tells exiles to pray for Babylon (29:7). Christ connection: Jesus prayed for His executioners (Luke 23:34) and "ever liveth to make intercession" (Hebrews 7:25). Christians are commanded to pray for those who mistreat them (Matthew 5:44).

### Jeremiah 7:17–19 — A Family Project for the Queen of Heaven

"Seest thou not what they do?" Every member of the family takes part: "The children gather wood, and the fathers kindle the fire, and the women knead their dough, to make cakes to the queen of heaven." The identity of this goddess is debated. Proposals include a Mesopotamian astral goddess, Astarte, or another figure, and the text does not say. Jeremiah 44:15–19 later shows refugees defending her worship, insisting life went better when they offered to her. Even disaster was interpreted in competing ways.

Verse 19: "Do they provoke me to anger?... do they not provoke themselves to the confusion of their own faces?" Idolatry injures the worshiper more than it injures God.

### Jeremiah 7:20 — Anger Poured on Everything

"Mine anger and my fury shall be poured out upon this place, upon man, and upon beast, and upon the trees of the field, and upon the fruit of the ground; and it shall burn, and shall not be quenched." The language of totality describes how invasion devastates people, animals, orchards, and crops together. Human covenant failure reaches the whole created order, which Paul says "groaneth" awaiting redemption (Romans 8:22).

### Jeremiah 7:21–23 — Eat Your Sacrifices; Obey My Voice

"Put your burnt offerings unto your sacrifices, and eat flesh." Burnt offerings were wholly burned for God (Leviticus 1), while other sacrifices were partly eaten. God's sarcasm is this: go ahead, combine them and eat it all, since without obedience it is only meat.

Verse 22, "I spake not unto your fathers... concerning burnt offerings or sacrifices," should not be read as denying that the Torah contains sacrificial law (Leviticus 1–7 clearly does). It is rhetoric about priority. At the Exodus, the foundational demand was this: "Obey my voice, and I will be your God, and ye shall be my people" (compare Exodus 19:5–6).

The theme runs through scripture: "to obey is better than sacrifice" (1 Samuel 15:22); "I desired mercy, and not sacrifice" (Hosea 6:6), which Jesus quoted (Matthew 9:13). Latter-day Saints remember that the risen Christ replaced burnt offerings with "a broken heart and a contrite spirit" (3 Nephi 9:19–20).

### Jeremiah 7:24–26 — Backward, Not Forward

"They hearkened not... but walked in the counsels and in the imagination of their evil heart, and went backward, and not forward." "Imagination" means stubbornness, a word Jeremiah uses repeatedly (3:17; 16:12; 18:12). God sent prophets "daily rising up early," and still "they hardened their neck: they did worse than their fathers." Divine persistence meets human persistence. Application: spiritual life always moves in some direction.

### Jeremiah 7:27–28 — Speak Even Though They Will Not Hear

"Thou shalt speak all these words unto them; but they will not hearken to thee." Claybaugh asks why the Lord would keep a prophet preaching to people who will not listen, and points to Moroni's resolve to keep laboring among a hardened people (Moroni 9:6). Possible answers include a faithful witness, the few who might listen, and a record for later generations.

Verse 28: "This is a nation that obeyeth not... truth is perished, and is cut off from their mouth." "Truth" here also means faithfulness: when integrity dies, it disappears from speech first.

### Jeremiah 7:29–31 — Cut Hair, Polluted House, Burned Children

"Cut off thine hair, O Jerusalem... and take up a lamentation on high places." Shaving the head was a mourning gesture (Job 1:20; Micah 1:16).

Verse 30: "They have set their abominations in the house which is called by my name." This recalls Manasseh's altars and image in the temple (2 Kings 21:4–7).

Verse 31 names the worst crime: "the high places of Tophet... in the valley of the son of Hinnom, to burn their sons and their daughters in the fire; which I commanded them not, neither came it into my heart." Some apparently believed child sacrifice could please the Lord (compare Micah 6:7). God's repudiation is total: it was never His command, not even His thought. The Torah forbids it (Leviticus 18:21; Deuteronomy 12:31), and Josiah defiled Tophet "that no man might make his son or his daughter to pass through the fire" (2 Kings 23:10).

### Jeremiah 7:32–34 — Valley of Slaughter, Silence of the Bride

Tophet will be renamed "the valley of slaughter: for they shall bury in Tophet, till there be no place." The place where children died becomes a mass grave in the coming war. Verse 33 describes corpses as "meat for the fowls... and none shall fray them away." No one is left to drive the birds off, fulfilling a covenant curse (Deuteronomy 28:26).

Verse 34 ends in silence: "the voice of mirth... the voice of the bridegroom, and the voice of the bride" will cease. Yet Jeremiah 33:10–11 later promises that these same voices will return.

The Hebrew name of this valley became "Gehenna," which Jesus used for final judgment (Matthew 5:22). Christ connection: the restored wedding voice points to the Bridegroom (Mark 2:19–20) and "the marriage of the Lamb" (Revelation 19:7).

**Questions to ponder**

- What "lying words" might give religious people false security today?
- How does Jesus's use of 7:11 deepen my understanding of His temple cleansing?
- Why might God speak through Jeremiah to people He knows will not listen?

## Jeremiah 16: A Prophet's Life as a Sign of Judgment and Gathering

[Read Jeremiah 16](https://www.churchofjesuschrist.org/study/scriptures/ot/jer/16?lang=eng). In chapter 16, Jeremiah's private life becomes public preaching, like other prophetic sign acts (Isaiah 20; Ezekiel 4–5; 24:15–24). Then the chapter turns surprisingly to a new exodus.

### Jeremiah 16:1–4 — No Wife, No Children, in This Place

"Thou shalt not take thee a wife, neither shalt thou have sons or daughters in this place." In a culture where marriage and children were among life's greatest blessings, Jeremiah's single life became a walking announcement of catastrophe. The command is specific: "in this place," during Jerusalem's crisis. It does not condemn marriage. [Noe Correa](https://scripturecentral.org/archive/books/book-chapter/jeremiah-13-7-1618-20) points out that the same prophet later tells exiles in Babylon to marry and have children (29:5–6), which shows the command belonged to its moment.

Verses 3–4 describe parents and children dying "of grievous deaths... as dung upon the face of the earth." This is war's reach, not a claim that children deserved it. Christ connection: on the road to Golgotha, Jesus warned the daughters of Jerusalem of days when people would say "Blessed are the barren, and the wombs that never bare" (Luke 23:29).

### Jeremiah 16:5–7 — No Mourning, Because Mercy Is Withdrawn

"Enter not into the house of mourning... for I have taken away my peace from this people... even lovingkindness and mercies." "Lovingkindness" is *hesed*. Jeremiah's absence from funerals signals a time when the ordinary consolations of covenant life will collapse.

Verses 6–7 describe customs that will stop: burial, lament, cutting, shaving for the dead (practices Deuteronomy 14:1 forbade, but which people apparently still observed), and comforting the bereaved with food and "the cup of consolation."

This does not condemn mourning. Jeremiah weeps elsewhere (9:1), and Latter-day Saints covenant to "mourn with those that mourn" (Mosiah 18:9). Jesus wept at a grave (John 11:35).

### Jeremiah 16:8–9 — No Feasting Either

"Thou shalt not also go into the house of feasting." Joy will cease as well as grief: "I will cause to cease out of this place in your eyes, and in your days, the voice of mirth... the voice of the bridegroom, and the voice of the bride." The words echo 7:34 and add urgency: "in your days." The prophet's absence from both funerals and feasts tells Jerusalem that ordinary life is ending.

### Jeremiah 16:10–13 — "What Is Our Iniquity?"

God anticipates the people's question: "Wherefore hath the Lord pronounced all this great evil against us?" The answer has two parts. "Your fathers have forsaken me," but also "ye have done worse than your fathers; for... ye walk every one after the imagination of his evil heart." The present generation is accountable for its own choices, not merely suffering for ancestors (compare 31:29–30; Ezekiel 18).

Verse 13 adds a bitter irony: in exile, "there shall ye serve other gods day and night," so they will have their fill of what they chose.

### Jeremiah 16:14–15 — A New Exodus

"Therefore, behold, the days come." Hope breaks in abruptly. Israel swore oaths by "The Lord liveth, that brought up the children of Israel out of the land of Egypt." A day will come when the oath changes to "from the land of the north, and from all the lands whither he had driven them." The promise appears again in 23:7–8.

**Ancient meaning.** Return from Babylon and from the wider dispersion. Jewish readers have long found hope here.

**Latter-day Saint teaching.** The Bible Dictionary calls this latter-day gathering greater in proportion than the Exodus under Moses. The official lesson notes President Nelson's teaching that gathering Israel is the most important work on earth today.

**Christ connection.** Jesus longed to gather Jerusalem's children (Matthew 23:37) and died to "gather together in one the children of God that were scattered abroad" (John 11:52).

### Jeremiah 16:16–18 — Fishers and Hunters

"Behold, I will send for many fishers... and after will I send for many hunters, and they shall hunt them from every mountain, and from every hill, and out of the holes of the rocks."

**In its immediate context,** the verse reads sternly. Verse 17 says "mine eyes are upon all their ways: they are not hid from my face," and verse 18 says "first I will recompense their iniquity and their sin double." Prophets elsewhere used fishing imagery for conquerors who carry people away (Amos 4:2; Habakkuk 1:14–15), and "holes of the rocks" are where people hide in judgment (Isaiah 2:19). Many readers therefore understand fishers and hunters as captors who find fugitives.

**Latter-day Saint application.** [Scripture Helps](https://www.churchofjesuschrist.org/study/manual/scripture-helps-old-testament/41-jeremiah-1-3-7-16-18-20?lang=eng) notes President Russell M. Nelson's 2006 teaching likening missionaries to fishers and hunters who seek scattered Israel.

I do not think we need to erase either reading. In context, the search is inescapable and exposes sin. In application, the same thoroughness becomes the search of love. Jesus Himself turned fishing toward rescue: "I will make you fishers of men" (Matthew 4:19). Verse 18's "double" recalls Isaiah 40:2, and "carcases of their detestable... things" pictures idols as corpses defiling God's inheritance.

### Jeremiah 16:19–21 — The Nations Renounce Their Inheritance of Lies

Jeremiah prays: "O Lord, my strength, and my fortress, and my refuge in the day of affliction." Then he looks far ahead: "the Gentiles shall come unto thee from the ends of the earth, and shall say, Surely our fathers have inherited lies." Gentiles, too, will confess their ancestors' errors, like Judah in 3:25.

"Shall a man make gods unto himself, and they are no gods?" God answers: "I will this once cause them to know... my name is The Lord." This is the covenant name revealed at the Exodus (Exodus 3:14–15). Christ connection: Paul foresaw every tongue confessing "that Jesus Christ is Lord" (Philippians 2:11). Latter-day Saints see the gathering as open to all nations who come to Him.

**Questions to ponder**

- How can a crisis-specific command like 16:2 teach without being turned into a universal rule?
- How do I hold together the contextual and latter-day readings of 16:16?
- What "inherited lies" might I need to renounce, as the Gentiles do in verse 19?

## Jeremiah 17: Engraved Hearts, Rooted Trees, and Sabbath Gates

[Read Jeremiah 17](https://www.churchofjesuschrist.org/study/scriptures/ot/jer/17?lang=eng). Chapter 17 is a collection: an inscription oracle, a wisdom poem, proverbs, Jeremiah's personal confession, and a prose Sabbath sermon. Like the rewritten scroll to which "many like words" were added (36:32), it gathers material of different kinds, and parts of verses 3–4 resemble 15:13–14.

### Jeremiah 17:1–2 — Sin Engraved Where Atonement Belongs

"The sin of Judah is written with a pen of iron, and with the point of a diamond" (a very hard stone used for engraving). It is "graven upon the table of their heart." "Table" means tablet, like the tablets of the commandments. Instead of the law, sin is inscribed. Jeremiah 31:33 later promises the reverse: "I will... write it in their hearts" (compare 2 Corinthians 3:3).

Sin is also engraved "upon the horns of your altars," precisely where atoning blood was applied (Leviticus 4:7; 16:18). Verse 2 adds that "their children remember their altars and their groves." Children learn worship from what parents actually do (contrast Deuteronomy 6:7). Application: what will my children remember about my worship?

### Jeremiah 17:3–4 — A Grim Sabbath for the Land

"O my mountain in the field" refers to Jerusalem. "I will give thy substance and all thy treasures to the spoil."

Verse 4: "Thou, even thyself, shalt discontinue from thine heritage." Judah will lose the inheritance it was given. 2 Chronicles 36:21 later interprets the exile as the time when the land "enjoyed her sabbaths." The verse thus anticipates the Sabbath sermon at the end of the chapter, and "ye have kindled a fire in mine anger" anticipates its last line.

### Jeremiah 17:5–6 — The Shrub in Salt Land

"Cursed be the man that trusteth in man, and maketh flesh his arm, and whose heart departeth from the Lord." Latter-day Saints hear Nephi's echo: "I will not put my trust in the arm of flesh" (2 Nephi 4:34). Such a person is "like the heath in the desert," a scrubby desert shrub (the exact plant is uncertain), which "shall not see when good cometh." Even when rain arrives, its location keeps it from benefiting. It lives in "parched places... a salt land and not inhabited." The contrast with the next verses is not effort but place: this shrub depends on a parched setting, while the tree has access to water.

### Jeremiah 17:7–8 — The Tree That Still Faces Drought

"Blessed is the man that trusteth in the Lord." He is "as a tree planted by the waters... and shall not see when heat cometh, but her leaf shall be green; and shall not be careful in the year of drought, neither shall cease from yielding fruit." Psalm 1:3 is the obvious parallel.

Read carefully: heat comes, and the year of drought comes. The tree is not exempt. It is rooted. "Not be careful" means not anxious, and the fruit continues. This poem does not promise a hardship-free life to the faithful. It promises a source that reaches deeper than the season.

Christ connection: "abide in me... the same bringeth forth much fruit" (John 15:4–5). Latter-day Saints also recall Alma's tree springing up unto everlasting life (Alma 32:41).

### Jeremiah 17:9–10 — Who Can Know the Heart?

"The heart is deceitful above all things, and desperately wicked: who can know it?" The heart, which in Hebrew thought includes mind and will, can deceive even its owner.

The question has an answer: "I the Lord search the heart, I try the reins" (literally the kidneys, the inner self), "even to give every man according to his ways." This is not a blanket condemnation of emotion or of careful judgment. It is a call to humility about self-knowledge, and an invitation like the psalmist's: "Search me, O God" (Psalm 139:23). The risen Christ claims the same role: "I am he which searcheth the reins and hearts" (Revelation 2:23).

### Jeremiah 17:11 — The Partridge Proverb

"As the partridge sitteth on eggs, and hatcheth them not; so he that getteth riches, and not by right, shall leave them in the midst of his days, and at his end shall be a fool." This is a proverb, built on an ancient folk observation about a bird whose brooding comes to nothing. The point is not ornithology. It is the emptiness of wealth gained unjustly, which deserts its owner. Jesus told of a rich man God called "fool" on the night his life was required (Luke 12:20). The verse condemns exploiters; it is not a claim that the exploited lose their reward.

### Jeremiah 17:12–14 — Throne, Fountain, Healing

"A glorious high throne from the beginning is the place of our sanctuary." This is praise for God's true dwelling. "O Lord, the hope of Israel," those who forsake Him "shall be written in the earth, because they have forsaken the Lord, the fountain of living waters." Names written in dust vanish, unlike names in God's book (Exodus 32:32; Daniel 12:1). Chapter 2's fountain returns here.

Verse 14 is the prophet's personal prayer: "Heal me, O Lord, and I shall be healed; save me, and I shall be saved: for thou art my praise." Christ connection: Jesus, the physician who came for the sick (Mark 2:17), offers healing to all who return (3 Nephi 9:13).

### Jeremiah 17:15–16 — "Where Is the Word of the Lord?"

The scoffers taunt: "Where is the word of the Lord? let it come now." The judgment Jeremiah predicted has not arrived, so they mock him (compare Isaiah 5:19; 2 Peter 3:4).

Jeremiah defends his motives: "I have not hastened from being a pastor to follow thee: neither have I desired the woeful day." The first clause is difficult; it may mean he has not run from his shepherding task. The second is clear: he never wanted disaster. "That which came out of my lips was right before thee."

### Jeremiah 17:17–18 — "Be Not a Terror Unto Me"

"Be not a terror unto me: thou art my hope in the day of evil." The plea sounds like an answer to his call, where God told him not to be "dismayed" (1:17). Jeremiah is holding God to that early promise.

Verse 18 turns fierce: "Let them be confounded that persecute me... bring upon them the day of evil, and destroy them with double destruction." Like imprecatory psalms (Psalm 35), this is anguish placed before God, not a model for seeking revenge. Scripture records it honestly. The gospel directs us to leave vengeance to God (Romans 12:19) and to "love your enemies" (Matthew 5:44).

### Jeremiah 17:19–20 — At Every Gate

"Go and stand in the gate of the children of the people, whereby the kings of Judah come in... and in all the gates of Jerusalem." City gates were courts, markets, and the king's route, the arteries of public life. The audience is everyone: "ye kings of Judah, and all Judah, and all the inhabitants of Jerusalem." The Sabbath was a matter for royal policy as well as household practice.

### Jeremiah 17:21–23 — Bear No Burden

"Take heed to yourselves... bear no burden on the sabbath day, nor bring it in by the gates." There should be no carrying loads out of houses and no work: "hallow ye the sabbath day, as I commanded your fathers."

The commandment protected rest for servants, animals, and strangers (Deuteronomy 5:12–15), so commercial traffic on the Sabbath was a justice issue too (compare Amos 8:5; Nehemiah 13:15–22). Verse 23 adds, "But they obeyed not... but made their neck stiff."

### Jeremiah 17:24–26 — A Conditional National Promise

"If ye diligently hearken... then shall there enter into the gates of this city kings and princes sitting upon the throne of David... and this city shall remain for ever." People will come from Benjamin, "the plain" (the western lowlands), "the mountains," and "the south" (the Negev), the whole land, bringing burnt offerings, grain offerings, incense, and "sacrifices of praise."

This was a covenant promise to Judah as a nation. Sabbath observance would show whether Judah would let God's rhythm limit its commerce. It is not a guarantee that individual Sabbath keepers today will avoid illness, financial strain, or national crisis.

Latter-day Saint application: Doctrine and Covenants 59:9–13 and Isaiah 58:13–14 present the Sabbath as a delight and a means of staying "unspotted." Christ connection, not as a prediction but as a clarification: "The sabbath was made for man, and not man for the sabbath" (Mark 2:27), and the Son of Man is Lord of it.

### Jeremiah 17:27 — Fire in the Gates

"But if ye will not hearken... then will I kindle a fire in the gates thereof, and it shall devour the palaces of Jerusalem, and it shall not be quenched." The gates where burdens entered will burn. Babylon burned "the king's house, and all the houses of Jerusalem" (Jeremiah 52:13). Application: the Sabbath is a gift and a covenant sign, not a magic charm. Its blessing is relationship with the Lord of rest.

**Questions to ponder**

- In what ways does the tree in 17:8 describe faith during hardship, not the absence of it?
- How does Jeremiah's honesty in 17:14–18 change my sense of what prayer can hold?
- What "burdens" might I lay down to hallow the Sabbath, and why?

## Jeremiah 18: The Potter, the Nations, and the Prophet's Anguish

[Read Jeremiah 18](https://www.churchofjesuschrist.org/study/scriptures/ot/jer/18?lang=eng). The potter passage is often read individually first. It is better to begin where the text begins, with "O house of Israel" and "a nation, and... a kingdom." Personal application follows as a secondary, devotional reading.

### Jeremiah 18:1–4 — At the Potter's Wheel

"Arise, and go down to the potter's house, and there I will cause thee to hear my words." The revelation comes through ordinary craft. The potter "wrought a work on the wheels." "The vessel that he made of clay was marred in the hand of the potter: so he made it again another vessel, as seemed good to the potter."

Two details matter. The flaw appears while the clay is still soft, and the potter reworks rather than discards it. In chapter 19, by contrast, Jeremiah shatters an earthen bottle, "a potter's vessel, that cannot be made whole again" (19:11). Set side by side, the two sign acts teach different things: at the wheel, a marred vessel is remade; at Hinnom, a finished vessel is smashed, a breaking the text calls beyond repair.

### Jeremiah 18:5–6 — As Clay in the Hand

"O house of Israel, cannot I do with you as this potter?... as the clay is in the potter's hand, so are ye in mine hand." God speaks to the nation. Isaiah uses the image to stress God's authority (Isaiah 29:16; 45:9) and to plead for mercy: "we are the clay, and thou our potter" (Isaiah 64:8). Paul applies it to God's freedom in salvation history (Romans 9:20–21).

Note what the verse does not say. It does not claim that every individual tragedy is God's shaping hand.

### Jeremiah 18:7–8 — When God Relents

"At what instant I shall speak concerning a nation... to pluck up, and to pull down, and to destroy it." This is the vocabulary of 1:10, applied to any nation. "If that nation... turn from their evil, I will repent of the evil that I thought to do unto them."

In the KJV, God's "repenting" means relenting or changing an announced course. It is not God confessing a moral fault. "Evil" means disaster. Nineveh is the classic case (Jonah 3:10). Jeremiah's own trial recalled that Hezekiah's repentance caused God to relent from Micah's threatened disaster (Jeremiah 26:18–19).

### Jeremiah 18:9–10 — When Promised Good Is Withdrawn

"At what instant I shall speak... to build and to plant it; If it do evil... then I will repent of the good." Promises of blessing are conditional too (compare 1 Samuel 2:30). Prophecy is not fatalism. It is covenant address that invites response. Latter-day Saints know the principle: "I, the Lord, am bound when ye do what I say; but when ye do not what I say, ye have no promise" (Doctrine and Covenants 82:10).

**Devotional application, clearly secondary.** The official lesson invites children to ponder what it means to be clay in the Lord's hands, with Isaiah 64:8. The image can encourage softness to the Spirit, willingness to be reshaped, and hope that a "marred" life is not discarded. It should not be pushed into fatalism, into the idea that God predestines wickedness, or into the claim that God causes abuse or tragedy to improve us.

### Jeremiah 18:11–12 — God's Plan Meets the People's "No Hope"

"Behold, I frame evil against you." "Frame" is *yotser*, the potter's verb. God is shaping disaster on the wheel, yet the clay can still change: "return ye now every one from his evil way."

The reply chills: "There is no hope: but we will walk after our own devices, and we will every one do the imagination of his evil heart." "Devices" mirrors God's "device." The people set their plans against His. "No hope" echoes 2:25. Despair here is not humility. It is refusal.

### Jeremiah 18:13–15 — Unnatural Forgetting

"Ask ye now among the heathen... the virgin of Israel hath done a very horrible thing." Even in accusation, God calls Israel by a tender title.

Verse 14 is hard to translate. "Will a man leave the snow of Lebanon...? or shall the cold flowing waters that come from another place be forsaken?" The general sense is that Lebanon's snowcaps and mountain streams are reliable. Nature keeps faith, as in 8:7 and Isaiah 1:3. People do not: "my people hath forgotten me."

Verse 15: they "burned incense to vanity," stumbled "from the ancient paths" (compare 6:16, "ask for the old paths"), and took "a way not cast up," an unbuilt track. The water motif returns: God is the dependable spring Israel abandoned.

### Jeremiah 18:16–17 — Hissing, Head-Wagging, East Wind

The land becomes "a perpetual hissing," the whistle of astonished passersby. Each "shall be astonished, and wag his head." At the cross, passersby "reviled him, wagging their heads" (Matthew 27:39). Christ entered the place of the mocked and desolate.

Verse 17: "I will scatter them as with an east wind." "I will shew them the back, and not the face" mirrors 2:27, where they turned "their back unto me, and not their face." The image is severe: the withdrawal of God's shining face, the opposite of the priestly blessing (Numbers 6:25–26).

### Jeremiah 18:18 — Confidence in the Institutions

The narrator reports the critics: "Come, and let us devise devices against Jeremiah; for the law shall not perish from the priest, nor counsel from the wise, nor the word from the prophet." They trust priests, sages, and prophets, institutions God had given, and conclude that this lone voice cannot be right. Ezekiel 7:26 warns that law and counsel can perish. "Let us smite him with the tongue": slander first, violence later. Application: respect for God-given institutions should open our ears, not shut them.

### Jeremiah 18:19–20 — Evil for Good

Jeremiah turns to God: "Give heed to me, O Lord." "Shall evil be recompensed for good? for they have digged a pit for my soul" (compare Psalm 35:7, 12). His sharpest pain is this: "Remember that I stood before thee to speak good for them, and to turn away thy wrath from them." The intercessor has become the target. Christ connection: Jesus, who prayed for His own, was hated "without a cause" (John 15:25).

### Jeremiah 18:21–23 — A Fierce Imprecation

These verses must not be softened. Jeremiah prays: "deliver up their children to the famine, and pour out their blood by the force of the sword; and let their wives be bereaved... and be widows... let their young men be slain." He asks for "a cry... from their houses" when soldiers arrive suddenly. Then: "forgive not their iniquity, neither blot out their sin from thy sight."

How should we read this? It is the speech of a man whose life is being plotted against ("all their counsel against me to slay me"). Its terms largely repeat judgments God had already announced, but its fury is Jeremiah's own. Scripture preserves it, as it preserves Psalms 109 and 137. This passage records a prayer, not an enacted assault: Jeremiah places his anguish before God, and God's reply is not recorded here. Elsewhere in the book he weeps for his people (9:1). [Correa](https://scripturecentral.org/archive/books/book-chapter/jeremiah-13-7-1618-20) observes that this outburst did not disqualify him as a prophet.

It is not a prayer template, and it need not be dismissed. Hebrew lament gives sufferers words for betrayal and terror and hands judgment over to God (compare Romans 12:19). Christ prayed within that tradition of lament, crying a psalm from the cross (Mark 15:34), and He also prayed, "Father, forgive them; for they know not what they do" (Luke 23:34); Stephen followed Him (Acts 7:60). Application: bring rage to God rather than acting it out, and let Christ, who knew both the cry of lament and the prayer of forgiveness, teach us what to pray next.

**Questions to ponder**

- How does reading 18:6–10 nationally first change my understanding of the potter image?
- What does it mean that God "relents," and why does that make prophecy an invitation?
- How can I be honest with God about anger the way Jeremiah was, while learning from Christ's response to enemies?

## Jeremiah 20: Stocks, a New Name, and the Fire in the Bones

[Read Jeremiah 20](https://www.churchofjesuschrist.org/study/scriptures/ot/jer/20?lang=eng). Chapter 19 ends with Jeremiah smashing a clay bottle at Hinnom and repeating the message in the temple court (19:14–15). Chapter 20 records the official response and the most searing of Jeremiah's personal laments, which scholars often call his "confessions."

### Jeremiah 20:1–2 — Beaten and Put in the Stocks

"Pashur the son of Immer the priest" belonged to a priestly house (1 Chronicles 24:14) and was "chief governor in the house of the Lord," an overseer responsible for temple order. (A different Pashur appears in 21:1.) He "heard," and he "smote Jeremiah the prophet, and put him in the stocks," a device that held the body in a cramped position. They were "in the high gate of Benjamin, which was by the house of the Lord." A priest's son from Benjamin was humiliated at a gate of Benjamin, in public, at the temple, by a fellow priest.

### Jeremiah 20:3–6 — Magor-missabib

Released "on the morrow," Jeremiah speaks first: "The Lord hath not called thy name Pashur, but Magor-missabib," meaning "terror on every side." The phrase recurs across Jeremiah (6:25; 46:5; 49:29) and appears in Psalm 31:13. Pashur will become "a terror to thyself, and to all thy friends."

Then comes the first time this book names Babylon outright: "I will give all Judah into the hand of the king of Babylon." Verse 5 lists the city's wealth and royal treasures carried off (compare 2 Kings 24:13). Verse 6 announces exile for Pashur's household and death in Babylon for him and "all thy friends, to whom thou hast prophesied lies." The priest had also prophesied, presumably promising safety (compare 14:13–15). The beaten man in the stocks speaks the word of the One who holds the outcome.

### Jeremiah 20:7–8 — "Thou Hast Deceived Me"

"O Lord, thou hast deceived me, and I was deceived: thou art stronger than I, and hast prevailed." The Hebrew verb can mean entice, seduce, or persuade. It is used of seducing a young woman (Exodus 22:16) and of luring Ahab to his doom (1 Kings 22:20–22). Scripture Helps notes the range "enticed" or "persuaded." [S. Kent Brown](https://scripturecentral.org/archive/books/book-chapter/jeremiahs-crisis-faith) argues that the negative sense should be kept: Jeremiah felt tricked.

We should not rush to tidy this up. He feels overpowered: "I am in derision daily, every one mocketh me." Verse 8: "I cried violence and spoil; because the word of the Lord was made a reproach unto me."

Brown also proposes, drawing on Herodotus, that an early northern Scythian threat appeared and then withdrew, making Jeremiah's warnings seem false for a time. In this guide we treat that as a historical hypothesis about his early reception, not a demonstrable explanation for this lament. Christ connection: Jesus "offered up prayers and supplications with strong crying and tears" (Hebrews 5:7).

### Jeremiah 20:9 — Fire Shut Up in the Bones

"Then I said, I will not make mention of him, nor speak any more in his name." He tried to quit. "But his word was in mine heart as a burning fire shut up in my bones, and I was weary with forbearing, and I could not stay."

The young man who said "I cannot speak" (1:6) and received words in his mouth (1:9) now has the word burning in his heart and cannot keep silent. Notice that fire is not comfortable. He is "weary," and the verse describes compulsion and pain together. Amos asked, "who can but prophesy?" (Amos 3:8), and Paul said, "woe is unto me, if I preach not the gospel!" (1 Corinthians 9:16). Disciples at Emmaus felt their hearts "burn within" them as the risen Christ opened the scriptures (Luke 24:32). The burning does not cancel the lament; verses 14–18 still follow.

### Jeremiah 20:10 — Friends Waiting for a Stumble

"I heard the defaming of many, fear on every side." His own phrase is turned back on him, perhaps as mockery. "Report, say they, and we will report it," meaning denounce him. "All my familiars," his close friends, "watched for my halting." They hope "Peradventure he will be enticed... and we shall take our revenge on him." The word recalls his complaint in verse 7: he feels enticed by God, and his friends hope he will be enticed into ruin.

Psalm 41:9 laments "mine own familiar friend," and Jesus applied it to His betrayal (John 13:18).

### Jeremiah 20:11–13 — Trust, Testing, and Praise

"But the Lord is with me as a mighty terrible one": a warrior, recalling 1:8 and 19. "Therefore my persecutors shall stumble," the very stumble they hoped for in him, "and they shall not prevail," echoing the promise of 1:19.

Verse 12: "O Lord of hosts, that triest the righteous, and seest the reins and the heart" (compare 17:10; 11:20). "Let me see thy vengeance on them: for unto thee have I opened my cause." He commits the case to God rather than taking it himself.

Verse 13 bursts into song: "Sing unto the Lord, praise ye the Lord: for he hath delivered the soul of the poor from the hand of evildoers." The praise is real. The chapter does not end there.

### Jeremiah 20:14–16 — Cursing the Day of Birth

Immediately after praise: "Cursed be the day wherein I was born: let not the day wherein my mother bare me be blessed." Job opens his lament the same way: "Let the day perish wherein I was born" (Job 3:3).

Jeremiah does not curse God or his parents. He curses a day and a messenger, "the man who brought tidings to my father, saying, A man child is born unto thee; making him very glad." He wishes that man to be "as the cities which the Lord overthrew, and repented not." This recalls Sodom and Gomorrah, and uses the "relent" verb of 18:8. He wishes him to hear battle cries "in the morning, and the shouting at noontide." These are poetic curses expressing how unbearable his life has become. They are not divine judgments on anyone.

### Jeremiah 20:17–18 — "Wherefore Came I Forth Out of the Womb?"

"Because he slew me not from the womb; or that my mother might have been my grave." The charge against the messenger is impossible, a sign of poetic extremity, not literal blame. It is not a judgment on his mother, on birth, or on newborns.

The chapter ends with a question, not a resolution: "Wherefore came I forth out of the womb to see labour and sorrow, that my days should be consumed with shame?" Compare 1:5: "before thou camest forth out of the womb I sanctified thee." The verse behind this week's title and this anguished cry use the same image. Scripture holds both.

We should not diagnose Jeremiah from a distance, and we should not shame people who feel what he felt. Jesus, too, prayed in anguish: "My soul is exceeding sorrowful, even unto death" (Matthew 26:38), and "My God, my God, why hast thou forsaken me?" (Mark 15:34). Lament is a form of faithful prayer.

The book continues beyond this chapter; elsewhere God renews Jeremiah's commission (15:19–21), and Jeremiah keeps speaking for years. Application: when someone around us is in Jeremiah's place, the gospel calls us to "mourn with those that mourn" (Mosiah 18:9), to stay near, and to help them find good support, not to leave them to endure alone.

**Questions to ponder**

- Why might scripture preserve 20:14–18 right after the praise of 20:13?
- How do 1:5 and 20:18 together reshape what "known before birth" means?
- What does the "fire in the bones" feel like in my own discipleship, including its weariness?

## Weekly Discussion and Study Plan

**Monday, October 19 — Jeremiah 1.** Read the call aloud, noticing every "I" God speaks. List what God gives Jeremiah: knowledge, words, presence, strength. Read Alma 13:3–4 and Abraham 3:22–23 alongside verse 5, and write one sentence about how foreordination and agency fit together.

**Tuesday, October 20 — Jeremiah 2.** Mark each water image in chapter 2: the fountain, the broken cisterns, the foreign rivers. Then read John 4:10–14 and talk about where your family actually draws its life.

**Wednesday, October 21 — Jeremiah 3.** Circle every form of "return," "turn," and "backsliding." Read verses 10 and 22 side by side. Discuss the difference between feigned and whole-hearted return.

**Thursday, October 22 — Jeremiah 7.** Read verses 1–11, then Matthew 21:12–13 and Mark 11:15–17. Ask what makes worship "profit" (7:8). Consider one concrete act of justice toward a "stranger, fatherless, or widow" this week.

**Friday, October 23 — Jeremiah 16–17.** Compare the two readings of 16:16. Sketch the shrub and the tree of 17:5–8, and add the drought; discuss what roots mean when the heat still comes. Plan one change to make the coming Sabbath more holy.

**Saturday, October 24 — Jeremiah 18 and 20.** If possible, work soft clay and then try to reshape a hardened piece. Read 18:6–10 as a statement about nations first. Then read 20:7–18 slowly, and notice the movement from complaint to resolve to praise to lament.

**Sunday, October 25 — Review and share.** Return to the question carried all week: how does God's word come to live deep in the heart? Share one verse that has done that for you.

**Discussion questions for the week**

1. Jeremiah was told he would be opposed but not overcome. How does that kind of promise differ from a promise of ease, and why might it be more trustworthy?
2. The temple, the ark, and the Sabbath are all honored in these chapters, yet none of them can replace covenant faithfulness. How do we honor sacred things without trusting in them as substitutes for justice and love?
3. Jeremiah's prophecies combine severe judgment with gathering hope. How should a disciple hold both without becoming either harsh or naive?
4. Jeremiah prays furious prayers (18:21–23) and despairing ones (20:14–18). What do these teach about honest prayer, and what do Christ's prayers add?
5. "Before I formed thee... I knew thee." How might believing this about every person, not just ourselves, change the way we treat others?
