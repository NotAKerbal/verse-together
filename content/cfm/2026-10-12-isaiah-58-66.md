# The Redeemer Shall Come to Zion: A Study Guide to Isaiah 58–66

*A personal Latter-day Saint study resource for Come, Follow Me, October 12–18, 2026. This is not an official Church publication.*

## Introduction and Historical Context

Isaiah 58–66 closes one of scripture's most quoted books. The chapters begin by examining worship, especially fasting and the Sabbath, and end with new heavens and a new earth. Along the way the reader meets an anointed messenger, watchmen who will not stay silent, a warrior in red garments, a community praying through grief, and a mother city giving birth to a nation in a day. The reading is uneven by design. Accusation turns into promise, promise into lament, and lament into divine answer. Readers who expect a single smooth argument may feel lost. Readers who follow the changes of voice will find a conversation between a struggling people and a God who refuses to abandon them.

### The setting the book gives

The book places Isaiah son of Amoz in the reigns of Uzziah, Jotham, Ahaz, and Hezekiah ([Isaiah 1:1](https://www.churchofjesuschrist.org/study/scriptures/ot/isa/1?lang=eng)). This was Judah's eighth-century crisis under Assyrian pressure (see the [Bible Dictionary entry on Isaiah](https://www.churchofjesuschrist.org/study/scriptures/bd/isaiah?lang=eng)). The final chapters, however, speak to a wider horizon:

- **Ruin.** The sanctuary has been "trodden down" (63:18). The "holy and beautiful house" has been "burned up with fire" (64:10–11).
- **Rebuilding.** Old wastes and "desolations of many generations" are to be rebuilt (58:12; 61:4).

These images match the biblical record of Jerusalem's fall in [2 Kings 25](https://www.churchofjesuschrist.org/study/scriptures/ot/2-kgs/25?lang=eng) and the decree permitting return in [Ezra 1](https://www.churchofjesuschrist.org/study/scriptures/ot/ezra/1?lang=eng). Scholars and believers have long discussed how these chapters relate to the eighth-century prophet. This guide does not try to settle questions of composition or set precise dates. It reads the chapters as they stand. They address a people living among ruins who are trying to worship faithfully, rebuild, and hope.

### Three horizons of meaning

Several layers of meaning appear throughout this guide. They should be kept distinct:

1. **The ancient horizon.** What the words said to Judah and Jerusalem, in poetry drawn from their landscape, temple, and history.
2. **The Christian fulfillment.** Jesus read Isaiah 61 in the Nazareth synagogue and declared it fulfilled ([Luke 4:16–21](https://www.churchofjesuschrist.org/study/scriptures/nt/luke/4?lang=eng)). Paul quoted Isaiah 59:20 and 65:1–2 (Romans 11; Romans 10).
3. **The latter-day application.** The Restoration applies Zion, gathering, and the Second Coming to God's work now and in the future.

All three are legitimate, and none cancels the others. Jewish readers have treasured these chapters for millennia as promises to Israel and Jerusalem. A Christian reading does not need to dismiss that. Christ himself came to Zion as a son of Jacob.

### What "Zion" means

In these chapters, Zion is first Jerusalem, often personified as a woman: bride, mother, or mourner. It is also God's gathered covenant community, defined by righteousness and his presence, which echoes the scriptural ideal of a people of "one heart and one mind" ([Moses 7:18](https://www.churchofjesuschrist.org/study/scriptures/pgp/moses/7?lang=eng)).

The images of nations bowing and bringing wealth (Isaiah 60–61) are poetic reversals of humiliation. They are not a charter for ethnic superiority or for dominating others today. Isaiah's own climax gathers "all nations and tongues" to see God's glory (66:18).

### How the genres shift

Watch for these forms:

- prophetic accusation (58:1–5; 59:1–8)
- communal confession (59:9–15)
- the divine warrior (59:15–20; 63:1–6)
- salvation oracles and songs to Zion (60–62)
- historical remembrance (63:7–14)
- lament and prayer (63:15–64:12)
- God's reply, with judgment and new creation (65–66)

### The official lesson in brief

The Church's lesson for this week, ["The Redeemer Shall Come to Zion"](https://www.churchofjesuschrist.org/study/manual/come-follow-me-for-home-and-church-old-testament-2026/42?lang=eng), emphasizes several themes:

- fasting that cares for the poor
- the Sabbath as a delight
- Jesus Christ as intercessor, healer, and Redeemer
- Zion's light
- Christ's future reign

It explicitly links Isaiah 61 with Luke 4:16–21, and it points readers to President Henry B. Eyring's 2015 message on fasting.

## Significant Points for This Week

These are the threads I have chosen to follow. They are my own selection, made for close reading and personal application.

1. **True worship includes our neighbor.** Isaiah 58 does not reject fasting or the Sabbath. It refuses to let them become private performances while workers are exploited and the hungry go unfed.
2. **God's arm acts when no one else can.** "There was no man… no intercessor" (59:16; compare 63:5). The Redeemer intervenes in a world where human justice has collapsed.
3. **Light is received before it is reflected.** "Arise, shine; for thy light is come" (60:1). Zion shines because the Lord rises on her, not because of her own brilliance.
4. **The Anointed One exchanges ashes for beauty.** Isaiah 61 is the mission statement Jesus claimed for himself. It is about binding, freeing, comforting, and clothing.
5. **New names mark renewed covenant.** Forsaken becomes Hephzibah, and Desolate becomes Beulah (62:4).
6. **Judgment and mercy both belong to redemption.** The red-garmented warrior of 63:1–6 is first a figure of judgment. The chapter then pivots to God's tender suffering with his people (63:9).
7. **Honest lament is a form of faith.** Isaiah 63:15–64:12 models praying through disaster with confession, protest, and trust: "we are the clay, and thou our potter."
8. **God answers with a new creation.** Isaiah 65–66 describe secure homes, long lives, answered prayers, and worship by "all flesh." The book still ends soberly, warning that rebellion has consequences.

## Isaiah 58: The Fast That I Have Chosen

Text: [Isaiah 58 (KJV)](https://www.churchofjesuschrist.org/study/scriptures/ot/isa/58?lang=eng)

### Isaiah 58:1–2 — A Trumpet Against Religious People

The prophet is told to cry "like a trumpet," the instrument that sounded alarms and assembled people for holy days. The target is surprising. The people are not careless pagans. They "seek me daily," "delight to know my ways," and "take delight in approaching to God."

Verse 2 says they behave "as a nation that did righteousness." The word "as" carries the critique: the outward form resembles covenant faithfulness. The irony is gentle but sharp. Their religious activity is visible, but the comparison exposes a gap between ritual and righteousness. They want God's "ordinances of justice," meaning his rulings and decisions, while ignoring justice toward others.

*Application:* Regular religious activity can coexist with blind spots. The chapter tests the people's seeking by asking what it has produced in their treatment of others.

### Isaiah 58:3–5 — Complaints at the Fast and God's Reply

**The people's complaint (verse 3a).** Isaiah quotes the people's own words: "Wherefore have we fasted… and thou seest not?" Fasting has become a transaction. We afflict ourselves, so God should notice.

**God's answer (verses 3b–4).**
- "In the day of your fast ye find pleasure, and exact all your labours." Employers fast while still driving their workers.
- The fast produces "strife and debate" and even violence, "the fist of wickedness."
- Such fasting will not "make your voice to be heard on high."

**The rhetorical questions (verse 5).** These questions mock fasting reduced to display: bowing the head "as a bulrush," a reed drooping in the wind, and lying on "sackcloth and ashes." These were genuine mourning customs. God is not condemning them. He is asking whether posture alone can be "an acceptable day to the Lord."

### Isaiah 58:6–7 — Loosing Bands and Opening the House

God now defines the fast he has "chosen." The verbs pile up: *loose, undo, let go free, break* every yoke. Then come concrete acts:

- dealing bread to the hungry
- bringing the "poor that are cast out" into one's house
- covering the naked
- refusing to "hide… from thine own flesh"

That last phrase treats the needy as kin. Avoiding them is a form of hiding.

This is the core of the chapter. Fasting does not become less spiritual. It becomes more embodied. The hunger of the faster is joined to the hunger of the neighbor. Compare [Matthew 25:35–40](https://www.churchofjesuschrist.org/study/scriptures/nt/matt/25?lang=eng), where feeding and clothing "the least of these" is service to Christ himself.

**Official teaching:**
- President Henry B. Eyring taught that fast offerings both relieve physical need and change the giver's heart, and he connected succoring others with serving Christ in Matthew 25 ([Eyring, 2015](https://www.churchofjesuschrist.org/study/general-conference/2015/04/is-not-this-the-fast-that-i-have-chosen?lang=eng)).
- Elder Ulisses Soares, in October 2026, described a fast that joins joyful worship, purposeful prayer, trust in God's will, and generous care for others. He taught that generous fast offerings put Isaiah's lifting of burdens into practice. He also observed that fasting and prayer may give strength to carry a burden rather than remove it, and that answers may differ from what we expect ([Soares, "A Higher and Holier Fast"](https://www.churchofjesuschrist.org/study/general-conference/2026/10/17soares?lang=eng)). His footnote recognizes those whose health does not allow fasting, nursing mothers, and young children who should not be compelled. Isaiah's emphasis on relieving burdens fits that compassion.

**Teaching suggestion:** Jonn Claybaugh's 2022 Interpreter Foundation study aid invites readers to list the acts named in Isaiah 58:6–7, then choose one and plan a specific way to improve in living the fast ([study aid](https://interpreterfoundation.org/cfm-study-aids-old-testament-2022-lesson-41)). It is an independent study aid, not official Church teaching, but it turns the chapter's many verbs into a single achievable step.

### Isaiah 58:8–9 — Light, Healing, and "Here I Am"

"Then" introduces the blessings. Light breaks "as the morning." Health "spring[s] forth speedily," like new skin over a wound. Righteousness goes ahead, and "the glory of the Lord shall be thy rearward," or rear guard. The image recalls the pillar of cloud and fire surrounding Israel in the Exodus.

The heart of the promise answers verse 3's complaint. Before, the people asked why God did not see. Now "thou shalt call, and the Lord shall answer… Here I am." God uses the words of ready servants such as Abraham and Samuel. He makes himself present to those who make themselves present to others.

Verse 9b adds conditions. The people must remove "the yoke," "the putting forth of the finger" (scornful accusation), and "speaking vanity," meaning empty or harmful speech.

### Isaiah 58:10–12 — Drawing Out the Soul

Verse 10 deepens verse 7. It is not enough to hand out bread. The faster must "draw out thy soul to the hungry," offering attention, sympathy, and self along with food. Then "thy light [shall] rise in obscurity, and thy darkness be as the noonday."

Verse 11 brings guidance "continually," satisfaction "in drought," strengthened bones, and two water images: "a watered garden" and "a spring… whose waters fail not." A garden needs water from outside, while a spring supplies water to others. The person who blesses the hungry is both.

Verse 12 moves from person to city. The people will rebuild "old waste places" and be named "The repairer of the breach, The restorer of paths to dwell in." A breach is a gap in a wall. Repairing it restores protection. Restoring paths makes a city livable again.

**Scholar's proposal:** Donald W. Parry highlights how Isaiah 58 is structured as an exchange between the Lord and Israel, setting outward ritual against help for the destitute. He reads the garden and the guarding glory as images of blessing, and he connects the Sabbath "heritage of Jacob" with covenant promises made to the fathers ([Parry, Isaiah 58](https://scripturecentral.org/archive/books/book-chapter/isaiah-58)). This is a scholarly reading, not official interpretation.

### Isaiah 58:13–14 — Calling the Sabbath a Delight

The phrase "turn away thy foot" suggests stepping back from trampling holy time. Isaiah sets "my holy day" against "thy pleasure," "thine own ways," and "thine own words." The contrast is not between joy and boredom. It is between self-direction and delight in the Lord. Verse 14 rewards those who "call the sabbath a delight" with the promise "Then shalt thou delight thyself in the Lord."

There are two further blessings. The first is riding "upon the high places of the earth," an image of security and triumph drawn from [Deuteronomy 32:13](https://www.churchofjesuschrist.org/study/scriptures/ot/deut/32?lang=eng). The second is being fed with "the heritage of Jacob."

The Sabbath here continues the fast's ethic. Both are times set apart, and both turn us from self toward God and others.

**Official teaching:** President Russell M. Nelson taught that the Sabbath is a sign between God and his people, and that worship and service can make it a delight ([Nelson, 2015](https://www.churchofjesuschrist.org/study/general-conference/2015/04/the-sabbath-is-a-delight?lang=eng)).

**Christ connection:** Jesus healed on the Sabbath and taught that "the sabbath was made for man" ([Mark 2:27](https://www.churchofjesuschrist.org/study/scriptures/nt/mark/2?lang=eng)). In doing so he loosed bands on the holy day, which joins verse 6 and verse 13.

**Questions for Isaiah 58:**
- Where might my worship resemble verse 2, sincere but partial?
- What "yoke" could I help lift this month?
- What would make my Sabbath feel like delight rather than restriction?

## Isaiah 59: No Intercessor, So His Arm Brought Salvation

Text: [Isaiah 59 (KJV)](https://www.churchofjesuschrist.org/study/scriptures/ot/isa/59?lang=eng)

### Isaiah 59:1–2 — Not God's Weakness but Our Separation

The chapter answers the same complaint as 58:3: why does God not act? Two body images reply. The Lord's "hand is not shortened," meaning his reach and power are intact. His "ear [is not] heavy," meaning he is not deaf.

The problem lies elsewhere: "your iniquities have separated between you and your God." The word "separated" pictures a dividing barrier. Sins have "hid his face," the face that in priestly blessing shines upon Israel ([Numbers 6:24–26](https://www.churchofjesuschrist.org/study/scriptures/ot/num/6?lang=eng)). The distance is real, but it is not caused by divine incapacity. That is a hopeful diagnosis. A barrier can be removed.

### Isaiah 59:3–4 — A Body Given to Wrong

Isaiah inventories the body:
- hands "defiled with blood"
- fingers with iniquity
- lips speaking lies
- tongue muttering perverseness

Verse 4 widens the view to public life. "None calleth for justice, nor any pleadeth for truth" probably pictures legal settings where no one brings an honest case. The verse ends with a pregnancy image: "they conceive mischief, and bring forth iniquity." Wrong is planned, carried, and delivered.

### Isaiah 59:5–6 — Viper Eggs and Spider Webs

Two vivid images follow:

- **Viper eggs.** People "hatch cockatrice' eggs," the eggs of a venomous serpent. Eating them kills. Crushing them releases a viper. The schemes are deadly whether embraced or disturbed.
- **Spider webs.** They "weave the spider's web," intricate but useless as clothing: "Their webs shall not become garments." Their works cannot cover them.

That last line anticipates the chapter's turn. Human weaving cannot clothe; God himself will put on garments in verse 17.

### Isaiah 59:7–8 — Feet That Run to Evil

The body inventory continues with feet. They "run to evil" and hurry "to shed innocent blood." Thoughts, too, are iniquity. "Wasting and destruction are in their paths."

Verse 8 describes a road network gone wrong. "The way of peace they know not." There is no judgment (justice) in their goings. Paths are made "crooked," and everyone traveling them loses peace. Sin is pictured as a public infrastructure that misleads others.

Paul quotes verses 7–8 in [Romans 3:15–17](https://www.churchofjesuschrist.org/study/scriptures/nt/rom/3?lang=eng) as part of his argument that all need God's righteousness.

**Scholar's proposal:** Parry notices how hands, fingers, lips, tongue, and feet each take part in wrongdoing. He traces truth's public collapse in verses 14–15, God's own arm bringing salvation, and the divine warrior's armor, which he compares with Paul's "whole armour of God" in [Ephesians 6:10–18](https://www.churchofjesuschrist.org/study/scriptures/nt/eph/6?lang=eng) ([Parry, Isaiah 59](https://scripturecentral.org/archive/books/book-chapter/isaiah-59)).

### Isaiah 59:9–11 — The People Confess: Groping at Noon

In verse 9 the voice shifts from "they" to "we." The community confesses. "Therefore is judgment far from us." They wait for light and walk in darkness, which is the reverse of 58:10's promise that darkness would become noonday.

In verse 10 they "grope for the wall like the blind" and "stumble at noonday as in the night." Some are "in desolate places as dead men."

Verse 11 gives two animal sounds: they "roar… like bears" in frustration and "mourn sore like doves" in grief. They look for judgment and salvation, and both feel "far off."

*Application:* Spiritual disorientation is described honestly, without pretense. Confession begins by naming the darkness.

### Isaiah 59:12–13 — "Our Iniquities, We Know Them"

Verse 12 is the turning point of honesty. "Our transgressions are multiplied before thee… our sins testify against us… we know them." The community no longer blames God's hand or ear.

Verse 13 lists the forms of the wrong:
- transgressing and lying against the Lord
- departing from God
- "speaking oppression and revolt"
- conceiving and uttering falsehood "from the heart"

The language of 59:4 returns, but now the people own it.

### Isaiah 59:14–15 — Truth Fallen in the Street

Isaiah personifies civic virtues as people in a ruined city:
- Judgment is "turned away backward."
- Justice "standeth afar off."
- Truth "is fallen in the street," collapsed in the public square.
- Equity "cannot enter," shut out at the gate.

Verse 15 adds a bitter result: the person who "departeth from evil maketh himself a prey." Honesty becomes dangerous.

Then the divine turn: "the Lord saw it, and it displeased him that there was no judgment." God's seeing, which the people doubted in 58:3, is now affirmed.

### Isaiah 59:16–17 — The Lord Puts On His Armor

"He saw that there was no man, and wondered that there was no intercessor." An intercessor is one who steps between, pleads, and intervenes. Finding none, "his arm brought salvation unto him; and his righteousness, it sustained him."

Verse 17 dresses the divine warrior:
- righteousness as a breastplate
- a helmet of salvation
- garments of vengeance
- zeal as a cloak

The people's spider-web garments could not cover them (verse 6). God's garments are effective.

**Christ connection:** The lesson emphasizes Christ as intercessor. Where Isaiah finds "no man," Christians see the one Mediator who stood in the gap ([1 Timothy 2:5](https://www.churchofjesuschrist.org/study/scriptures/nt/1-tim/2?lang=eng); [Hebrews 7:25](https://www.churchofjesuschrist.org/study/scriptures/nt/heb/7?lang=eng)). Paul transfers the armor to believers in Ephesians 6, so that we wear what God first wore.

### Isaiah 59:18–19 — Repayment and the Rising Standard

Verse 18 promises repayment "according to their deeds," reaching even "to the islands," the distant coastlands. Justice will be thorough and wide.

The purpose is revelation, not mere punishment. "So shall they fear the name of the Lord from the west, and his glory from the rising of the sun." Reverence will span east to west.

The final image: when "the enemy shall come in like a flood, the Spirit of the Lord shall lift up a standard against him." A standard is a banner or rallying signal. The Spirit raises it against the flood.

### Isaiah 59:20–21 — The Redeemer Comes to Zion

The week's title comes from verse 20: "the Redeemer shall come to Zion, and unto them that turn from transgression in Jacob." The coming has a direction. He comes to those who turn, picking up the confession of verses 12–13. Paul quotes this line, in its Greek form, in [Romans 11:26](https://www.churchofjesuschrist.org/study/scriptures/nt/rom/11?lang=eng) with hope for all Israel.

Verse 21 is a covenant addressed to a singular "thee." God's Spirit "upon thee" and his words "in thy mouth" will not depart from "thy seed, nor… thy seed's seed… for ever." Revelation passing through generations is the antidote to verse 4's lying mouths.

*Application:* The cure for a culture of falsehood is a covenant people carrying God's words to their children and grandchildren.

**Questions for Isaiah 59:**
- Which of my "body parts," such as hands, tongue, or feet, most need consecrating?
- Where have I seen "truth fallen in the street," and how can I help lift it?
- What does Christ as intercessor mean to me personally?

## Isaiah 60: Arise, Shine

Text: [Isaiah 60 (KJV)](https://www.churchofjesuschrist.org/study/scriptures/ot/isa/60?lang=eng)

### Isaiah 60:1–3 — Light Has Come

"Arise, shine; for thy light is come." The commands are addressed to Zion as a woman, perhaps lying in the dust of mourning. The reason precedes her action: light has already come, and "the glory of the Lord is risen upon thee."

Verse 2 sets the contrast: "darkness shall cover the earth, and gross darkness the people," but the Lord "shall arise upon thee." Zion's glory is reflected glory, like a city catching the dawn.

Verse 3: "the Gentiles shall come to thy light, and kings to the brightness of thy rising." Zion does not conquer the nations. She attracts them.

**Christ connection:** Jesus is "the light of the world" and tells his followers to let their light shine ([John 8:12](https://www.churchofjesuschrist.org/study/scriptures/nt/john/8?lang=eng); [Matthew 5:14–16](https://www.churchofjesuschrist.org/study/scriptures/nt/matt/5?lang=eng)). The order is the same: received light, then shared light.

### Isaiah 60:4–5 — Children Return and the Heart Grows

"Lift up thine eyes round about, and see." Zion, who perhaps had been looking down in grief, is told to look around. Her sons come "from far," and daughters are "nursed at thy side," carried on the hip as small children were.

Verse 5 describes her emotional response: she will "flow together," or be radiant, and her heart will "fear, and be enlarged." She trembles and swells with joy at once. The "abundance of the sea" and "forces of the Gentiles" (their wealth and strength) turn toward her.

### Isaiah 60:6–7 — Caravans and Flocks

Ancient trade routes pour in:
- camels and dromedaries from Midian and Ephah
- people from Sheba, bringing "gold and incense," who "shew forth the praises of the Lord"
- flocks of Kedar and rams of Nebaioth, Arabian peoples, offered "with acceptance on mine altar"

The purpose is worship: "I will glorify the house of my glory." Matthew's wise men bringing gold and frankincense to the infant Jesus echo verse 6 ([Matthew 2:11](https://www.churchofjesuschrist.org/study/scriptures/nt/matt/2?lang=eng)).

### Isaiah 60:8–9 — Like Clouds and Doves

"Who are these that fly as a cloud, and as the doves to their windows?" The question pictures ships with white sails, or returning people, streaming like birds heading home to their dovecotes.

Verse 9 names "the isles" and "the ships of Tarshish," large seagoing vessels that bring Zion's sons "from far" with silver and gold, "unto the name of the Lord." The poetry uses ancient transport.

*Caution:* It is better not to read verse 8 as a coded prediction of airplanes. The beauty of the image lies in homecoming birds. Latter-day Saints may rightly see gathering in many forms, but that is application, not the verse's literal claim.

**Scholar's proposal:** Parry observes that the Hebrew addresses Zion with feminine forms, personifying her as a woman. He also traces how gathering and light recur throughout the chapter. He treats the camels, cargo, and ships as pictures of peoples and resources arriving, and he compares the Lord as everlasting light with [Revelation 21:23–25](https://www.churchofjesuschrist.org/study/scriptures/nt/rev/21?lang=eng) and [22:5](https://www.churchofjesuschrist.org/study/scriptures/nt/rev/22?lang=eng) ([Parry, Isaiah 60](https://scripturecentral.org/archive/books/book-chapter/isaiah-60)).

### Isaiah 60:10–12 — Strangers Build and Gates Stay Open

In verse 10 foreigners rebuild Zion's walls and their kings serve her. The theological explanation is key: "in my wrath I smote thee, but in my favour have I had mercy on thee." The reversal flows from mercy, not merit.

Verse 11: "thy gates shall be open continually… day nor night." Ancient gates closed for safety at night. Open gates mean security and constant welcome.

Verse 12 is stern: the nation that "will not serve thee shall perish." In context, this means refusing to join God's purpose for Zion.

*Caution:* Read the verse within the chapter's vision of God's reign. It does not license one people today to subjugate others.

### Isaiah 60:13–14 — Lebanon's Trees and a New Name

Cedar-forest trees, "the fir tree, the pine tree, and the box," come "to beautify the place of my sanctuary." God calls the temple "the place of my feet," his footstool (compare 66:1).

Verse 14 reverses humiliation. The descendants of oppressors come "bending" and call Jerusalem "The city of the Lord, The Zion of the Holy One of Israel." Zion's honor lies in the name of her God.

### Isaiah 60:15–16 — From Hated to Joy

"Whereas thou hast been forsaken and hated, so that no man went through thee" pictures an empty city on a deserted road. That city becomes "an eternal excellency, a joy of many generations."

Verse 16 uses nursing imagery. Zion will "suck the milk of the Gentiles… the breast of kings," and be nourished by the nations. The purpose is knowledge: "thou shalt know that I the Lord am thy Saviour and thy Redeemer, the mighty One of Jacob."

### Isaiah 60:17–18 — Upgraded Materials, Peaceful Officers

Verse 17 upgrades everything: gold for brass, silver for iron, brass for wood, iron for stones. Then it upgrades government: "I will also make thy officers peace, and thine exactors righteousness." The "exactors" are the taskmasters or tribute collectors who once oppressed. Peace and righteousness themselves now govern.

Verse 18: "Violence shall no more be heard." Walls are named "Salvation" and gates "Praise." The city's defenses become theology.

### Isaiah 60:19–20 — The Lord, an Everlasting Light

Sun and moon are no longer needed: "the Lord shall be unto thee an everlasting light, and thy God thy glory." The sun will "no more go down," and the moon will not "withdraw itself," so there is light without interruption. "The days of thy mourning shall be ended." John's vision of the holy city that needs no sun echoes this passage (Revelation 21:23).

### Isaiah 60:21–22 — Branch of My Planting

"Thy people also shall be all righteous." The plant images return: "the branch of my planting, the work of my hands," all "that I may be glorified." Zion is God's garden, and he is the gardener.

Verse 22 multiplies the small: "a little one shall become a thousand." The final line holds tension well: "I the Lord will hasten it in his time." Both haste and God's timing are true. This guide makes no attempt to fix a date.

**Questions for Isaiah 60:**
- How is my light "received" before it is shared?
- What gates in my life or ward could be "open continually"?
- Which part of the chapter's reversal speaks to a forsaken place in me?

## Isaiah 61: Beauty for Ashes

Text: [Isaiah 61 (KJV)](https://www.churchofjesuschrist.org/study/scriptures/ot/isa/61?lang=eng)

### Isaiah 61:1–2 — The Anointed Messenger

A speaker says, "The Spirit of the Lord God is upon me; because the Lord hath anointed me." Anointing set apart kings and priests. Here it sets apart a messenger whose tasks are all verbs of mercy:

- preach good tidings to the meek
- bind up the brokenhearted, as one bandages a wound
- proclaim liberty to captives
- proclaim the opening of the prison
- proclaim "the acceptable year of the Lord" and "the day of vengeance of our God"
- comfort all that mourn

**Official context:** The Church's Scripture Helps connects "the acceptable year" with the Jubilee of [Leviticus 25](https://www.churchofjesuschrist.org/study/scriptures/ot/lev/25?lang=eng), the fiftieth year of release when land returned to its families ([Scripture Helps: Isaiah 58–66](https://www.churchofjesuschrist.org/study/manual/scripture-helps-old-testament/40-isaiah-58-66?lang=eng)).

**Christ connection:** In Nazareth, Jesus read this passage and said, "This day is this scripture fulfilled in your ears" ([Luke 4:16–21](https://www.churchofjesuschrist.org/study/scriptures/nt/luke/4?lang=eng)). Luke records that he stopped reading before the "day of vengeance." Many readers find that pause meaningful: his first coming emphasized the year of favor. The official lesson makes this Luke connection central.

**Course insight:** John Hilton III observes that when Jesus declared Isaiah 61 fulfilled "this day," he presented the Jubilee release of Leviticus 25 as something he offers now, not only once every fifty years ([episode, 17:14](https://www.youtube.com/watch?v=FXwwZ1vAOhY&t=1034)). Read this way, verse 1's liberty becomes a present invitation to turn to Christ. *What release might he be offering me today?*

### Isaiah 61:3 — Exchanges of Mourning for Joy

Verse 3 gives three exchanges to "them that mourn in Zion":

- **Beauty for ashes.** Scripture Helps explains "beauty" as an ornamental headdress replacing the ashes mourners put on their heads.
- **The oil of joy for mourning.** Anointing oil was used for festive grooming, which mourners avoided.
- **The garment of praise for the spirit of heaviness.** Clothing that expresses praise replaces a faint, despairing spirit.

The result is a new name: "trees of righteousness, the planting of the Lord." The phrase echoes 60:21. Ashes on the head become a crown, and grief becomes rooted stability.

*Application:* God does not ask mourners to pretend they feel no grief. He brings a change of clothing, and transformation takes time, like a tree's growth.

### Isaiah 61:4–6 — Rebuilders and Priests

The comforted become builders. They "build the old wastes" and "repair the waste cities, the desolations of many generations." The healing of individuals in verse 1 leads to the restoration of whole communities, which fits the ruined setting described in the introduction.

Verse 5: strangers will tend flocks, plow, and dress vines.

Verse 6 explains the role reversal: "ye shall be named the Priests of the Lord… the Ministers of our God." Israel becomes the "kingdom of priests" of [Exodus 19:6](https://www.churchofjesuschrist.org/study/scriptures/ot/ex/19?lang=eng). Others tend fields so that Israel can serve all.

*Caution:* The point is vocation, not hierarchy. A priest serves on behalf of others. Compare [1 Peter 2:9](https://www.churchofjesuschrist.org/study/scriptures/nt/1-pet/2?lang=eng).

### Isaiah 61:7–9 — Double Portion and Everlasting Covenant

"For your shame ye shall have double." Instead of confusion they will "rejoice in their portion" and possess "the double" in their land. A double portion was the firstborn's inheritance ([Deuteronomy 21:17](https://www.churchofjesuschrist.org/study/scriptures/ot/deut/21?lang=eng)). The shamed are treated as firstborn heirs.

Verse 8 grounds the promise in God's character: "I the Lord love judgment, I hate robbery for burnt offering." God rejects worship funded by injustice, which echoes Isaiah 58. He will "direct their work in truth" and "make an everlasting covenant with them."

Verse 9: their seed will be "known among the Gentiles," recognized as "the seed which the Lord hath blessed."

### Isaiah 61:10–11 — Clothed as Bride and Groom

A joyful first-person voice, perhaps Zion herself or the anointed one, sings, "I will greatly rejoice in the Lord." God has "clothed me with the garments of salvation" and "covered me with the robe of righteousness," like a bridegroom with his ornaments or a bride with her jewels. The person does not dress themselves. God dresses them.

Verse 11 ends with a garden image. As earth brings forth buds, "the Lord God will cause righteousness and praise to spring forth before all the nations." Righteousness grows. It is not manufactured.

**Scholar's proposal:** Parry connects the Anointed One both with Jesus's Nazareth reading in Luke 4 and with the Savior's ministry to the spirits in prison in [Doctrine and Covenants 138:42](https://www.churchofjesuschrist.org/study/scriptures/dc-testament/dc/138?lang=eng). He also suggests temple associations for the robes and headwear in verses 3 and 10 ([Parry, Isaiah 61](https://scripturecentral.org/archive/books/book-chapter/isaiah-61)). Those associations are an interpretive proposal. The text's immediate imagery is mourning, festive dress, and wedding attire.

**Questions for Isaiah 61:**
- What "ashes" in my life has Christ already exchanged? What exchange am I still waiting for?
- How can comforted people become rebuilders?
- Why might Jesus have chosen this passage to begin his ministry?

## Isaiah 62: Hephzibah and Beulah

Text: [Isaiah 62 (KJV)](https://www.churchofjesuschrist.org/study/scriptures/ot/isa/62?lang=eng)

### Isaiah 62:1–3 — I Will Not Rest

The speaker, either the prophet or God, vows persistence: "For Zion's sake will I not hold my peace… I will not rest." The goal is that Zion's righteousness will "go forth as brightness" and her salvation "as a lamp that burneth."

Verse 2: nations and kings will see, and Zion will receive "a new name, which the mouth of the Lord shall name." Naming is God's act of claiming.

Verse 3: Zion becomes "a crown of glory in the hand of the Lord, and a royal diadem in the hand of thy God." The crown is held in God's hand, not worn on his head. It is cherished, displayed, and protected.

### Isaiah 62:4–5 — From Forsaken to Married

The old names were "Forsaken" and "Desolate." The new names are "Hephzi-bah," explained as the Lord delighting in her, and "Beulah," "thy land shall be married."

Verse 5 extends the marriage metaphor. As a young man marries a maiden, "so shall thy sons marry thee," meaning that Zion's people will settle and belong to the land. And "as the bridegroom rejoiceth over the bride, so shall thy God rejoice over thee."

This is one of scripture's most tender pictures. God rejoices over his people as a groom rejoices on his wedding day.

**Scholar's proposal:** Parry glosses Hephzibah as "My Delight is in Her" and Beulah as "Married." He reads the renaming as covenant renewal that gives Zion a new identity. He links the watchmen of verse 6 with prophets and the coming "salvation" of verse 11 with Christ ([Parry, Isaiah 62](https://scripturecentral.org/archive/books/book-chapter/isaiah-62)).

### Isaiah 62:6–7 — Watchmen Who Give God No Rest

"I have set watchmen upon thy walls, O Jerusalem." The sentries are posted on the city wall, and they "never hold their peace day nor night." Their task is to "make mention of the Lord," keeping him in remembrance through prayer, and to "give him no rest, till he establish… Jerusalem a praise in the earth."

The boldness is striking. God invites persistent, even insistent, prayer. Compare the persistent widow in [Luke 18:1–8](https://www.churchofjesuschrist.org/study/scriptures/nt/luke/18?lang=eng).

*Application:* Within the poem, the watchmen are praying sentinels. Latter-day Saints often apply the image to prophets, leaders, and parents. That is a fitting application, and every disciple can also take a turn on the wall in prayer.

### Isaiah 62:8–9 — Sworn by His Right Hand

God swears "by his right hand, and by the arm of his strength." Enemies will no longer eat Zion's grain, and strangers will no longer drink wine "for the which thou hast laboured." This reverses the covenant curses of [Deuteronomy 28:30–33](https://www.churchofjesuschrist.org/study/scriptures/ot/deut/28?lang=eng), where others consumed Israel's harvest.

Verse 9: "they that have gathered it shall eat it, and praise the Lord," drinking "in the courts of my holiness." Harvest becomes temple festival.

### Isaiah 62:10–12 — Prepare the Way

Verse 10 brims with commands: "Go through, go through the gates; prepare ye the way… cast up, cast up the highway; gather out the stones; lift up a standard." It echoes [Isaiah 40:3](https://www.churchofjesuschrist.org/study/scriptures/ot/isa/40?lang=eng) but now prepares a road for people. Obstacles are cleared so others can come.

Verse 11 announces to "the end of the world," "Say ye to the daughter of Zion, Behold, thy salvation cometh; behold, his reward is with him." Salvation arrives as a person, bringing his reward. [Matthew 21:5](https://www.churchofjesuschrist.org/study/scriptures/nt/matt/21?lang=eng) uses the words "tell ye the daughter of Sion" at Christ's triumphal entry, blending this line with [Zechariah 9:9](https://www.churchofjesuschrist.org/study/scriptures/ot/zech/9?lang=eng).

Verse 12 ends with new names: "The holy people, The redeemed of the Lord," and for the city, "Sought out, A city not forsaken."

*Application:* Being "Sought out" is a beautiful identity. We are not merely tolerated by God. He searched for us.

**Questions for Isaiah 62:**
- Which name does God offer me in place of "Forsaken"?
- What stones could I clear from someone else's path?
- How might I pray like a watchman this week?

## Isaiah 63: The Warrior from Edom and the Prayer of Memory

Text: [Isaiah 63 (KJV)](https://www.churchofjesuschrist.org/study/scriptures/ot/isa/63?lang=eng)

### Isaiah 63:1–3 — Who Is This in Red?

A dramatic dialogue begins. An unnamed speaker asks, "Who is this that cometh from Edom, with dyed garments from Bozrah?" Edom was Israel's neighbor and frequent foe, and Bozrah was its city. The figure is "glorious in his apparel… mighty to save," and identifies himself: "I that speak in righteousness."

Verse 2 asks why his garments are red, like those of one who treads the "winefat" (winepress).

Verse 3 answers: "I have trodden the winepress alone." The rest of the verse explains: "I will tread them in mine anger… their blood shall be sprinkled upon my garments."

**What the passage says.** In the immediate text, the stains are the blood of enemies defeated in judgment. Grapes crushed underfoot picture nations brought low. The Church's Scripture Helps reads the passage this way: the warrior defeats enemies, and the blood is theirs ([Scripture Helps](https://www.churchofjesuschrist.org/study/manual/scripture-helps-old-testament/40-isaiah-58-66?lang=eng)). Edom here likely represents hostile powers generally, as it does in [Isaiah 34](https://www.churchofjesuschrist.org/study/scriptures/ot/isa/34?lang=eng).

### Isaiah 63:4–6 — Vengeance and Redemption in One Day

"The day of vengeance is in mine heart, and the year of my redeemed is come." The two lines pair what 61:2 also paired. Judgment on oppressors is the same act as deliverance for the oppressed.

Verse 5 repeats 59:16 almost word for word: "I looked, and there was none to help… therefore mine own arm brought salvation." This again echoes God's intervention without human help in Isaiah 59:16.

Verse 6 describes treading peoples down and making them "drunk in my fury," an image of staggering under judgment.

The scene is severe. It should not be softened into abstraction, nor turned into a warrant for human violence. Vengeance belongs to God alone ([Romans 12:19](https://www.churchofjesuschrist.org/study/scriptures/nt/rom/12?lang=eng)).

**Restoration teaching and devotional connection:**
- [Doctrine and Covenants 133:46–53](https://www.churchofjesuschrist.org/study/scriptures/dc-testament/dc/133?lang=eng) applies this scene to the Savior's Second Coming in red apparel. In that passage, the same Lord who treads the winepress also remembers his people's afflictions and redeems them, so justice and mercy stand together.
- Separately, Latter-day Saints have found a devotional connection to Christ's solitary atoning suffering. Scripture Helps presents Elder Neal A. Maxwell's association of the lonely winepress with the Savior's suffering.
- Christ suffered so intensely that he bled at every pore ([Doctrine and Covenants 19:16–19](https://www.churchofjesuschrist.org/study/scriptures/dc-testament/dc/19?lang=eng); [Mosiah 3:7](https://www.churchofjesuschrist.org/study/scriptures/bofm/mosiah/3?lang=eng)). He also trod alone.

This is a devotional association layered onto the text. It does not replace the passage's plain meaning of judgment. The two readings together say that the Judge of the wicked is the one who bore the cost of mercy.

**Scholar's proposal:** Parry reads Edom and Bozrah as images of hostile, worldly opposition overcome by the divine warrior. He connects the red apparel with the Second Coming through Doctrine and Covenants 133:46–53 ([Parry, Isaiah 63](https://scripturecentral.org/archive/books/book-chapter/isaiah-63)).

### Isaiah 63:7–9 — In All Their Affliction He Was Afflicted

The genre shifts sharply. A worshiper begins: "I will mention the lovingkindnesses of the Lord." The word "lovingkindnesses" appears twice, with "mercies" and "great goodness" heaped beside it.

Verse 8 recalls God's hopeful trust: "Surely they are my people, children that will not lie: so he was their Saviour."

Verse 9 is among the most tender lines in the Old Testament: "In all their affliction he was afflicted, and the angel of his presence saved them." God feels his people's suffering. In love and pity he "redeemed them… bare them, and carried them all the days of old," like a parent carrying a child.

**Christ connection:** Alma teaches that Christ took upon himself our pains, afflictions, and infirmities so he could succor his people ([Alma 7:11–12](https://www.churchofjesuschrist.org/study/scriptures/bofm/alma/7?lang=eng)). Isaiah 63:9 anticipates a God who does not watch suffering from far away.

### Isaiah 63:10–14 — Rebellion and the Memory of the Sea

Verse 10 is honest history: "they rebelled, and vexed his holy Spirit: therefore he was turned to be their enemy." The relationship reversed, but because of the people's rebellion.

Verse 11 begins the turn back: "Then he remembered the days of old, Moses." The subject may be the people remembering. A string of questions follows: "Where is he that brought them up out of the sea… where is he that put his holy Spirit within him?"

Verses 12–14 recall the Exodus ([Exodus 14](https://www.churchofjesuschrist.org/study/scriptures/ot/ex/14?lang=eng)):
- God's "glorious arm" at Moses's right hand
- the dividing of the water
- leading the people through the deep "as an horse in the wilderness," so surefooted "that they should not stumble"
- "As a beast goeth down into the valley, the Spirit of the Lord caused him to rest." Cattle descending to a quiet valley pasture is a picture of rest after crossing.

The double purpose: "to make himself an everlasting name… a glorious name."

*Application:* Remembering what God has done is fuel for asking him to do it again.

### Isaiah 63:15–16 — "Doubtless Thou Art Our Father"

The communal lament begins and continues through chapter 64. "Look down from heaven… where is thy zeal and thy strength, the sounding of thy bowels and of thy mercies?" The phrase "sounding of thy bowels" is ancient language for deep inward compassion. The people ask whether those mercies are "restrained."

Verse 16 is bold faith: "Doubtless thou art our father, though Abraham be ignorant of us, and Israel acknowledge us not." Even if the ancestors would not recognize this ruined generation, God is still "our father, our redeemer; thy name is from everlasting." This is one of the clearest Old Testament addresses to God as Father.

### Isaiah 63:17–19 — An Anguished Question

"O Lord, why hast thou made us to err from thy ways, and hardened our heart?" These are the words of a grieving community, not a theological statement that God forces disobedience.

Lament in scripture speaks raw feeling directly to God, much as the Psalms do. The same prayer confesses "we have sinned" (64:5), and Isaiah repeatedly places responsibility on the people (59:2, 12; 63:10). Restoration scripture affirms moral agency ([2 Nephi 2:27](https://www.churchofjesuschrist.org/study/scriptures/bofm/2-ne/2?lang=eng)).

The plea that follows is the heart of the verse: "Return for thy servants' sake, the tribes of thine inheritance."

Verse 18 grieves that God's holy people possessed their land "but a little while; our adversaries have trodden down thy sanctuary." The verb "trodden" echoes the winepress of verse 3. The people ask God to tread down those who trod on his sanctuary.

Verse 19: "We are thine: thou never barest rule over them; they were not called by thy name." We belong to you, unlike those who never bore your name.

**Questions for Isaiah 63:**
- How do justice and mercy meet in the one red-garmented Redeemer?
- What "days of old" from my own life can I recall in prayer?
- Is it faithful to bring anguished questions to God?

## Isaiah 64: Rend the Heavens

Text: [Isaiah 64 (KJV)](https://www.churchofjesuschrist.org/study/scriptures/ot/isa/64?lang=eng)

### Isaiah 64:1–3 — Oh That Thou Wouldest Come Down

The lament reaches its most daring wish: "Oh that thou wouldest rend the heavens, that thou wouldest come down," tearing the sky like fabric, so that "the mountains might flow down at thy presence."

Verse 2 compares the effect to fire making water boil, so that God's "name [might be] known to thine adversaries."

Verse 3 grounds the wish in memory: "When thou didst terrible things which we looked not for, thou camest down."

**Scholar's proposal:** Parry compares the rending heavens and quaking mountains to God's descent on Sinai ([Exodus 19](https://www.churchofjesuschrist.org/study/scriptures/ot/ex/19?lang=eng)). He reads the clay and potter of verse 8 as a picture of complete dependence on God ([Parry, Isaiah 64](https://scripturecentral.org/archive/books/book-chapter/isaiah-64)). Mark's account of the heavens "opened" at Jesus's baptism may echo this longing ([Mark 1:10](https://www.churchofjesuschrist.org/study/scriptures/nt/mark/1?lang=eng)).

### Isaiah 64:4–5 — What God Has Prepared

"Since the beginning of the world men have not heard… neither hath the eye seen, O God, beside thee, what he hath prepared for him that waiteth for him." No other god acts for those who wait. Paul adapts this line in [1 Corinthians 2:9](https://www.churchofjesuschrist.org/study/scriptures/nt/1-cor/2?lang=eng). Restoration scripture echoes it as well ([Doctrine and Covenants 76:10](https://www.churchofjesuschrist.org/study/scriptures/dc-testament/dc/76?lang=eng)).

Verse 5 says God "meetest him that rejoiceth and worketh righteousness, those that remember thee." Then comes confession: "thou art wroth; for we have sinned." The final clause, "in those is continuance, and we shall be saved," is difficult. It may mean that salvation endures in God's ways, or it may be a wistful question. Either way, hope survives inside the confession.

### Isaiah 64:6–7 — Filthy Rags and a Fading Leaf

"We are all as an unclean thing, and all our righteousnesses are as filthy rags." The language comes from the vocabulary of ritual impurity. The community confesses that even its acts of righteousness have been stained by the corruption described in Isaiah 58–59.

*Caution:* This is collective confession in a time of crisis. It does not teach that sincere goodness is worthless to God. Isaiah 58 itself praises true fasting, and verse 5 here says God meets those who work righteousness. It also does not teach that illness, impurity, or disability prove personal sin. The image is about the community's need for cleansing.

"We all do fade as a leaf; and our iniquities, like the wind, have taken us away." A withered leaf is blown off by the wind.

Verse 7: "none… calleth upon thy name… stirreth up himself to take hold of thee." Spiritual inertia is confessed, along with a felt divine hiddenness: "thou hast hid thy face from us."

### Isaiah 64:8–9 — The Clay and the Potter

"But now, O Lord, thou art our father." This renews the address to God as Father (compare 63:16). "We are the clay, and thou our potter; and we all are the work of thy hand." The image does not express fatalism. It is an appeal. A potter does not abandon what he has formed, and clay can be reworked ([Jeremiah 18:1–6](https://www.churchofjesuschrist.org/study/scriptures/ot/jer/18?lang=eng)).

Verse 9: "Be not wroth very sore… neither remember iniquity for ever: behold, see, we beseech thee, we are all thy people." The repeated "look, please" carries the urgency.

*Application:* When we feel the least worthy, the most powerful prayer may be to remind God whose we are.

### Isaiah 64:10–12 — The Burned House

The prayer names the devastation. "Thy holy cities are a wilderness, Zion is a wilderness, Jerusalem a desolation." Verse 11 says, "Our holy and our beautiful house, where our fathers praised thee, is burned up with fire." These lines evoke Jerusalem's ruin and the burning of the temple ([2 Kings 25:9](https://www.churchofjesuschrist.org/study/scriptures/ot/2-kgs/25?lang=eng)). The phrase "where our fathers praised thee" carries generations of memory.

Verse 12 ends with two questions, not a resolution: "Wilt thou refrain thyself… wilt thou hold thy peace?" Chapter 65 is God's answer. He will not stay silent (65:6). The prayer is heard.

**Questions for Isaiah 64:**
- How can I confess honestly without despising the good God has helped me do?
- What does it mean to me to be clay in the Potter's hands?
- How can a community pray together through loss?

## Isaiah 65: New Heavens and a New Earth

Text: [Isaiah 65 (KJV)](https://www.churchofjesuschrist.org/study/scriptures/ot/isa/65?lang=eng)

### Isaiah 65:1–2 — Behold Me, Behold Me

God answers the lament with a surprising claim: "I am sought of them that asked not for me… I said, Behold me, behold me, unto a nation that was not called by my name."

In context, God was available all along, even before he was sought. Verse 2: "I have spread out my hands all the day unto a rebellious people." The posture is open arms, an embrace that has been refused. Those people walk "after their own thoughts."

Paul applies verse 1 to Gentiles who found God and verse 2 to Israel's resistance ([Romans 10:20–21](https://www.churchofjesuschrist.org/study/scriptures/nt/rom/10?lang=eng)). That application does not cancel God's continuing love for Israel. Romans 11 insists on it.

**Christ connection:** Christ's outspread arms invite all to come ([3 Nephi 9:14](https://www.churchofjesuschrist.org/study/scriptures/bofm/3-ne/9?lang=eng)).

### Isaiah 65:3–5 — Holier Than Thou

The rebellious people's practices are listed:
- sacrificing in gardens
- burning incense on brick altars, outside the commanded altar
- staying "among the graves" and lodging "in the monuments," probably seeking contact with the dead
- eating swine's flesh, with "broth of abominable things"

Verse 5 exposes the worst irony. They say, "Stand by thyself, come not near to me; for I am holier than thou." People practicing forbidden rites claim superior holiness. To God such people are "a smoke in my nose, a fire that burneth all the day," a constant irritant.

*Application:* The phrase "holier than thou" entered our language from this verse. It warns every religious person against isolating pride.

### Isaiah 65:6–7 — Written Before Me

"It is written before me: I will not keep silence." This answers 64:12 directly. God records and repays "into their bosom," that is, into the fold of a garment where measured goods were poured.

Verse 7 includes "the iniquities of your fathers together" with their own, describing incense on mountains and blasphemy on hills. The present generation continues the ancestors' pattern. The point is not that children are punished for parents' choices. Compare [Ezekiel 18:20](https://www.churchofjesuschrist.org/study/scriptures/ot/ezek/18?lang=eng).

**Course insight:** Hilton asks how a "day of vengeance" could comfort mourners (61:2). Drawing on a theologian shaped by war in the former Yugoslavia, he suggests that trust in God's final justice frees victims from both despair and personal retaliation. Compare 65:6 with Romans 12:19 ([episode, 26:01](https://www.youtube.com/watch?v=FXwwZ1vAOhY&t=1561)). *What wrong could I leave in God's hands?*

### Isaiah 65:8–10 — A Blessing in the Cluster

"As the new wine is found in the cluster, and one saith, Destroy it not; for a blessing is in it." A grape-gatherer finds good juice in a mostly poor cluster and spares it. "So will I do for my servants' sakes, that I may not destroy them all."

Verse 9: God will bring "a seed out of Jacob" and "an inheritor of my mountains."

Verse 10 names two places:
- **Sharon**, the fertile western plain, becomes a fold for flocks.
- **The valley of Achor**, remembered for Achan's sin and judgment ([Joshua 7:24–26](https://www.churchofjesuschrist.org/study/scriptures/ot/josh/7?lang=eng)), becomes a resting place for herds. Compare Hosea's "door of hope" ([Hosea 2:15](https://www.churchofjesuschrist.org/study/scriptures/ot/hosea/2?lang=eng)).

These blessings are "for my people that have sought me." The answer to the lament's "we are all thy people" is that God will preserve those who seek him.

**Scholar's proposal:** Parry explains Gad and Meni in verse 11 as the gods of Fortune and Destiny, treating the meal and drink offerings as idolatrous worship. He reads the new wine in the cluster as the preservation of a faithful remnant. He also traces the chapter's sustained contrast between God's servants and those who reject him ([Parry, Isaiah 65](https://scripturecentral.org/archive/books/book-chapter/isaiah-65)).

### Isaiah 65:11–12 — A Table for Fortune

The KJV's "that troop" and "that number" render divine names that Parry identifies as Fortune and Destiny. People who "forget my holy mountain" spread tables and pour drinks for luck gods. The wordplay continues: those who serve "number" will be numbered "to the sword."

The reason is stated plainly: "when I called, ye did not answer; when I spake, ye did not hear… did choose that wherein I delighted not." The verbs of choice emphasize their agency.

*Application:* Trusting luck or fate instead of God remains a temptation today.

### Isaiah 65:13–16 — Two Destinies and a New Name

Verses 13–14 form a series of contrasts introduced by "Behold":

- My servants eat, but you hunger.
- My servants drink, but you thirst.
- My servants rejoice, but you are ashamed.
- My servants sing "for joy of heart," but you cry "for sorrow of heart."
- You "howl for vexation of spirit."

Verse 15: the rejecters leave their name "for a curse," while God calls "his servants by another name."

Verse 16 reveals what that new order is like. People bless and swear by "the God of truth," who is faithful and reliable. Why? "Because the former troubles are forgotten… hid from mine eyes." That clause leads directly into verse 17.

### Isaiah 65:17–19 — Creating Joy

"For, behold, I create new heavens and a new earth." The verb "create" echoes Genesis 1. "The former shall not be remembered, nor come into mind." Old griefs no longer dominate the mind.

Verse 18: "be ye glad and rejoice for ever in that which I create: for, behold, I create Jerusalem a rejoicing, and her people a joy." God creates joy as well as matter.

Verse 19: God himself rejoices, and "the voice of weeping shall be no more heard in her." Revelation picks up this promise ([Revelation 21:4](https://www.churchofjesuschrist.org/study/scriptures/nt/rev/21?lang=eng)).

**Official context:** Scripture Helps reads Isaiah 65:17–25 in relation to the Millennium and its peace.

### Isaiah 65:20 — Long Life and the Hundred-Year Child

This verse needs care. "There shall be no more thence an infant of days, nor an old man that hath not filled his days." Infant death and cut-short lives end. "The child shall die an hundred years old," which means that dying at a hundred would seem like dying young. "The sinner being an hundred years old shall be accursed."

**What the text says.** The verse still speaks of death, of age, and of sinners. It describes extraordinary longevity and security, not explicitly an existence without death. It should not be pressed into a universal fixed lifespan of exactly one hundred.

**Restoration teaching.** [Doctrine and Covenants 101:26–31](https://www.churchofjesuschrist.org/study/scriptures/dc-testament/dc/101?lang=eng) teaches that in the Millennium children will grow up until they become old, and that a person will be changed "in the twinkling of an eye" rather than sleeping in the dust. Latter-day Saints read Isaiah 65:20 in harmony with this. That is a Restoration interpretation added to Isaiah's poetic language about long life. It is not the verse's explicit wording.

**Final creation.** Revelation 21:1–4 describes the final new creation where "there shall be no more death."

Holding these together without forcing a rigid timetable honors each text.

### Isaiah 65:21–23 — Houses, Vineyards, Children

"They shall build houses, and inhabit them… plant vineyards, and eat the fruit." These ordinary joys reverse the covenant curses in which others took one's house, vineyard, and harvest (Deuteronomy 28:30–33).

Verse 22: "as the days of a tree are the days of my people." Trees can outlive generations. The elect "shall long enjoy the work of their hands."

Verse 23: they will not labor in vain "nor bring forth for trouble," meaning children will not be born into calamity. They are "the seed of the blessed of the Lord, and their offspring with them."

*Application:* Salvation includes meaningful, secure work and families who flourish. These are holy things.

### Isaiah 65:24–25 — Before They Call

"Before they call, I will answer; and while they are yet speaking, I will hear." This is a complete reversal of 59:2 and 64:7, where God's face was hidden and no one called. The relationship is now so close that answers anticipate requests.

Verse 25 echoes [Isaiah 11:6–9](https://www.churchofjesuschrist.org/study/scriptures/ot/isa/11?lang=eng). "The wolf and the lamb shall feed together, and the lion shall eat straw like the bullock." Predator and prey are at peace.

There is one deliberate exception: "dust shall be the serpent's meat." This recalls the curse of [Genesis 3:14](https://www.churchofjesuschrist.org/study/scriptures/ot/gen/3?lang=eng). Even in Eden restored, the serpent remains humbled. Evil does not regain power.

"They shall not hurt nor destroy in all my holy mountain."

**Questions for Isaiah 65:**
- Where do I see God's "spread out" hands?
- Which "former troubles" do I hope God will help me forget?
- What do verses 21–23 teach about the worth of ordinary labor and family life?

## Isaiah 66: All Flesh Shall Come to Worship

Text: [Isaiah 66 (KJV)](https://www.churchofjesuschrist.org/study/scriptures/ot/isa/66?lang=eng)

### Isaiah 66:1–2 — To This Man Will I Look

"The heaven is my throne, and the earth is my footstool: where is the house that ye build unto me?" God cannot be contained by any structure. Solomon said the same at the temple's dedication ([1 Kings 8:27](https://www.churchofjesuschrist.org/study/scriptures/ot/1-kgs/8?lang=eng)). Stephen quoted these words before his death ([Acts 7:48–50](https://www.churchofjesuschrist.org/study/scriptures/nt/acts/7?lang=eng)).

Verse 2 gives God's preferred dwelling: "to this man will I look, even to him that is poor and of a contrite spirit, and trembleth at my word."

The point is not that temples are unimportant. Isaiah calls the temple "the house of my glory" (60:7) and envisions worship at "my holy mountain" (66:20). The point is that buildings cannot substitute for humble hearts. Compare the broken heart and contrite spirit of [3 Nephi 9:20](https://www.churchofjesuschrist.org/study/scriptures/bofm/3-ne/9?lang=eng).

### Isaiah 66:3–4 — Sacrifice Without Humility

Verse 3 pairs legitimate offerings with abhorrent acts:
- killing an ox is "as if he slew a man"
- sacrificing a lamb is like breaking "a dog's neck"
- an oblation is like "swine's blood"
- incense is like blessing "an idol"

When worship comes from people who "have chosen their own ways" and delight in abominations, God sees their ritual as no better than sacrilege. The point is God rejecting hypocritical ritual, not abolishing temple worship.

Verse 4 mirrors their choices: "I also will choose their delusions… because when I called, none did answer." These words repeat 65:12. Their chosen path brings the fears they hoped to avoid.

### Isaiah 66:5–6 — Ye That Tremble at His Word

The audience shifts to those who "tremble at his word" (verse 2). Their own "brethren… hated you, [and] cast you out for my name's sake," while mockingly saying, "Let the Lord be glorified." God promises: "he shall appear to your joy, and they shall be ashamed."

Verse 6: "A voice of noise from the city, a voice from the temple." The Lord speaks in recompense from the very sanctuary that was misused.

*Application:* Those who are excluded for faithfulness are seen by God. Jesus pronounced a blessing on those reviled for his sake ([Matthew 5:10–12](https://www.churchofjesuschrist.org/study/scriptures/nt/matt/5?lang=eng)).

### Isaiah 66:7–9 — A Nation Born in a Day

Zion, the mother, gives birth "before she travailed… before her pain came." The birth is miraculously fast.

Verse 8 marvels: "Shall… a nation be born at once? for as soon as Zion travailed, she brought forth her children." A city that mourned its absent children (49:21; 54:1) now delivers a people suddenly.

Verse 9 is God's guarantee: "Shall I bring to the birth, and not cause to bring forth?… shall I… shut the womb?" God does not start a work and abandon it halfway.

*Application:* Many readers apply this to God's promises in their own lives. What he begins, he finishes ([Philippians 1:6](https://www.churchofjesuschrist.org/study/scriptures/nt/philip/1?lang=eng)).

### Isaiah 66:10–13 — Comforted as by a Mother

"Rejoice ye with Jerusalem… all ye that mourn for her." Mourners for Zion become rejoicers with her (compare 61:3).

Verse 11 invites them to "suck, and be satisfied with the breasts of her consolations" and be "delighted with the abundance of her glory."

Verse 12: God extends "peace to her like a river," with the nations' glory "like a flowing stream." Children are "borne upon her sides, and dandled upon her knees."

Verse 13 reaches the climax: "As one whom his mother comforteth, so will I comfort you." God compares himself to a mother, joining the maternal images of [Isaiah 49:15](https://www.churchofjesuschrist.org/study/scriptures/ot/isa/49?lang=eng). Compare also Christ's longing to gather Jerusalem as a hen gathers chicks ([Matthew 23:37](https://www.churchofjesuschrist.org/study/scriptures/nt/matt/23?lang=eng)).

**Scholar's proposal:** Parry notes the chapter's interwoven themes: temple and humility, idolatry, Lady Zion as mother, the gathering of all nations, and final renewal ([Parry, Isaiah 66](https://scripturecentral.org/archive/books/book-chapter/isaiah-66)).

### Isaiah 66:14–17 — Fire, Sword, and Garden Rites

Verse 14: "your bones shall flourish like an herb," like fresh grass, while the Lord's hand is "known toward his servants, and his indignation toward his enemies."

Verses 15–16 describe theophany, the appearance of God: the Lord comes "with fire," his chariots "like a whirlwind," to "plead with all flesh" by fire and sword. "The slain of the Lord shall be many." The language is the stark vocabulary of divine judgment. Restoration scripture links fire with the Second Coming ([Doctrine and Covenants 133:41](https://www.churchofjesuschrist.org/study/scriptures/dc-testament/dc/133?lang=eng)).

Verse 17 names the targets. They "sanctify themselves… in the gardens behind one tree in the midst," and eat swine, "abomination, and the mouse." This connects back to 65:3–4. Self-made holiness ends.

### Isaiah 66:18–21 — Gathering All Nations

"I know their works and their thoughts." Then comes the widest vision in the book: "I will gather all nations and tongues; and they shall come, and see my glory."

Verse 19: God sets "a sign among them" and sends survivors to Tarshish, Pul, Lud ("that draw the bow"), Tubal, Javan, and "the isles afar off" that "have not heard my fame." These are the farthest places known to the ancient world. The rescued become messengers who "declare my glory among the Gentiles."

Verse 20: they bring "all your brethren… out of all nations" on horses, chariots, litters, mules, and swift beasts. The gathered people are themselves "an offering unto the Lord… in a clean vessel," like grain carried to the temple.

Verse 21: "I will also take of them for priests and for Levites." The word "them" may refer to the gathered nations or to the returning brethren. Interpreters differ, and the verse does not specify exact arrangements. What is clear is a remarkable widening of who may serve before God.

*Latter-day application:* Latter-day Saints see in this gathering a pattern for missionary work and temple service worldwide. That is an application of the passage, not its only meaning.

### Isaiah 66:22–24 — Remaining Before Him, and a Sober Ending

Verse 22: as "the new heavens and the new earth… shall remain before me… so shall your seed and your name remain." God's covenant people last as long as creation.

Verse 23: "from one new moon to another, and from one sabbath to another, shall all flesh come to worship before me." The Sabbath of chapter 58 returns, now universal. The book's long movement from hypocritical worship to worship by "all flesh" is complete.

Verse 24 is the final line of Isaiah. Worshipers "go forth, and look upon the carcases of the men that have transgressed against me: for their worm shall not die, neither shall their fire be quenched; and they shall be an abhorring unto all flesh." The picture is of corpses outside the city, consumed by worms and fire. It is a grim memorial of rebellion's end.

Jesus used this language to warn of Gehenna ([Mark 9:43–48](https://www.churchofjesuschrist.org/study/scriptures/nt/mark/9?lang=eng)). [Doctrine and Covenants 19:6–12](https://www.churchofjesuschrist.org/study/scriptures/dc-testament/dc/19?lang=eng) explains that phrases like "endless torment" name God's punishment, because Endless is his name, rather than describing punishment without end for every soul.

*Caution:* This should not be read as a technical map of the fate of every person outside the Church. Its severity should not be erased either. Isaiah ends by insisting that rejecting God is real and has consequences.

**Questions for Isaiah 66:**
- Do I "tremble at his word," and what does that look like day to day?
- How does God's mothering comfort change my image of him?
- How should I feel about a book that ends with both universal worship and solemn warning?

## Study Plan and Teaching Discussion

### A seven-day personal plan

- **Day 1: Isaiah 58.** Read verses 3–7 aloud. Plan a fast with a specific purpose. Consider whether a fast offering could be more generous. If health does not permit fasting, choose another act of burden-lifting.
- **Day 2: Isaiah 59.** Mark every body part named in verses 3–8. Then read verses 16–21 and write what "intercessor" means to you.
- **Day 3: Isaiah 60.** Underline every reference to light. Ask where you have received light that you could now reflect.
- **Day 4: Isaiah 61–62.** Read Luke 4:16–21 next to Isaiah 61:1–3. List the three exchanges and the new names, and choose one that speaks to you.
- **Day 5: Isaiah 63.** Read verses 1–6 first as a judgment scene. Then read Doctrine and Covenants 19:16–19 and verses 7–9. Reflect on how the same Lord brings justice and suffers with his people.
- **Day 6: Isaiah 64.** Write your own short lament in the pattern of 63:15–64:12: remembrance, honest confession, "Thou art our father," and a plea.
- **Day 7: Isaiah 65–66.** Read God's answer. Compare 65:24 with 64:7, and 66:23 with 58:13. Notice how the book closes the circle.

### Teaching ideas for home or class

- **Two kinds of fast.** Divide the board into "the fast they chose" (58:3–5) and "the fast God chose" (58:6–10), and let learners fill in each column from the text.
- **Name changes.** List the old and new names in 60:14, 18; 61:3, 6; and 62:2, 4, 12. Ask: what is God saying about identity?
- **Justice and mercy.** Teach Isaiah 63 in two steps. Start with the plain text of judgment, then the devotional connection to the Atonement. Ask how both comfort the oppressed.
- **Lament as faith.** Ask whether it is acceptable to say to God what 63:17 or 64:12 says. Discuss how confession and protest coexist in faithful prayer.
- **Ordinary blessings.** Use 65:21–23 to discuss homes, work, and children as places of salvation. Be sensitive to learners who experience childlessness or insecure housing. Isaiah's promise is God's future, not a measure of anyone's present righteousness.

### Discussion questions spanning the chapters

1. How do Isaiah 58's fast and Isaiah 66's universal Sabbath frame the whole section?
2. The divine "arm" appears in 59:16, 62:8, and 63:5, 12. What does it mean that God's own arm saves?
3. How does Jesus's reading of Isaiah 61 shape the way we read the rest of these chapters?
4. Where in these chapters do you see God answering a specific complaint or prayer from earlier in the reading?
