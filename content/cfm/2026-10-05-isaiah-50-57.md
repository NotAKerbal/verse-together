# He Hath Borne Our Griefs, and Carried Our Sorrows

## Come, Follow Me study guide: Isaiah 50–57, October 5–11, 2026

This guide covers all 124 verses of Isaiah 50–57 in the King James Version. Every chapter is discussed in order, and every verse appears under a numbered subheading. The official lesson for the week is [Come, Follow Me—For Home and Church: Old Testament 2026, "Isaiah 50–57"](https://www.churchofjesuschrist.org/study/manual/come-follow-me-for-home-and-church-old-testament-2026/41?lang=eng). Its emphases are covenant hope, the Savior's suffering, the endurance of God's mercy, and seeking the Lord. The companion resource, [Old Testament Scripture Helps, "Isaiah 50–57"](https://www.churchofjesuschrist.org/study/manual/scripture-helps-old-testament/39-isaiah-50-57?lang=eng), supplies short explanations of several images discussed below.

Most of the interpretation here comes from close reading of the public-domain KJV. Modern commentators are cited by name, with links, where their proposals are used. The guide tries to keep five things distinct:

- what the text likely meant in its ancient setting;
- how later scripture explicitly reads it;
- traditional Christian and Latter-day Saint readings;
- proposals by individual scholars;
- devotional applications offered here.

## Historical and literary orientation

### The world these chapters imagine

Isaiah 50–57 speaks into a world of exile and return. Jerusalem fell to Babylon in the early sixth century before Christ. The temple was burned, the Davidic king was deported, and many of Judah's leading families were resettled in Mesopotamia. Isaiah 40–55 repeatedly addresses this displaced people. It names Cyrus of Persia (44:28; 45:1), whose conquest of Babylon in 539 BC opened the way home. It calls the exiles to leave Babylon (48:20; 52:11). It addresses Jerusalem as a ruined, bereaved mother waiting for her children (49:14–21; 51:17–20; 54:1).

Scholars and believing readers disagree about how this literary setting relates to authorship. Many modern scholars attribute much of chapters 40–55 to a prophet writing during the exile, and much of chapters 56–66 to the period after the return. Many Latter-day Saints affirm the eighth-century Isaiah as author. One reason is that Book of Mormon writers before Christ quote substantial portions of Isaiah 48–53, though not every verse: Isaiah 48–49 in 1 Nephi 20–21, Isaiah 50–51 and 52:1–2 in 2 Nephi 7–8, Isaiah 52:7–10 in Mosiah 12 and 15, and Isaiah 53 in Mosiah 14. The Book of Mormon presents its Isaiah material as words its writers had from the brass plates, which Lehi's family carried from Jerusalem around 600 BC. Isaiah 54 is quoted only later, by the resurrected Jesus in 3 Nephi 22. That quotation does not by itself show that Isaiah 54 was on the brass plates. This guide does not need to settle that debate. Either way, the text deliberately speaks to exiles and returnees. Its images come from that experience: debt slavery, divorce, prison, ruined walls, a long road home, temple vessels carried back, and a community arguing about who belongs.

### The movement from chapter 50 to chapter 57

These eight chapters move in recognizable stages.

Chapters 50–52 answer a complaint. Zion has said, "The LORD hath forsaken me" (49:14). Chapter 50 asks who actually ended the relationship. Chapter 51 calls the people to remember Abraham, Sarah, Eden, and the Exodus. Chapter 52 wakes Jerusalem from the dust and announces a messenger of peace.

Then comes the long poem that begins at 52:13 and runs through 53:12. The chapter break inherited from medieval Bible divisions obscures this. The poem should be read as one unit across the chapter boundary. The Hebrew does not label its speakers, so any division into voices is a reconstruction. A common and plausible reading hears three voices. God appears to speak first (52:13–15). A group calling itself "we" then seems to confess how badly it misjudged the servant (roughly 53:1–10a). Who the "we" are is debated. God's voice then seems to return to vindicate the servant through 53:12. Some readers place that return at 53:10b, and others at 53:11 ("my righteous servant").

Chapter 54 turns to the city as a once-barren mother whose tent must now expand. Chapter 55 is an invitation to a free feast and an everlasting covenant.

With chapter 56 the tone changes. The audience now seems to be a community back in the land, wrestling with Sabbath, temple, and membership. Foreigners and eunuchs are promised a place, while careless leaders are condemned. Chapter 57 laments righteous people who die unnoticed, attacks idolatry and child sacrifice in sharp metaphor, and closes with one of scripture's most tender descriptions of God dwelling with the contrite. The refrain "There is no peace ... unto the wicked" closes both 48:22 and 57:21, which marks a section boundary.

John Hilton III's slides for [Finding Christ in the Old Testament, Class 40, "The Founder of Our Peace"](https://johnhiltoniii.com/findingchrist/class-40-the-founder-of-our-peace/) organize these chapters around four kinds of hope: hope in exile (chapters 50–52), in the courtroom (50 and 53), in the present (54), and in the face of exclusion (56).

### The servant problem

The word "servant" carries many meanings in Isaiah. Israel or Jacob is explicitly called God's servant (41:8–9; 44:1–2; 49:3). Yet in 49:5–6 the servant has a mission *to* Israel, to restore Jacob and be a light to the Gentiles. The servant is therefore sometimes the nation, sometimes a figure acting on the nation's behalf, and sometimes ambiguous.

Since the late nineteenth century, scholars have grouped four passages as "servant songs": 42:1–9; 49:1–6; 50:4–9 (sometimes extended to 50:11); and 52:13–53:12.

Jewish interpreters have long read the suffering servant collectively, as Israel suffering among the nations, or as the faithful remnant. Some Jewish and Christian readers have also seen a prophet, possibly the author, in the poem. These are serious contextual readings. They are not evasions.

The New Testament explicitly applies Isaiah 53 to Jesus in several places (Matthew 8:17; Luke 22:37; John 12:38; Acts 8:32–35; 1 Peter 2:22–25). In the Book of Mormon, Abinadi quotes the whole chapter and identifies its subject as Christ (Mosiah 14–15). Christian readers therefore see Jesus as the fullest fulfillment of the servant's pattern. Latter-day Saints add further layers from 3 Nephi 20–21, discussed at 52:13–15.

The honest approach holds both truths together. The poem grows out of Israel's experience. It also points beyond any single generation to a figure whose innocent suffering heals others. Christian readers find that figure in Jesus.

## Isaiah 50

### Chapter orientation

Chapter 50 begins in a courtroom and ends at a campfire. It has three parts. In verses 1–3, God answers the charge of abandonment. In verses 4–9, an individual speaker, never named here as "servant" until verse 10, describes how he learned to listen, how he was abused, and why he expects vindication. In verses 10–11, God offers two paths: trust in darkness, or reliance on a fire one lights for oneself.

The chapter follows 49:14–26, where Zion complained of being forgotten and God replied with the image of a nursing mother and hands engraved with Zion's walls. Chapter 50 continues the argument in legal terms. The Book of Mormon reproduces this chapter in 2 Nephi 7. There Jacob reads it to his people as part of a sermon on the covenant with Israel.

### Isaiah 50:1–3 — Who ended the marriage, and who sold the children?

> "Where is the bill of your mother's divorcement, whom I have put away? or which of my creditors is it to whom I have sold you?" (50:1)

**Verse 1** uses two legal images from ordinary Israelite life. The first is divorce. Deuteronomy 24:1–4 assumes a man who sends away his wife gives her a written certificate. Without that document, her status is unresolved. The second is debt slavery. A father under crushing debt could sell children into servitude (Exodus 21:7; 2 Kings 4:1; Nehemiah 5:5).

God asks the exiles to produce the paperwork. If he had formally divorced their "mother" (the city or nation), where is the certificate? If he had sold them to pay a debt, who is the creditor? The rhetorical answer is that neither document exists. God owes no one. He has not issued a final decree. The companion Scripture Helps explains both images along these lines ([Scripture Helps](https://www.churchofjesuschrist.org/study/manual/scripture-helps-old-testament/39-isaiah-50-57?lang=eng)).

The second half of the verse names the real cause: "for your iniquities have ye sold yourselves, and for your transgressions is your mother put away." The exile is real and has a moral cause in the community's covenant unfaithfulness. But it is not a permanent legal severance initiated by God.

Two cautions belong here. First, the mother and children are a corporate image of Jerusalem and her people. The verse is not commentary on any individual divorce. It does not suggest that divorced people, or children of divorce, carry a particular guilt. Second, Jeremiah 3:8 uses the divorce image differently, saying God gave faithless Israel a bill of divorce. The prophets used the metaphor flexibly for different rhetorical purposes. Isaiah 50 uses it to deny finality.

The Book of Mormon text in 2 Nephi 7:1 adds wording to the opening, framing the question as whether the Lord has put Israel away or cast them off forever. That variant should be read as the Book of Mormon text, not imported silently into the KJV. Still, it sharpens the same point: the separation is not forever.

**Verse 2** moves from law to history. "Wherefore, when I came, was there no man? when I called, was there none to answer?" The problem was not God's absence. God came and called, and nobody answered.

Then come two questions about power: "Is my hand shortened at all, that it cannot redeem? or have I no power to deliver?" A "shortened hand" is a Hebrew idiom for weakness. Numbers 11:23 uses it when Moses doubts God can feed the people in the wilderness. The answer is a cascade of Exodus imagery. At God's rebuke the sea dries up, rivers become wilderness, and fish die and stink. This recalls the Red Sea and the first plague, when the Nile's fish died and the river stank (Exodus 7:18, 21).

**Verse 3** adds darkness: "I clothe the heavens with blackness, and I make sackcloth their covering." This recalls the plague of darkness (Exodus 10:21–23). Sackcloth was mourning clothing, so the sky itself is dressed as if in grief. The God who can make the cosmos mourn is not too weak to bring exiles home.

These three verses frame the rest of the chapter. Delay in redemption does not come from God's inability or indifference. Something is wrong on the human side of the relationship. Yet the relationship is not over.

### Isaiah 50:4–5 — A teacher who first learned to listen

> "The Lord GOD hath given me the tongue of the learned, that I should know how to speak a word in season to him that is weary: he wakeneth morning by morning, he wakeneth mine ear to hear as the learned." (50:4)

The speaker changes abruptly to "me." This first-person speaker is the subject of the third servant song.

**Verse 4** is built around a pairing: the "tongue" and the "ear" of "the learned." The Hebrew word behind "learned" means a disciple or one who has been taught. The same word appears in 8:16 ("my disciples"). The speaker can speak well because he has first been taught. The purpose of his speech is not display but help: "to speak a word in season to him that is weary." The weary person needs a timely word, and the speaker's skill exists for that person.

The source of the skill is daily: "he wakeneth morning by morning, he wakeneth mine ear." The repetition suggests a rhythm of being roused each day to listen. The servant does not receive a single commission and then operate alone. He is taught daily.

**Verse 5** completes the thought. "The Lord GOD hath opened mine ear, and I was not rebellious, neither turned away back." The servant's openness is set against the people of verse 2, who did not answer when called. The servant answers. This contrast between the unresponsive people and the responsive servant is one reason interpreters see the servant as more than a simple personification of Israel. He does what Israel failed to do.

Christian readers have noticed how closely this resembles the Gospels' portrait of Jesus. Mark 1:35 shows him rising "a great while before day" to pray. In John 5:30 and 8:28 he says he does nothing of himself but speaks what the Father has taught him. The Gospels do not cite Isaiah 50:4 explicitly. The resemblance is thematic, not a direct quotation, and it is worth seeing as such.

### Isaiah 50:6 — Back, cheek, and face

> "I gave my back to the smiters, and my cheeks to them that plucked off the hair: I hid not my face from shame and spitting."

This verse describes abuse in three parts, and in each part the speaker's own action comes first. "I gave," "I hid not." The servant's back is struck. His beard is torn, the "hair" of the cheeks, which in Israelite culture was an act of extreme humiliation (compare 2 Samuel 10:4–5, where shaving half of the envoys' beards is a grave insult). His face receives spit.

The grammar matters. The servant is not described as passive in the sense of helpless. He chose not to avoid the consequences of his obedience in verse 5. The suffering follows from faithfulness, not from guilt.

Matthew 26:67 records that Jesus was spat upon and struck during his trial before the council. The parallel is close. Matthew 27:26–31 adds scourging and further mockery by soldiers. One honest limit: no Gospel describes Jesus's beard being plucked. The correspondence is strong but not total. The Gospels do not quote this verse as a formal fulfillment text. The connection rests on the pattern and on Christianity's wider reading of the servant songs as a whole.

One more caution. The servant "gave" his back. That does not mean anyone who is abused is being called to offer their back. The verse describes a specific act of costly faithfulness in a prophetic mission. It is not a rule for people facing violence at home or elsewhere. Seeking safety does not contradict this text.

### Isaiah 50:7–9 — A face like flint and a God who stands near in court

> "For the Lord GOD will help me; therefore shall I not be confounded: therefore have I set my face like a flint, and I know that I shall not be ashamed." (50:7)

**Verse 7** turns from what was done to the servant to what he expects. "The Lord GOD will help me." The verb is future, though often read with present force. Because of that expected help, he will not be "confounded," that is, disgraced. The face that was spat upon in verse 6 is now "set ... like a flint." Flint is an exceptionally hard stone, and the prophet uses it as an image of a face fixed in unyielding resolve. The image is not of a man who feels nothing. It is of a man whose direction cannot be shifted.

Luke 9:51 uses similar language: Jesus "stedfastly set his face to go to Jerusalem." Luke does not cite Isaiah 50, but the shared image of a determined face is a meaningful thematic link.

**Verse 8** introduces the courtroom. "He is near that justifieth me; who will contend with me? let us stand together: who is mine adversary? let him come near to me." "Justify" here is legal: to declare someone in the right. The judge who will acquit the servant is "near," and in that confidence the servant invites any accuser to bring charges. "Let us stand together" means let us stand up together in court.

**Verse 9** completes the legal sequence. "Behold, the Lord GOD will help me; who is he that shall condemn me? lo, they all shall wax old as a garment; the moth shall eat them up." The accusers do not last. Like a garment eaten by moths, their power decays. The same moth image returns in 51:8.

Romans 8:33–34 closely resembles these verses: "Who shall lay any thing to the charge of God's elect? It is God that justifieth. Who is he that condemneth?" Paul applies the servant's courtroom confidence to everyone in Christ. Because Christ died, rose, and intercedes, no accusation can finally stand against those who belong to him.

Paul's use stays close to Isaiah's logic. He does not deny that accusers exist. He denies that their charges can finally stand when God is the one who justifies.

A further caution: this passage does not promise that faithful people avoid accusation or suffering. The servant has already been beaten in verse 6. Vindication follows suffering; it does not prevent it.

### Isaiah 50:10 — The faithful person who walks in darkness

> "Who is among you that feareth the LORD, that obeyeth the voice of his servant, that walketh in darkness, and hath no light? let him trust in the name of the LORD, and stay upon his God."

This may be one of the most pastorally important verses in the chapter. The person described is faithful. They fear the Lord and obey the servant's voice. Yet they walk "in darkness" and have "no light." Isaiah does not treat this person's darkness as evidence of disobedience. He describes a reverent, obedient person who still cannot see.

The counsel is not to manufacture light. It is to "trust in the name of the LORD, and stay upon his God." To "stay" means to lean on, or to be supported by. The image is of leaning one's weight on something one cannot see clearly.

The servant himself provides the model. In verses 6–9 he endured abuse without being able to see his vindication yet. He relied on the God who "will help." The faithful listener of verse 10 is invited to the same posture.

This verse preserves room for lament. It does not tell the reader to be cheerful in the dark, or to explain the darkness, or to claim it will soon lift. It simply names the condition and offers somewhere to lean. Psalm 88, which ends in darkness without resolution, shows that scripture can hold faithful people in unresolved trouble.

### Isaiah 50:11 — Living by sparks one has lit

> "Behold, all ye that kindle a fire, that compass yourselves about with sparks: walk in the light of your fire, and in the sparks that ye have kindled. This shall ye have of mine hand; ye shall lie down in sorrow."

The contrast with verse 10 is direct. Some people in darkness respond by lighting their own fires and surrounding themselves with sparks. The verse then grants their wish with irony: "walk in the light of your fire." The result, given "of mine hand," is to "lie down in sorrow."

The verse should not be read as a ban on human effort, planning, or practical problem-solving. Isaiah elsewhere commends wise action. The issue is the substitution of self-made light for trust in God. In context, the self-lit fire probably represents trust in other powers, idols, or political schemes, which Isaiah regularly mocks (compare 44:15–17, where a man cooks dinner and makes a god from the same wood).

Sparks give little light and burn out quickly. People who rely on them end up where they began, in darkness, now with the added sorrow of failed self-reliance. Verse 10 offers no fire at all, only a name to trust. That is harder, and Isaiah says it is safer.

### Christ in Isaiah 50

The case for reading Jesus in this chapter rests on three things:

1. The whole of 50:4–9 is a servant song, and later scripture reads the servant songs as fulfilled in Jesus. In the Book of Mormon, Jacob reads this chapter to his people as a witness of Christ (2 Nephi 6–8).
2. Particular details align with the Passion: striking, spitting, a set face, and a vindicating Father.
3. Paul takes the courtroom of verses 8–9 and applies it to those in Christ (Romans 8:31–34).

The servant listens daily, speaks to the weary, suffers without turning back, and trusts his vindication to God. Christian readers see Jesus here because this is how the Gospels depict him. The chapter is equally a description of discipleship. Verse 10 invites any faithful person in darkness to trust as the servant trusted.

### Questions for study

1. Verses 1–3 put a question to the exiles about who ended the relationship. What do you notice about the way God answers his own question? What does he admit and what does he deny?
2. The servant learns before he teaches (verses 4–5). What does the phrase "a word in season to him that is weary" suggest about what kind of teaching Isaiah values?
3. In verse 6, the servant says "I gave." Why might the text emphasize the servant's choice? What would be lost if this verse were used to tell people facing abuse to stay in harm's way?
4. Compare verses 7–9 with Romans 8:31–34. What does Paul keep from Isaiah, and what does he add?
5. Verse 10 describes a faithful person who has no light. What does it ask that person to do, and what does it notice it does not ask them to do?
6. In verse 11, what distinguishes a self-lit fire from ordinary responsible effort?

### Cross-references

- Deuteronomy 24:1–4 explains the written certificate of divorce that verse 1 assumes.
- Jeremiah 3:8 uses the divorce metaphor differently, showing prophets used it flexibly.
- 2 Nephi 7 reproduces this chapter in Jacob's covenant sermon, with a Book of Mormon variant at verse 1 about being cast off forever.
- Exodus 7:18–21 and 10:21–23 supply the stinking fish and darkness images of verses 2–3.
- Matthew 26:67 and 27:26–31 record the striking, spitting, and scourging that parallel verse 6.
- Luke 9:51 describes Jesus setting his face toward Jerusalem, echoing verse 7's flint-like face.

## Isaiah 51

### Chapter orientation

Chapter 51 is a sequence of summons. "Hearken" appears in verses 1, 4, and 7. "Awake, awake" appears in verses 9 and 17. The urgent repetitions set the pace of the chapter. Isaiah first calls the faithful to look back at Abraham and Sarah. He then turns to God himself and calls on God's arm to wake as in the days of the Exodus. Finally he calls Jerusalem to wake from a drunken stupor caused by the cup of God's wrath. The chapter ends with the cup taken away and given to the oppressors.

The Book of Mormon includes this chapter in 2 Nephi 8, continuing Jacob's reading. There are several textual differences, noted below where relevant. Like chapter 50, chapter 51 addresses people who feel forgotten. Its answer combines memory, cosmic power, and justice.

### Isaiah 51:1–2 — The rock and the quarry

> "Hearken to me, ye that follow after righteousness, ye that seek the LORD: look unto the rock whence ye are hewn, and to the hole of the pit whence ye are digged." (51:1)

**Verse 1** addresses not the whole nation but a narrower group: "ye that follow after righteousness, ye that seek the LORD." These are the faithful, perhaps discouraged by their small numbers. The image is a quarry. A building stone is cut from the rock face, and the pit left behind shows where it came from. Isaiah tells the faithful to look back at their origin.

**Verse 2** explains: "Look unto Abraham your father, and unto Sarah that bare you: for I called him alone, and blessed him, and increased him." Sarah appears by name alongside Abraham. This is rare in the prophets and significant here. Abraham was the rock and Sarah was the quarry. The two of them together are the origin of the people.

Sarah's story especially fits the exiles. She was barren for decades and laughed at the promise of a son (Genesis 18:12; 21:6–7). In chapter 54 the barren mother is Zion herself. Isaiah is preparing that image here.

The phrase "I called him alone" makes the point clear. God's work began with a single man and a barren woman. If God could build a people from one couple, he can rebuild Zion from a small remnant. The faithful minority should not measure their prospects by their present size.

### Isaiah 51:3 — From wilderness to Eden

> "For the LORD shall comfort Zion: he will comfort all her waste places; and he will make her wilderness like Eden, and her desert like the garden of the LORD; joy and gladness shall be found therein, thanksgiving, and the voice of melody."

Having recalled Abraham, Isaiah reaches back further, to Eden. The ruined land around Jerusalem, "her waste places," will become "like the garden of the LORD."

The verse moves from landscape to sound. It ends with joy, gladness, thanksgiving, and "the voice of melody." Comfort is not just repaired ground. It is a renewed community with singing in it. The ruined city in Lamentations is silent and weeping (Lamentations 1:4). Isaiah promises the opposite.

"Comfort" connects this verse to 40:1 ("Comfort ye, comfort ye my people"), the opening of this section of Isaiah. The word keeps returning, here and at 51:12, 51:19, 52:9, and 54:11.

### Isaiah 51:4–6 — Justice for the nations and a salvation that outlasts the sky

**Verse 4** widens the audience to "my people" and "my nation." The promise: "a law shall proceed from me, and I will make my judgment to rest for a light of the people." The word "law" here likely means instruction in the broad sense, God's teaching. "Judgment" means justice. God's justice will become a light for the peoples, recalling the servant's mission as "a light to the Gentiles" (49:6).

**Verse 5** speaks of nearness and reach. "My righteousness is near; my salvation is gone forth, and mine arms shall judge the people; the isles shall wait upon me, and on mine arm shall they trust." In Isaiah, "righteousness" and "salvation" often appear as a pair. Here righteousness includes God's faithful action to put things right. The "isles," the farthest coastlands, wait for God's arm. The image of the arm reappears in 51:9, 52:10, and 53:1.

**Verse 6** contrasts permanence and impermanence. "The heavens shall vanish away like smoke, and the earth shall wax old like a garment, and they that dwell therein shall die in like manner: but my salvation shall be for ever." What seems most stable, the sky and ground, is temporary compared with God's salvation. The moth and garment image from 50:9 is extended to the whole cosmos. The comparison does not belittle the created world. It shows the reliability of God's saving purpose by contrast with even the most stable things humans know.

### Isaiah 51:7–8 — The law in the heart and the brief power of ridicule

> "Hearken unto me, ye that know righteousness, the people in whose heart is my law; fear ye not the reproach of men, neither be ye afraid of their revilings." (51:7)

**Verse 7** addresses a third group: "ye that know righteousness, the people in whose heart is my law." This is the language of internalized covenant, close to Jeremiah 31:33, where God promises to write his law on the people's hearts.

Such people will likely face "reproach" and "revilings," meaning public scorn and insults. Isaiah does not say they will not be mocked. He says they need not fear the mockers.

**Verse 8** gives the reason, using a doubled image: "the moth shall eat them up like a garment, and the worm shall eat them like wool." The mockers are as perishable as fabric. Against them stands God's righteousness, which lasts "for ever," and his salvation, which lasts "from generation to generation."

The logic resembles 50:9. The servant's accusers wear out like a garment, and so do the scornful who oppose the faithful. Ridicule is real and can be painful, but it does not last.

### Isaiah 51:9–11 — Wake up, arm of the Lord

> "Awake, awake, put on strength, O arm of the LORD; awake, as in the ancient days, in the generations of old. Art thou not it that hath cut Rahab, and wounded the dragon?" (51:9)

The direction of the summons reverses. In verses 1–8, God called his people to listen. Now someone calls on God. This is the language of lament psalms, which ask God to wake (Psalm 44:23).

**Verse 9** asks the arm of the Lord to act as it did "in the ancient days." It then names two defeated foes: Rahab and the dragon.

The name Rahab here is not the woman of Jericho (Joshua 2). The two names are spelled differently in Hebrew. In poetry, Rahab names a monster associated with the sea and chaos. In Isaiah 30:7 and Psalm 87:4 it is also a poetic name for Egypt. Donald W. Parry's chapter page notes that the referent here is uncertain: either a chaos monster or Egypt, and the passage may hold both in view ([Parry, Old Testament Minute: Isaiah 51](https://scripturecentral.org/archive/books/book-chapter/isaiah-51)).

The surrounding poetry supports the double reading. Psalm 74:13–17 praises God for dividing the sea and breaking the heads of dragons, and it moves from creation to deliverance. In Israel's poetic imagination, the God who subdued the chaos waters at creation is the God who split the Red Sea at the Exodus.

**Verse 10** makes the Exodus explicit. "Art thou not it which hath dried the sea, the waters of the great deep; that hath made the depths of the sea a way for the ransomed to pass over?" The "great deep" recalls Genesis 1:2. The "way for the ransomed" recalls Exodus 14–15. Isaiah merges creation and redemption into a single act of power.

**Verse 11** draws the conclusion. "Therefore the redeemed of the LORD shall return, and come with singing unto Zion; and everlasting joy shall be upon their head: they shall obtain gladness and joy; and sorrow and mourning shall flee away." This verse is nearly identical to 35:10. The repetition is deliberate. The return from Babylon will be a new Exodus.

The phrasing at the end is striking. Sorrow and mourning do not simply fade. They "flee away," as if they were pursuers who now run from the redeemed. The verse acknowledges that sorrow has been real. It promises that it will not have the last word.

### Isaiah 51:12–14 — Real terror, deeper comfort

**Verse 12** answers the plea. "I, even I, am he that comforteth you." The doubled "I" is emphatic. God then asks why his people fear "a man that shall die, and of the son of man which shall be made as grass." The grass image recalls 40:6–8, where all flesh is grass but the word of God stands.

**Verse 13** describes the problem in detail: "And forgettest the LORD thy maker, that hath stretched forth the heavens, and laid the foundations of the earth; and hast feared continually every day because of the fury of the oppressor, as if he were ready to destroy? and where is the fury of the oppressor?"

The people's fear is not imaginary. Babylon was a real oppressor. Isaiah's point is about proportion. Constant fear of a mortal tyrant has crowded out memory of the Creator. The closing question, "where is the fury of the oppressor?", anticipates a moment when the empire's power will be gone.

**Verse 14** is concrete. "The captive exile hasteneth that he may be loosed, and that he should not die in the pit, nor that his bread should fail." The prisoner is bowed down and fears dying in a pit and going hungry. The promise is release and food. Divine comfort here is not only an inner feeling. It includes freedom from prison and enough to eat.

This is an important balance in the chapter. The text does not tell people in danger that their fear is spiritual failure. It names danger as danger, then sets God's power and faithfulness against it. Reading these verses as a rebuke to the frightened would misunderstand them. They are a promise to the frightened.

### Isaiah 51:15–16 — The Creator's words in a sheltered mouth

**Verse 15** reaffirms God's identity. "But I am the LORD thy God, that divided the sea, whose waves roared: The LORD of hosts is his name." This repeats the Exodus image and adds a formal name declaration.

**Verse 16** returns to an individual or collective addressee: "And I have put my words in thy mouth, and I have covered thee in the shadow of mine hand, that I may plant the heavens, and lay the foundations of the earth, and say unto Zion, Thou art my people."

The language closely resembles 49:2, where the servant says God hid him "in the shadow of his hand." It also resembles Jeremiah 1:9, where God touches Jeremiah's mouth and puts his words there. The addressee in 51:16 may be Israel, or the servant, or the prophet speaking on Israel's behalf. The ambiguity seems to fit Isaiah's fluid use of servant language.

The purpose clause is significant. God shelters his messenger so that he may "plant the heavens," "lay the foundations of the earth," and "say unto Zion, Thou art my people." The restoration of Zion is described as a new creation. The covenant formula, "my people," is the climax.

### Isaiah 51:17–18 — Jerusalem drunk on the cup of wrath

> "Awake, awake, stand up, O Jerusalem, which hast drunk at the hand of the LORD the cup of his fury; thou hast drunken the dregs of the cup of trembling, and wrung them out." (51:17)

The second "awake, awake" is directed at Jerusalem. **Verse 17** uses a prophetic image found in Jeremiah 25:15–17 and Psalm 75:8: the cup of God's wrath. Drinking it causes staggering and collapse. Jerusalem has drunk it all, "the dregs," and "wrung them out."

The image describes the experience of judgment as overwhelming disorientation. It does not depict God as cruel. It portrays the consequences of covenant unfaithfulness in the stark language of covenant poetry.

**Verse 18** adds a painful detail. "There is none to guide her among all the sons whom she hath brought forth; neither is there any that taketh her by the hand of all the sons that she hath brought up." In ancient Israel a son was expected to support a parent who had drunk too much (a duty mentioned in Ugaritic literature and implied here). Jerusalem's children cannot do this. They have been killed or exiled. The city staggers alone.

### Isaiah 51:19–20 — Two calamities and sons trapped like animals

**Verse 19**: "These two things are come unto thee; who shall be sorry for thee? desolation, and destruction, and the famine, and the sword: by whom shall I comfort thee?" The "two things" are probably two pairs: desolation and destruction affecting the land, famine and sword affecting the people. The questions "who shall be sorry for thee?" and "by whom shall I comfort thee?" echo Lamentations 2:13, where the poet cannot find anything to compare to Jerusalem's suffering.

**Verse 20**: "Thy sons have fainted, they lie at the head of all the streets, as a wild bull in a net: they are full of the fury of the LORD, the rebuke of thy God." The image is of a wild animal caught in a hunter's net, exhausted from struggling. The sons lie collapsed at street corners. Lamentations 2:19 and 4:1 describe children fainting from hunger "in the top of every street," in nearly identical language.

The Book of Mormon text differs here. In 2 Nephi 8:19, "these two things" becomes "these two sons," and 2 Nephi 8:20 reads "Thy sons have fainted, save these two." Parry's chapter page discusses this variant ([Parry, Isaiah 51](https://scripturecentral.org/archive/books/book-chapter/isaiah-51)). Latter-day Saint readers have connected the "two sons" with the two witnesses of Revelation 11. That reading belongs to the Book of Mormon form of the text and its interpretation. The phrase "save these two" is not in the KJV of Isaiah and should not be read into it.

### Isaiah 51:21–23 — The cup taken away and given to the tormentors

**Verse 21**: "Therefore hear now this, thou afflicted, and drunken, but not with wine." Jerusalem's staggering is caused by suffering, not wine. The city is addressed with sympathy as "afflicted."

**Verse 22** reverses the judgment. "Thus saith thy Lord the LORD, and thy God that pleadeth the cause of his people, Behold, I have taken out of thine hand the cup of trembling, even the dregs of the cup of my fury; thou shalt no more drink it again." The phrase "pleadeth the cause of his people" returns to courtroom language. God now acts as Jerusalem's defense advocate. The cup is removed with a promise of finality: "no more."

**Verse 23** completes the reversal. "But I will put it into the hand of them that afflict thee; which have said to thy soul, Bow down, that we may go over: and thou hast laid thy body as the ground, and as the street, to them that went over." The oppressors had forced Jerusalem to lie down so they could walk on her back, an ancient practice of humiliating conquered peoples. Now they must drink the cup.

Two things should be said about this verse. First, it is a matter of justice, not personal revenge. God, not Jerusalem, transfers the cup. The text gives no license for the oppressed to inflict cruelty in turn. Second, it insists that redemption is not merely inner calm. People who have been crushed are not told simply to feel better. The structures of oppression are dismantled. God's comfort includes justice.

### Christ in Isaiah 51

Chapter 51 does not mention a servant who suffers, and the New Testament does not quote it as a prophecy of Jesus. The connection to Christ is more indirect but still real. First, verses 9–11 call on the "arm of the LORD" to wake and redeem. In 53:1 the "arm of the LORD" is revealed in the servant himself. Christian readers see the answer to 51:9's plea in the servant of 53.

Second, the image of the cup carries into the Gospels. In Gethsemane Jesus prays that "this cup" might pass (Matthew 26:39; Mark 14:36). In Doctrine and Covenants 19:18 he speaks of drinking a "bitter cup." Isaiah 51:22 promises that God will take the cup of fury from his people. Christian theology sees Jesus drinking the cup himself so that it might be taken from others. That is a theological synthesis built across several texts, not Isaiah's own claim. It does help explain why Christians feel Gethsemane resonates so strongly with this chapter.

### Questions for study

1. Why might verse 2 name Sarah alongside Abraham? How does her story prepare the reader for chapter 54?
2. Verses 7–8 say the faithful will face reproach but need not fear it. What reasons does the text offer, and what does it not promise?
3. In verses 9–11, Isaiah merges creation and Exodus. What does that combination suggest about how he understands redemption from exile?
4. Verses 12–14 address real fear of a real oppressor. How does Isaiah respond to that fear without dismissing it?
5. In verses 22–23 the cup is taken from Jerusalem and given to her tormentors. What does this suggest about the relationship between comfort and justice? Why is it important that God, rather than Jerusalem, transfers the cup?

### Cross-references

- Genesis 12:1–3 and 21:1–7 recount Abraham's call and Sarah's late child, the background of verses 1–2.
- 2 Nephi 8 reproduces this chapter, with variants at verses 19–20 that should be labeled when cited.
- Jeremiah 31:33 promises the law written on hearts, the internal covenant of verse 7.
- Psalm 74:13–17 joins dragon-slaying with creation, illuminating Rahab and the dragon in verse 9.
- Exodus 14–15 describes the sea crossing that verses 10 and 15 recall.
- Isaiah 35:10 is nearly identical to verse 11 and shows the return as a new Exodus.
- Isaiah 49:2 and Jeremiah 1:9 share verse 16's language of words placed in the mouth and the sheltering hand.
- Matthew 26:39 records Jesus's prayer about the cup, which Christian readers connect to verses 17–22.

## Isaiah 52

### Chapter orientation

Chapter 52 continues the "awake, awake" sequence from chapter 51. Jerusalem, who drank the cup of wrath in 51:17, is now told to get up, dress, and shake off the dust. A messenger runs across the mountains with good news. Watchmen sing. The exiles are told to leave Babylon carrying the temple vessels.

At verse 13 the tone shifts dramatically as God introduces his servant. Verses 13–15 begin the great poem that continues through chapter 53. Nearly every verse of this chapter is quoted or echoed in the Book of Mormon (2 Nephi 8:24–25; Mosiah 12, 15; 3 Nephi 16, 20–21) and in the Doctrine and Covenants (section 113). The Restoration has paid special attention to this chapter, which this guide notes where appropriate.

### Isaiah 52:1–2 — Get up, get dressed, get loose

> "Awake, awake; put on thy strength, O Zion; put on thy beautiful garments, O Jerusalem, the holy city: for henceforth there shall no more come into thee the uncircumcised and the unclean." (52:1)

**Verse 1** uses the same words God's people addressed to the arm of the Lord in 51:9 ("Awake, awake, put on strength"). Now God says them back to Zion. Zion is told to put on "strength" and "beautiful garments," like a bride or priest preparing for festival. The promise that the "uncircumcised and the unclean" will no longer come in refers to foreign conquerors who had entered and defiled the city. In context, this is a promise of security from invasion. It is not an exclusion of foreigners as such. Chapter 56 explicitly welcomes foreigners who join themselves to the Lord.

**Verse 2** is a series of commands. "Shake thyself from the dust; arise, and sit down, O Jerusalem: loose thyself from the bands of thy neck, O captive daughter of Zion." "Sit down" likely means to sit enthroned, rising from the dust onto a seat of dignity. The verse reverses 47:1, where "daughter of Babylon" is told to "sit in the dust" and lose her throne. Babylon goes down while Zion comes up.

"Daughter of Zion" is a personification of the city and its people. It is not a commentary on women. The bands on the neck are the chains of captivity, which the captive is told to loosen.

Doctrine and Covenants 113:7–10 gives an inspired interpretation of these two verses. The "strength" Zion is to put on is the authority of the priesthood, which Zion has a right to by lineage and through which she will be restored to lost power. The bands on her neck are the curses of God, and loosening them means the scattered remnants returning to the Lord. This is an explicitly Restoration reading applied to latter-day Zion. It builds on the verses' ancient sense of restored status and freedom.

### Isaiah 52:3–6 — Sold for nothing, redeemed without money

**Verse 3**: "For thus saith the LORD, Ye have sold yourselves for nought; and ye shall be redeemed without money." This returns to the debt-slavery image of 50:1. God received no payment when his people went into captivity, so he owes no repayment to get them back. The redemption is free, not because it costs nothing but because it is not a financial transaction.

1 Peter 1:18–19 builds on this: "ye were not redeemed with corruptible things, as silver and gold ... But with the precious blood of Christ, as of a lamb without blemish." Peter keeps Isaiah's point that redemption is not bought with money, then names the cost that is paid.

**Verse 4** reviews history. "My people went down aforetime into Egypt to sojourn there; and the Assyrian oppressed them without cause." Two past oppressions are named: Egypt, where Israel first went as guests and became slaves, and Assyria, which oppressed "without cause." The phrase "without cause" is notable. Isaiah elsewhere treats Assyria as an instrument of judgment (10:5–6). Here he says Assyria exceeded any right it had.

**Verse 5** applies the pattern to the present. "Now therefore, what have I here, saith the LORD, that my people is taken away for nought? they that rule over them make them to howl, saith the LORD; and my name continually every day is blasphemed." The people are taken away "for nought," and their rulers make them wail. Their humiliation becomes a mockery of God's name. Ezekiel 36:20–23 develops the same idea: the exile itself profanes God's name, because the nations conclude Israel's God could not protect them.

**Verse 6** responds. "Therefore my people shall know my name: therefore they shall know in that day that I am he that doth speak: behold, it is I." Exodus 6:6–7 makes the same promise: God will redeem Israel with a stretched-out arm, and they will know he is the Lord. The phrase "behold, it is I" is simple and direct. The God whose name was mocked will be recognized by his own act of deliverance.

### Isaiah 52:7 — Beautiful feet on the mountains

> "How beautiful upon the mountains are the feet of him that bringeth good tidings, that publisheth peace; that bringeth good tidings of good, that publisheth salvation; that saith unto Zion, Thy God reigneth!"

The scene is Jerusalem waiting for news. A runner appears on the mountains around the city. In ordinary circumstances, dusty, calloused feet are not beautiful. These are, because of what they bring. The messenger announces peace, good, salvation, and one sentence that sums up the rest: "Thy God reigneth!" God's kingship over Babylon and the nations is the substance of the good news. The companion Scripture Helps explains the messenger's feet in this setting ([Scripture Helps](https://www.churchofjesuschrist.org/study/manual/scripture-helps-old-testament/39-isaiah-50-57?lang=eng)). Nahum 1:15 uses a nearly identical image of a messenger announcing Nineveh's fall.

Paul quotes this verse in Romans 10:15 to describe preachers sent to proclaim the gospel. The Greek word translated "gospel" in the New Testament carries the meaning of "good tidings" announced here.

The Book of Mormon presents the most extended engagement with this verse. In Mosiah 12:20–24, the priests of King Noah ask Abinadi what Isaiah 52:7–10 means. A Scripture Central KnowHy proposes that the question was a legal or theological challenge. The priests may have meant to use the passage's joyful tone to undercut Abinadi's message of judgment ([Scripture Central, "Why Would Noah's Priests Quiz Abinadi on Isaiah?"](https://scripturecentral.org/knowhy/why-would-noahs-priests-quiz-abinadi-on-isaiah)). The priests' exact motives are a scholarly reconstruction, not something the narrative states. What the narrative does show is Abinadi's response. He reads Isaiah 53 to them, setting the joyful passage back in its context of suffering (Mosiah 14). In Mosiah 15:13–18, he identifies the beautiful messengers as the prophets who publish peace and, above all, the Lord himself as "the founder of peace."

### Isaiah 52:8–10 — Watchmen, ruins, and the nations all see

**Verse 8**: "Thy watchmen shall lift up the voice; with the voice together shall they sing: for they shall see eye to eye, when the LORD shall bring again Zion." The watchmen on Jerusalem's walls see the runner and then see what he announces: the Lord returning to Zion.

The phrase "eye to eye" in English often means agreement of opinion. In Hebrew it is closer to "with their own eyes," that is, seeing directly, face to face (compare Numbers 14:14, where the Lord is "seen face to face," literally "eye to eye"). The watchmen sing together because they all witness the same thing. Shared sight produces shared song. Latter-day Saint usage sometimes uses "see eye to eye" for unity of understanding. That is a meaningful application, but the verse's primary point is eyewitness seeing.

**Verse 9**: "Break forth into joy, sing together, ye waste places of Jerusalem: for the LORD hath comforted his people, he hath redeemed Jerusalem." Even the ruins are called to sing. The verbs are in the past tense: God "hath comforted," "hath redeemed." This is the Hebrew prophetic perfect, which describes a certain future as though it had already happened.

**Verse 10**: "The LORD hath made bare his holy arm in the eyes of all the nations; and all the ends of the earth shall see the salvation of our God." A warrior rolled up his sleeve to fight. God's arm, called upon in 51:9, is now bared. The audience expands: "all the nations" and "all the ends of the earth" will see this salvation. Zion's restoration is meant to be public and universal.

The resurrected Jesus quotes verses 8–10 to the Nephites in 3 Nephi 16:18–20 and again in 3 Nephi 20:32–35, applying them to the gathering of Israel and the latter-day fulfillment of the covenant. In 3 Nephi 20:34–35 the waste places and the nations seeing God's arm are tied to the redemption of Jerusalem in the last days. Abinadi also quotes these verses in Mosiah 15:29–31.

### Isaiah 52:11–12 — Depart cleanly, without panic

> "Depart ye, depart ye, go ye out from thence, touch no unclean thing; go ye out of the midst of her; be ye clean, that bear the vessels of the LORD." (52:11)

**Verse 11** commands the exiles to leave Babylon. They are to "touch no unclean thing" and be "clean," because they carry "the vessels of the LORD." When Babylon sacked Jerusalem, it took the temple's gold and silver vessels (2 Kings 25:13–15). Ezra 1:7–11 records that Cyrus returned these vessels, which were carried back to Jerusalem. The companion Scripture Helps notes this connection ([Scripture Helps](https://www.churchofjesuschrist.org/study/manual/scripture-helps-old-testament/39-isaiah-50-57?lang=eng)). Those carrying holy things were to be ritually clean, as priests were.

Paul quotes this verse in 2 Corinthians 6:17 ("come out from among them, and be ye separate ... and touch not the unclean thing") when urging the Corinthian Christians to avoid idolatry. Doctrine and Covenants 38:42 and 133:5 also apply its language to latter-day disciples. In these later uses, the vessels become a figure for the disciples themselves or the sacred things entrusted to them.

**Verse 12** contrasts this departure with the Exodus. "For ye shall not go out with haste, nor go by flight: for the LORD will go before you; and the God of Israel will be your rereward." The first Exodus was in haste: Exodus 12:11 tells Israel to eat the Passover "in haste" with sandals on. This new Exodus need not be panicked. God goes before them and also serves as "rereward," the rear guard. Exodus 14:19–20 shows the angel and pillar of cloud moving behind Israel to protect them from Pharaoh's army. Here God guards both the front and rear of the column.

### Isaiah 52:13–15 — The servant exalted, disfigured, and astonishing

> "Behold, my servant shall deal prudently, he shall be exalted and extolled, and be very high." (52:13)

Here the great servant poem begins. It continues to 53:12, and the chapter break should not separate these verses from what follows.

**Verse 13** opens with God's voice and announces the end before the beginning. The servant "shall deal prudently," a verb that means to act wisely and therefore succeed. He will be "exalted and extolled, and be very high." The three verbs pile up exaltation. Isaiah uses similar language for God himself, "high and lifted up" in 6:1 and "the high and lofty One" in 57:15. The servant's exaltation is described in terms usually reserved for the divine.

The poem begins with vindication so the reader knows the story's outcome before hearing the suffering. That structure matters. What follows is not tragedy without hope.

**Verse 14** introduces the shock. "As many were astonied at thee; his visage was so marred more than any man, and his form more than the sons of men." "Astonied" is archaic English for "astonished" or "appalled." The servant's appearance is "marred," disfigured beyond recognition as human.

The grammar shifts from "thee" to "his," a change preserved in the KJV. Some interpreters think "thee" refers to Israel or Zion, briefly addressed, and others think it addresses the servant directly before shifting. The shift is part of the passage's texture.

**Verse 15** completes the sentence. "So shall he sprinkle many nations; the kings shall shut their mouths at him: for that which had not been told them shall they see; and that which they had not heard shall they consider."

The word translated "sprinkle" is a verb used for priests sprinkling blood or water in purification rites (Leviticus 4:6; 16:14). Many modern translations read "startle" or "astonish," partly because the ancient Greek translation reads that many nations "shall marvel." The companion Scripture Helps acknowledges these options, "sprinkle," "startle," and "astound," and notes that the Joseph Smith Translation reads "gather" ([Scripture Helps](https://www.churchofjesuschrist.org/study/manual/scripture-helps-old-testament/39-isaiah-50-57?lang=eng)). Each reading yields a different emphasis:

- "Sprinkle" suggests a priestly act of purification extended to the nations.
- "Startle" suggests the nations' shock.
- "Gather" connects the servant's work to the gathering of Israel and the nations.

Whichever reading is adopted, the overall movement is clear. As many were appalled at the servant's disfigurement, so many nations and kings will be dumbstruck by his exaltation. Kings "shut their mouths" in the ancient gesture of awe. They see and understand what no one had told them.

Paul quotes the second half of verse 15 in Romans 15:21 to explain his mission to preach Christ where he had not been named. The servant's story reaching those who had never heard of it becomes, for Paul, the pattern of the Gentile mission.

In 3 Nephi 20:43–45 the resurrected Jesus quotes verses 13–15. In 3 Nephi 21:8–10 he speaks of a latter-day work at which "kings shall shut their mouths," and then says: "the life of my servant shall be in my hand; therefore they shall not hurt him, although he shall be marred because of them." The companion Scripture Helps notes that this marred servant in 3 Nephi 21 can be understood as applying to Joseph Smith, while identifying Jesus as the ultimate fulfillment. A Scripture Central KnowHy surveys a range of identifications of the servant in 3 Nephi 21, including Israel collectively, individual readings, and a proposal by Gaye Strathearn and Jacob Moody that the Book of Mormon itself is personified in 3 Nephi 21:10 ([Scripture Central, "Who Is the Servant Spoken of by Christ?"](https://scripturecentral.org/knowhy/who-is-the-servant-spoken-of-by-christ)). That proposal adds a possible layer of meaning. It does not settle the question, and it does not replace the Christological reading.

This is servant language working at multiple levels. One textual pattern can describe Israel, a prophet, a latter-day instrument, and Jesus Christ, in whom Christian and Latter-day Saint readers find it finally fulfilled.

### Christ in Isaiah 52

The chapter provides at least four Christ-centered connections, each with a different textual basis:

1. Verse 7's messenger is explicitly identified with Christ by Abinadi, who calls the Lord the "founder of peace" (Mosiah 15:18). Paul applies the verse to gospel preachers (Romans 10:15).
2. Verse 3's redemption "without money" is the basis for 1 Peter 1:18–19, which names Christ's blood as the price.
3. Verses 13–15 begin the poem that the New Testament and the Book of Mormon most explicitly read as fulfilled in Jesus: exalted, disfigured, astonishing kings.
4. Verse 10's bared arm of the Lord leads directly into 53:1's question about to whom that arm is revealed. Christian readers find the answer in the servant.

### Questions for study

1. Verse 1 repeats words from 51:9, now spoken by God to Zion. What does this reversal suggest about the relationship between asking God to act and being asked to act?
2. Why does Isaiah describe feet, of all things, as beautiful in verse 7? What does Abinadi add when he identifies who the messengers are?
3. If "eye to eye" in verse 8 means seeing directly rather than agreeing in opinion, how does that change the way you read the verse?
4. Verse 12 contrasts this Exodus with the first. What is the significance of having God guard both the front and the rear?
5. Verse 13 announces the servant's exaltation before describing his suffering. How might that order shape the way you read chapter 53?
6. What does each reading of 52:15, "sprinkle," "startle," and "gather," emphasize? Why might it be useful to hold several in view?

- Doctrine and Covenants 113:7–10 interprets 52:1–2 as priesthood authority and the return of scattered remnants.

- 3 Nephi 16:18–20 and 20:32–35 have Jesus quote verses 8–10 regarding latter-day gathering.
- Ezra 1:7–11 records the return of the temple vessels that verse 11 envisions.
- Exodus 12:11 and 14:19–20 illuminate verse 12's contrast between haste and protected departure.
- Romans 15:21 and 3 Nephi 20:43–45; 21:8–10 apply verses 13–15 to mission and latter-day work.

## Isaiah 53

### Chapter orientation

This chapter is the center of the week's reading and continues the poem begun at 52:13. The Hebrew does not mark speakers, so the following divisions are a common, plausible reconstruction rather than a settled fact. On that reading, God speaks in 52:13–15. A group calling itself "we" then speaks through roughly 53:10a, confessing its earlier misjudgment of the servant. God's voice returns at 53:10b or 53:11 and continues through 53:12.

Who is the "we"? Several answers are possible. It may be the kings and nations of 52:15, who have now come to understand. It may be Israel, or a part of Israel, confessing its failure to recognize the servant. In the Christian reading, it is everyone who confesses that Christ's suffering was for them. The text does not fix the answer. Readers have found each of these meaningful.

Donald W. Parry, in "The Messiah in Isaiah 53: Four Recurring Themes," organizes the chapter around four themes:

1. the servant's suffering;
2. his bearing of others' burdens;
3. his death;
4. his vindication and reward.

These four groupings are Parry's ([Parry, "The Messiah in Isaiah 53"](https://biblecentral.info/library/chapter/the-messiah-in-isaiah-53-four-recurring-themes/)). This guide uses them as a theological synthesis rather than only a checklist of fulfilled predictions. That use is this guide's editorial application, not a claim about Parry's own view. In this guide's reading, the themes interweave through the poem rather than appearing in neat sequence.

In the Book of Mormon, Abinadi quotes the entire chapter to Noah's priests (Mosiah 14). One modern scholarly proposal about how Mosiah uses the chapter is noted briefly at verse 11.

### Isaiah 53:1 — Who could have believed it?

> "Who hath believed our report? and to whom is the arm of the LORD revealed?"

The first question can be read two ways. "Our report" may be something "we" heard, the astonishing news of 52:13–15, which nobody would have believed. Or it may be something "we" announced, which our hearers did not believe. Either way, the servant's story was beyond belief.

The second question takes up the image of God's arm, bared in 52:10 and called upon in 51:9. The arm of the Lord is God's saving power. To whom has it been revealed? The surprising answer, which unfolds in the following verses, is that it was revealed in this despised servant. No one expected God's power to look like this.

John 12:37–38 quotes this verse to explain why many did not believe in Jesus despite his signs. Romans 10:16 quotes it to explain why not all accepted the gospel. Mosiah 14:1 places it at the start of Abinadi's quotation. All three uses concern the same problem: people failing to recognize God's work when it does not match their expectations.

### Isaiah 53:2 — A sapling in dry ground

> "For he shall grow up before him as a tender plant, and as a root out of a dry ground: he hath no form nor comeliness; and when we shall see him, there is no beauty that we should desire him."

The servant grows up "before him," before the Lord, like a "tender plant," a young shoot, and "a root out of a dry ground." The image recalls 11:1 and 11:10, where the messianic king is a shoot from the stump of Jesse. Here the emphasis is on fragility. A sapling in dry ground is unlikely to thrive and easy to overlook.

The second half of the verse says the servant had "no form nor comeliness" and no "beauty that we should desire him." This does not mean he was ugly. Its point is that nothing in his appearance signaled importance. David was described as "of a beautiful countenance" (1 Samuel 16:12). Saul was taller than anyone (1 Samuel 9:2). The servant had no such marks of royalty.

This verse is sometimes read as a physical description of Jesus. The Gospels never describe what Jesus looked like, and the verse is not intended as a portrait. It describes how the "we" failed to see anything special about the servant, which is a statement about the observers' perception as much as about the servant.

### Isaiah 53:3 — Despised, and we looked away

> "He is despised and rejected of men; a man of sorrows, and acquainted with grief: and we hid as it were our faces from him; he was despised, and we esteemed him not."

The word "despised" begins and ends the verse, framing it. Between those two words is the servant's experience.

"A man of sorrows, and acquainted with grief": the Hebrew words translated "sorrows" and "grief" include physical pain and sickness. The phrase "acquainted with" uses a verb meaning to know intimately. The servant was familiar with pain and sickness in a personal, experiential way.

The response of the "we" is the most painful part of the verse: "we hid as it were our faces from him." This is how people sometimes react to the severely ill or disfigured, looking away from what is hard to see. "We esteemed him not" sums up their judgment: the servant was of no account.

This is the beginning of a confession. The speakers do not describe the servant's suffering from a safe distance. They confess their own failure to see him. In Isaiah 53 understanding the servant requires admitting that one first misjudged him.

### Isaiah 53:4 — The great reversal

> "Surely he hath borne our griefs, and carried our sorrows: yet we did esteem him stricken, smitten of God, and afflicted."

This verse turns the poem. The first word, "Surely," marks a discovery. The griefs and sorrows that the servant knew in verse 3 were *ours*. He did not just know grief. He carried our grief, as one carries a heavy load.

The second half of the verse confesses the earlier misjudgment. "We did esteem him stricken, smitten of God, and afflicted." Ancient people often assumed that severe suffering meant divine punishment for the sufferer's own sin. Job's friends assumed this. Jesus's disciples assumed it about the man born blind (John 9:2). The speakers assumed it about the servant. Verse 4 is the moment that assumption collapses. The servant was not suffering for his own sins. He was carrying theirs.

This reversal has a pastoral corollary. If the speakers were wrong to assume the servant's suffering proved God's rejection, readers should be cautious about drawing the same conclusion about any sufferer. Isaiah 53 gives no support to the idea that suffering is a sign of guilt. It explicitly confesses that that idea was wrong in the servant's case.

Matthew 8:16–17 quotes this verse in the context of Jesus's healing ministry: "Himself took our infirmities, and bare our sicknesses." Matthew applies it to healing of disease and demon possession, not only to the cross. This reflects the range of the Hebrew words. The servant bears pain and sickness as well as sin.

Alma 7:11–13 develops this extensively. Christ will "take upon him the pains and the sicknesses of his people," and also their infirmities, "that his bowels may be filled with mercy, according to the flesh, that he may know according to the flesh how to succor his people according to their infirmities." Alma reads the servant's burden-bearing as the basis for Christ's understanding and compassion. Alma 7 does not say that every sufferer will discover why they suffer. It says that the Savior knows their suffering from the inside.

### Isaiah 53:5–6 — Wounded for us, straying all of us

> "But he was wounded for our transgressions, he was bruised for our iniquities: the chastisement of our peace was upon him; and with his stripes we are healed." (53:5)

**Verse 5** expands the reversal through a series of contrasts. In each clause, the servant receives suffering and the "we" receive the benefit:

- he was wounded, for our transgressions;
- he was bruised, for our iniquities;
- the chastisement was upon him, for our peace;
- the stripes were his, and we are healed.

"Wounded" is a strong word that can mean pierced. "Bruised" means crushed. "Chastisement" means discipline or correction. "Stripes" are the welts of blows. The images are concrete, physical violence.

The verse draws on several frameworks at once. Wounding and healing are medical images. Chastisement and peace come from a framework of discipline and reconciliation. Transgressions and iniquities belong to legal and covenant language. Isaiah does not explain how the servant's suffering heals others. He gives multiple images, each adding a dimension. Christian theology has developed various models of atonement from these images. The text itself is richer than any single model.

John 19:1 records that Pilate had Jesus scourged, a concrete instance of the "stripes" this verse names. 1 Peter 2:24 quotes the verse directly: "by whose stripes ye were healed."

> "All we like sheep have gone astray; we have turned every one to his own way; and the LORD hath laid on him the iniquity of us all." (53:6)

**Verse 6** begins and ends with "all." "All we" went astray, and the Lord laid on the servant "the iniquity of us all." Between these two "alls" is the image of sheep scattering, each wandering in its own direction. Sin is described here not only as rebellion but as wandering, a loss of direction that is both collective and individual.

The final clause, "the LORD hath laid on him the iniquity of us all," introduces God as the actor. This raises a theological question addressed more fully at verse 10. For now, notice that the verse places God's action within a story of human straying. The servant does not suffer because God is arbitrary. He bears the consequences of human wandering.

1 Peter 2:25 completes the image: "For ye were as sheep going astray; but are now returned unto the Shepherd and Bishop of your souls." The straying sheep of Isaiah 53:6 find their way back to the one who bore their iniquity.

### Isaiah 53:7 — Silent like a lamb

> "He was oppressed, and he was afflicted, yet he opened not his mouth: he is brought as a lamb to the slaughter, and as a sheep before her shearers is dumb, so he openeth not his mouth."

The sheep image from verse 6 continues, but with a reversal. In verse 6, "we" were the sheep, wandering. In verse 7, the servant is a sheep led to slaughter and a ewe before shearers, silent.

"He opened not his mouth" appears twice, framing the verse. The servant does not protest, threaten, or retaliate.

The Gospels record Jesus's silence before his accusers. Mark 14:60–61: "he held his peace, and answered nothing." Matthew 26:63 records the same silence before the high priest. Jesus's silence is not total. He speaks at points during his trials. His silence is a refusal to defend himself in ways that would obstruct the course he had chosen.

Acts 8:32–35 is the most explicit New Testament reading of this verse. An Ethiopian official is reading Isaiah 53:7–8 in his chariot. He asks Philip whether the prophet speaks of himself or someone else. Philip "began at the same scripture, and preached unto him Jesus." The quotation in Acts follows the ancient Greek translation, so its wording differs from the KJV of Isaiah. The Ethiopian's question, "of whom speaketh the prophet this?", is the same question this guide has kept in view. Philip's answer is the Christian one.

A crucial caution: Jesus's silence before unjust accusers was a specific, voluntary act tied to his unique mission. It is not a model for people suffering abuse. The verse must never be used to tell someone being harmed to stay quiet, endure, or refrain from seeking help or justice. 1 Peter 2:21–23 draws on Christ's patient suffering as an example to slaves facing unjust masters, and it too has been misused to urge passivity under abuse. Nothing in Isaiah or the gospel requires victims to remain silent. Indeed, the servant poem itself is an act of speaking up about an injustice that others failed to see.

### Isaiah 53:8 — Taken away by an unjust process

> "He was taken from prison and from judgment: and who shall declare his generation? for he was cut off out of the land of the living: for the transgression of my people was he stricken."

This verse is notoriously difficult in Hebrew, and translations vary widely. "Taken from prison and from judgment" may mean taken away by oppression and legal process, that is, by an unjust trial. "Who shall declare his generation?" is ambiguous:

- It may mean "who could imagine his future descendants," since he was cut off with no children.
- It may mean "who among his generation considered" what was happening to him.
- It may mean "who can describe his life story."

"Cut off out of the land of the living" is a clear reference to death. "For the transgression of my people was he stricken" repeats the substitution theme. The phrase "my people" may shift to God's voice or may still be the community speaking.

Acts 8:33 quotes this verse in its Greek form: "In his humiliation his judgment was taken away: and who shall declare his generation? for his life is taken from the earth." Philip applies it to Jesus's unjust trial and death.

In Mosiah 15:10–13, Abinadi takes up the question of the servant's "generation" through verse 10's promise that he "shall see his seed." His seed, Abinadi says, are the prophets and all who have hearkened to their words and looked forward to redemption. The one "cut off" without natural descendants has a vast spiritual posterity.

### Isaiah 53:9 — An innocent man's grave

> "And he made his grave with the wicked, and with the rich in his death; because he had done no violence, neither was any deceit in his mouth."

The servant is buried among the wicked and the rich. In Hebrew poetry the two may be parallel, with "the rich" meaning those who enrich themselves by oppression. Or they may contrast, with the servant assigned a criminal's grave but ending up in a rich man's tomb.

The second half insists on his innocence: "he had done no violence, neither was any deceit in his mouth." His burial among the wicked was undeserved.

Matthew 27:57–60 describes Joseph of Arimathea, "a rich man," placing Jesus in his own new tomb. Matthew does not cite Isaiah 53:9 explicitly, so the connection rests on readers' recognition of the pattern. 1 Peter 2:22 does quote the verse's second half: "Who did no sin, neither was guile found in his mouth."

### Isaiah 53:10 — "It pleased the LORD": offering, seed, and life

> "Yet it pleased the LORD to bruise him; he hath put him to grief: when thou shalt make his soul an offering for sin, he shall see his seed, he shall prolong his days, and the pleasure of the LORD shall prosper in his hand."

This verse is theologically sensitive and needs careful reading.

"It pleased the LORD to bruise him": the Hebrew verb means to will, intend, or purpose. It is the same root found later in the verse, "the pleasure of the LORD shall prosper in his hand." The repetition is deliberate. The servant's suffering was within God's purpose, and the same purpose will succeed through him. The verse does not say God enjoyed the servant's pain. It says the suffering was not meaningless or outside God's plan. Reading "pleased" as cruel satisfaction misreads both the Hebrew and the poem, which has spent several verses insisting that the servant suffered for others, not as an object of divine hostility.

"When thou shalt make his soul an offering for sin": the Hebrew word translated "offering for sin" is the term for the guilt offering, the sacrifice that made reparation for a wrong (Leviticus 5). The servant's life becomes the reparation that restores what was broken between God and the people.

The verse then turns. The one who was "cut off out of the land of the living" in verse 8 "shall see his seed, he shall prolong his days." This is language of life after death. The servant who died will see descendants and live long. Mosiah 15:10–13 reads "his seed" as the faithful prophets and those who hearken to them. Christian readers have seen in this turn a pointer toward resurrection. In the poem itself, it is the beginning of divine vindication.

### Isaiah 53:11 — The travail of his soul, and satisfaction

> "He shall see of the travail of his soul, and shall be satisfied: by his knowledge shall my righteous servant justify many; for he shall bear their iniquities."

Many readers hear God's voice here, if it has not already resumed in verse 10b. "My righteous servant" supports that reading, although the text does not label its speakers.

"He shall see of the travail of his soul, and shall be satisfied": the servant will look on the result of his labor and be content. "Travail" in Hebrew means hard toil and trouble.

"By his knowledge shall my righteous servant justify many": "his knowledge" can mean the servant's own knowledge, perhaps his experiential knowledge of suffering (verse 3, "acquainted with grief"), or others' knowledge of him. Both senses have been proposed. "Justify many" means to make many righteous or declare many in the right, the legal language of 50:8.

"For he shall bear their iniquities": the burden-bearing of verse 4 is repeated, now as the basis of justification.

Source proposal: Matthew L. Bowen ([Interpreter 68, 2026, PDF](https://cdn.interpreterfoundation.org/jnlpdf/bowen-v68-2026-289-310-PDF.pdf)) proposes that Isaiah 53 patterns the story of Alma's oppressed community in Mosiah 18–24. He gives particular attention to experiential knowledge in Mosiah 24:14. He also proposes, as a possibility rather than an established fact, that the name Amulon may derive from the Hebrew word for "toil" or "travail" in this verse. He cites 2 Nephi 29 in urging empathy for Jewish suffering.

This guide's own position, separate from that proposal: Jewish readings of the servant as suffering Israel are serious readings of the text. Christian readings of Isaiah 53 have sometimes been turned against Jewish people. Any such use is antisemitism, and it must be rejected.

None of this implies that victims deserve persecution or that God imposes all suffering. The narrative of Mosiah 24 shows God sustaining and delivering an oppressed people, not justifying their oppressors. Alma 7:12 connects "according to the flesh" with the Savior's knowledge of how to succor his people, which resonates with "by his knowledge" here.

### Isaiah 53:12 — A portion with the great, numbered with transgressors

> "Therefore will I divide him a portion with the great, and he shall divide the spoil with the strong; because he hath poured out his soul unto death: and he was numbered with the transgressors; and he bare the sin of many, and made intercession for the transgressors."

The final verse uses the image of a victorious king sharing out the spoils of war. The servant receives a share with "the great" and divides spoil with "the strong." But the reason for his victory is the reverse of ordinary conquest. He has not taken lives. He has "poured out his soul unto death." He was "numbered with the transgressors," treated as a criminal. He "bare the sin of many" and "made intercession for the transgressors."

The servant's triumph comes through self-giving, not domination. He wins by losing his life. He pleads for the very people who wronged him.

Luke 22:37 is one of the clearest New Testament applications. On the night of his arrest Jesus quotes this verse about himself: "this that is written must yet be accomplished in me, And he was reckoned among the transgressors." Jesus himself reads the servant poem as fulfilled in his own suffering. At his crucifixion he is placed between two criminals (Luke 23:32–33) and prays for those crucifying him (Luke 23:34). Hebrews 7:25 describes the risen Christ as living to "make intercession" for those who come to God through him. Mosiah 15:8–9 describes Christ standing between the people and justice, having taken their iniquity.

The poem ends, as it began in 52:13, with exaltation. The servant who was despised, struck, silenced, and buried is given a portion with the great.

### Christ in Isaiah 53

The textual case for reading Jesus in this chapter is the strongest anywhere in Isaiah:

- Luke 22:37 records Jesus himself applying verse 12 to his arrest and death.
- Acts 8:32–35 shows Philip preaching Jesus from verses 7–8.
- Matthew 8:17 applies verse 4 to Jesus's healings.
- John 12:38 and Romans 10:16 apply verse 1 to unbelief in Jesus and the gospel.
- 1 Peter 2:22–25 uses verses 5, 6, and 9 to describe Christ's sinless suffering and its healing effect.
- Abinadi quotes the full chapter and explains it as Christ's atonement (Mosiah 14–15).
- Alma 7:11–13 develops the burden-bearing of verse 4 into an explanation of Christ's compassion.

These later uses do not erase the poem's ancient setting. The servant poem grew out of Israel's experience of exile, suffering among the nations, and hope for vindication. Jewish readers who see the servant as Israel are reading something real in the text. Christian readers believe Jesus took up this pattern and fulfilled it in a single, innocent life given for many. The poem's own movement, from misjudgment to confession, from suffering to vindication, prepared later readers to recognize him.

### Questions for study

1. Who might the "we" be in verses 1–6? How does the poem read differently depending on the answer?
2. Verse 4 reverses the speakers' assumption that the servant's suffering meant God had rejected him. What implications does this reversal have for how people interpret others' suffering?
3. Verse 5 combines medical, disciplinary, and legal images. What does each contribute that the others lack?
4. In verse 7 the servant is silent. What distinguishes his voluntary silence from the silence sometimes demanded of people being harmed? Why does that distinction matter?
5. How does "it pleased the LORD" in verse 10 read differently when connected with "the pleasure of the LORD" later in the same verse?
6. Verse 12 describes victory through self-giving and intercession. How does this differ from ordinary images of triumph?

### Cross-references

- John 12:37–38 and Romans 10:16 quote verse 1 to explain unbelief in response to God's work.
- Matthew 8:16–17 applies verse 4 to Jesus's healing of sickness, reflecting the Hebrew's range.
- Alma 7:11–13 develops verse 4 into Christ's experiential compassion for every kind of suffering.
- 1 Peter 2:22–25 quotes verses 5, 6, and 9, describing healing and the return of straying sheep.
- Acts 8:32–35 shows Philip preaching Jesus from verses 7–8.
- Mark 14:60–61 and Matthew 26:63 record Jesus's silence, echoing verse 7.
- Matthew 27:57–60 describes Jesus's burial by a rich man, resonating with verse 9.
- Leviticus 5 describes the guilt offering behind verse 10's "offering for sin."
- Mosiah 14–15 quotes and explains the whole chapter, including the servant's seed in verse 10.
- Luke 22:37 records Jesus applying verse 12 to himself.
- Hebrews 7:25 describes Christ's continuing intercession, extending verse 12's final clause.

## Isaiah 54

### Chapter orientation

After the servant's suffering and vindication comes a song of joy for Zion. The barren mother bursts into singing, her tent expands, and God speaks to her as a husband restoring a wife he had sent away. The chapter returns to the marriage image of 50:1 and the barren mother of 49:21 and 51:2. It answers both with abundance.

The placement is significant. Chapter 54 follows chapter 53 directly. The servant "shall see his seed" (53:10), and now Zion sees children she never expected.

In the Book of Mormon, the resurrected Jesus quotes the entire chapter in 3 Nephi 22. A Scripture Central KnowHy reads this quotation as the covenant blessings that close Jesus's discourse on the covenant, applied to his Nephite audience ([Scripture Central, "Why Did Jesus Quote All of Isaiah 54?"](https://scripturecentral.org/knowhy/why-did-jesus-quote-all-of-isaiah-54)).

The marriage metaphor needs care. It describes God's relationship with a people, using an ancient image of marriage that differs from modern expectations. God's anger and hiddenness are metaphors for the exile's experience. They are never a model for how a husband should treat a wife. No one should use this chapter to justify enduring abuse or danger in a marriage.

### Isaiah 54:1 — Sing, barren one

> "Sing, O barren, thou that didst not bear; break forth into singing, and cry aloud, thou that didst not travail with child: for more are the children of the desolate than the children of the married wife, saith the LORD."

Zion is addressed as a barren woman. In ancient Israel, barrenness was deeply painful and often socially shameful (compare Hannah in 1 Samuel 1). Now the barren woman is told to sing, because her children will outnumber those of the woman who was married all along.

The image is corporate. "The desolate" is the ruined city. The verse speaks of Jerusalem's repopulation by returning exiles, not of individual women's fertility. It does not promise biological children to anyone. It does not imply that childlessness reflects divine disfavor. If anything, it suggests that God's purposes are not limited to ordinary ways of building a family.

Paul quotes this verse in Galatians 4:27 as part of an allegory contrasting the earthly Jerusalem with the "Jerusalem which is above," which "is free, which is the mother of us all." The barren woman's many children become all who belong to the covenant of promise through Christ, including Gentiles.

### Isaiah 54:2–3 — Make room

> "Enlarge the place of thy tent, and let them stretch forth the curtains of thine habitations: spare not, lengthen thy cords, and strengthen thy stakes." (54:2)

**Verse 2** pictures a tent being enlarged to fit a growing family. Two actions are paired. The tent must be lengthened, cords and curtains stretched out. It must also be strengthened, stakes driven deeper. A larger tent needs firmer anchors. Growth without stability would bring the tent down.

**Verse 3** explains: "For thou shalt break forth on the right hand and on the left; and thy seed shall inherit the Gentiles, and make the desolate cities to be inhabited." The family spreads in every direction, and empty cities fill again.

This tent image shapes Latter-day Saint vocabulary. Doctrine and Covenants 82:14 says Zion must increase in beauty and her stakes be strengthened and her borders enlarged. Doctrine and Covenants 115:6 describes stakes as a defense and refuge. Moroni 10:31 closes the Book of Mormon by quoting 52:1–2 and this verse's command to strengthen stakes and enlarge borders. The term "stake" for a Church unit draws directly on this image.

As a devotional application: the verse holds two goals in balance, making room for more people and strengthening what holds the structure together. A community that only expands, or only consolidates, neglects half the command.

### Isaiah 54:4–6 — Shame forgotten, Maker as husband

**Verse 4** speaks to shame directly. "Fear not; for thou shalt not be ashamed: neither be thou confounded; for thou shalt not be put to shame: for thou shalt forget the shame of thy youth, and shalt not remember the reproach of thy widowhood any more." The word "shame" and its synonyms appear four times. "The shame of thy youth" may refer to Israel's early history, perhaps the Egyptian slavery or early unfaithfulness. "The reproach of thy widowhood" refers to the exile, when Zion was like a woman without a husband. Both will be forgotten. In the ancient Near East a widow often lost social protection, so "widowhood" here conveys vulnerability as well as grief.

**Verse 5** gives the reason. "For thy Maker is thine husband; the LORD of hosts is his name; and thy Redeemer the Holy One of Israel; The God of the whole earth shall he be called." The titles stack:

- Maker;
- husband;
- the LORD of hosts;
- Redeemer;
- the Holy One of Israel;
- God of the whole earth.

The God who made Zion is also her husband and her redeemer. In Israelite law a redeemer was a close relative who bought back property or people lost to debt (Leviticus 25:25; Ruth 4). God takes on that family role.

**Verse 6** recalls the separation. "For the LORD hath called thee as a woman forsaken and grieved in spirit, and a wife of youth, when thou wast refused, saith thy God." The verse is ambiguous in a moving way. God "hath called" Zion back while she was still "forsaken and grieved in spirit." She had been "refused," rejected like a young wife sent away. God calls her not after her grief has healed but in the middle of it.

### Isaiah 54:7–8 — A small moment and an everlasting kindness

> "For a small moment have I forsaken thee; but with great mercies will I gather thee. In a little wrath I hid my face from thee for a moment; but with everlasting kindness will I have mercy on thee, saith the LORD thy Redeemer."

These two verses are built on contrasts:

- a "small moment" set against "great mercies";
- "a little wrath" set against "everlasting kindness";
- forsaking set against gathering;
- hiding the face set against mercy.

In each pair, the second element vastly outweighs the first. The word translated "kindness" is a covenant term meaning steadfast, loyal love.

The verses must be read carefully. From God's perspective, the exile was "a small moment." For those who lived through it, it was decades, the loss of a temple, a homeland, and many lives. Lamentations 5:20 asks, "Wherefore dost thou forget us for ever, and forsake us so long time?" Isaiah 54:7–8 does not dismiss that lament. It does not say the suffering was small to those who suffered. It says that measured against God's everlasting kindness, even long pain is not the final word.

As a devotional application: these verses can offer perspective without demanding that anyone feel their own suffering is minor. The lament of Lamentations and the promise of Isaiah 54 stand together in the canon.

### Isaiah 54:9–10 — Noah's covenant and immovable kindness

**Verse 9**: "For this is as the waters of Noah unto me: for as I have sworn that the waters of Noah should no more go over the earth; so have I sworn that I would not be wroth with thee, nor rebuke thee." God compares the end of exile to the end of the flood. In Genesis 9:8–17 God swore never again to destroy the earth by flood and set the rainbow as a sign. Now God swears with the same seriousness that his wrath against Zion is over.

**Verse 10**: "For the mountains shall depart, and the hills be removed; but my kindness shall not depart from thee, neither shall the covenant of my peace be removed, saith the LORD that hath mercy on thee." Mountains were the most permanent things in the ancient world. God says even they may move before his kindness does. "The covenant of my peace" is a covenant that establishes peace between God and people, a theme Ezekiel 34:25 and 37:26 also develop.

This verse should not be misread as a promise of physical safety. It speaks of God's covenant kindness, not immunity from hardship. The servant of chapter 53 was within God's covenant purpose and still suffered. Covenant peace is the security of a relationship that will not be revoked, not an exemption from pain.

### Isaiah 54:11–12 — A storm-battered city rebuilt in jewels

> "O thou afflicted, tossed with tempest, and not comforted, behold, I will lay thy stones with fair colours, and lay thy foundations with sapphires." (54:11)

**Verse 11** addresses Zion with three descriptions: "afflicted, tossed with tempest, and not comforted." The last phrase is poignant. In 40:1 God commanded comfort for his people. Here the city is described honestly as not yet comforted. The promise is to rebuild it in splendor: stones set in "fair colours" (perhaps an ornamental mortar) and foundations of sapphires.

**Verse 12** continues: "And I will make thy windows of agates, and thy gates of carbuncles, and all thy borders of pleasant stones." Windows, gates, and walls all shimmer with precious stones. The exact identification of some of the gems is uncertain, as with many ancient gem names, but the effect is clear. A city battered by storms will become a city of light and beauty.

Revelation 21:18–21 describes the New Jerusalem with walls of jasper, foundations garnished with precious stones, and gates of pearl. John does not quote Isaiah 54:11–12, but the resonance is strong. The rebuilt Zion of Isaiah becomes, in Revelation, an image of the redeemed creation.

### Isaiah 54:13–14 — Children taught by the Lord

**Verse 13**: "And all thy children shall be taught of the LORD; and great shall be the peace of thy children." Zion's children, the returning people, will be taught directly by God. Their peace will be "great."

Jesus quotes this verse in John 6:45: "It is written in the prophets, And they shall be all taught of God. Every man therefore that hath heard, and hath learned of the Father, cometh unto me." Jesus understands the promise as fulfilled in those who come to him, drawn by the Father.

The verse should not be read as a guarantee that every faithful family's children will choose faith, or that families who experience heartbreak have failed. The promise in context is to the restored covenant community, and its fulfillment is God's work. Readers whose children have taken different paths should not hear this verse as an accusation.

**Verse 14**: "In righteousness shalt thou be established: thou shalt be far from oppression; for thou shalt not fear: and from terror; for it shall not come near thee." The city will be founded on righteousness, the right relationships and justice that God brings. Oppression and terror will be far from it.

### Isaiah 54:15–17 — Hostility does not have the last word

**Verse 15**: "Behold, they shall surely gather together, but not by me: whosoever shall gather together against thee shall fall for thy sake." The verse admits that enemies will gather against Zion. It also clarifies that their hostility is "not by me," not God's initiative. This qualification is important. Not every attack is sent by God. Some opposition comes from human will alone.

**Verse 16**: "Behold, I have created the smith that bloweth the coals in the fire, and that bringeth forth an instrument for his work; and I have created the waster to destroy." This might seem to contradict verse 15, since God created the weapon-maker and the destroyer. The point is sovereignty. Every human power, even a destructive one, exists within creation and under God's ultimate authority. The weapon-maker is a creature, not a rival god. This does not make God the author of every act of destruction. Verse 15 has just said he is not. It means destructive power is limited.

**Verse 17**: "No weapon that is formed against thee shall prosper; and every tongue that shall rise against thee in judgment thou shalt condemn. This is the heritage of the servants of the LORD, and their righteousness is of me, saith the LORD."

The verse addresses both weapons and words, physical attack and legal accusation. Neither will finally succeed. The courtroom language recalls 50:8–9, where the servant's accusers wear out. Now the courtroom victory belongs to all "the servants of the LORD." After the singular servant of 52:13–53:12, the focus moves in 54:17 to a faithful community collectively called "the servants of the LORD." After the singular servant of 52:13–53:12, his followers become servants too. "Their righteousness is of me": their vindication comes from God, not from their own achievement.

"No weapon ... shall prosper" is a promise of ultimate covenant security, not a guarantee against all harm. The servant himself was struck. The heritage of the servants is that no attack will finally defeat God's purpose for them.

### Christ in Isaiah 54

The connections to Christ in this chapter come through:

- its placement immediately after the servant poem, so that the barren mother's children answer the servant's "seed" (53:10);
- Galatians 4:27, which reads the barren woman's children as all who belong to the covenant through Christ;
- John 6:45, where Jesus applies verse 13 to those who come to him;
- 3 Nephi 22, where the resurrected Christ quotes the full chapter as covenant promise to his people;
- verse 17's plural "servants," who share the singular servant's vindication.

The chapter shows what the servant's suffering accomplishes: a restored, enlarged, beautiful community secured by a covenant that will not be removed.

### Questions for study

1. Why might chapter 54 directly follow the servant poem? What connections can you trace between 53:10 and 54:1?
2. Verse 2 pairs lengthening cords with strengthening stakes. What happens to a tent that only does one?
3. How do verses 7–8 hold together divine perspective and human experience? What would be lost if they were used to dismiss someone's long suffering?
4. What does the Noah comparison in verse 9 add to the promise of verse 10?
5. Verses 15 and 16 can seem to be in tension. How do they work together?
6. After the singular servant of 52:13–53:12, verse 17 refers collectively to "the servants of the LORD." How might this literary shift present the community as sharing in the servant's vindication, and how have different interpretive traditions understood the relationship between the servant and this community?

### Cross-references

- Galatians 4:27 quotes verse 1 regarding the "Jerusalem which is above" and her many children.
- 1 Samuel 1 shows the pain of barrenness that verse 1's corporate image draws upon.
- Doctrine and Covenants 82:14 and 115:6 and Moroni 10:31 apply verse 2's stakes to latter-day Zion.
- Leviticus 25:25 and Ruth 4 explain the kinship role behind "Redeemer" in verse 5.
- Lamentations 5:20–22 voices the long lament that verses 7–8 answer without silencing.
- Genesis 9:8–17 records Noah's covenant, the model for verse 9.
- Revelation 21:18–21 describes the jeweled New Jerusalem, resonating with verses 11–12.
- John 6:45 records Jesus quoting verse 13.
- 3 Nephi 22 has Jesus quote this chapter as covenant blessing to the Nephites.

## Isaiah 55

### Chapter orientation

Chapter 55 is an invitation. After the servant's suffering (chapter 53) and Zion's restoration (chapter 54), God calls everyone who is thirsty to come and eat for free. The chapter is the conclusion of Isaiah 40–55. It ends where the section began, with the reliability of God's word (compare 40:8 and 55:11).

The chapter moves in five stages:

1. a free feast (verses 1–2);
2. an everlasting covenant (verses 3–5);
3. a call to seek and return (verses 6–7);
4. God's higher ways (verses 8–9);
5. the effectiveness of God's word (verses 10–11), ending in a joyful exodus through singing creation (verses 12–13).

The lesson's emphasis on seeking the Lord is centered here.

### Isaiah 55:1–2 — Free food, and food that fills

> "Ho, every one that thirsteth, come ye to the waters, and he that hath no money; come ye, buy, and eat; yea, come, buy wine and milk without money and without price." (55:1)

**Verse 1** sounds like a market vendor calling to passersby. The invitation is paradoxical: "buy ... without money and without price." The water, wine, and milk are free. Water is basic survival. Wine and milk are luxury and nourishment. Everything needed is offered to everyone who is thirsty, especially "he that hath no money."

This is grace in its most concrete Old Testament form. Nothing is earned. Thirst is the only qualification.

**Verse 2** asks why people spend money on what doesn't satisfy. "Wherefore do ye spend money for that which is not bread? and your labour for that which satisfieth not? hearken diligently unto me, and eat ye that which is good, and let your soul delight itself in fatness." The verse contrasts a costly pursuit that leaves people hungry with free abundance that satisfies. In the ancient world "fatness" meant rich food, the best of a feast.

The key move is "hearken diligently unto me." The way to eat is to listen. The food is God's word, God's covenant, God himself.

The Book of Mormon uses this invitation in 2 Nephi 9:50–51, where Jacob calls the poor to come and buy without money and not spend labor on what cannot satisfy. In 2 Nephi 26:25, Nephi has the Lord invite all the ends of the earth to buy milk and honey without money and without price. In John 7:37, Jesus cries out at the feast, "If any man thirst, let him come unto me, and drink." Revelation 22:17 closes the Bible with the invitation to "whosoever will" to "take the water of life freely."

### Isaiah 55:3–5 — The sure mercies of David, extended to the nations

**Verse 3**: "Incline your ear, and come unto me: hear, and your soul shall live; and I will make an everlasting covenant with you, even the sure mercies of David." Listening again leads to life. The "everlasting covenant" is described as "the sure mercies of David," meaning God's faithful covenant love promised to David.

In 2 Samuel 7:12–16 God promised David a lasting dynasty. By the exile that dynasty seemed finished. The king was a prisoner in Babylon. Isaiah reapplies the promise to the whole people: what was promised to David is now extended to "you," plural. The Davidic covenant is democratized.

Paul, in Acts 13:34, quotes this verse in his argument that God raised Jesus from the dead: "I will give you the sure mercies of David." For Paul, the resurrection of Jesus, David's descendant, is how the sure mercies become permanent. A king who will never die again means a covenant that will never end.

**Verse 4**: "Behold, I have given him for a witness to the people, a leader and commander to the people." "Him" is David, or the Davidic figure. He serves as witness and leader to the peoples. In Christian reading, the ultimate Davidic leader is Jesus (Revelation 1:5 calls him "the faithful witness").

**Verse 5**: "Behold, thou shalt call a nation that thou knowest not, and nations that knew not thee shall run unto thee because of the LORD thy God, and for the Holy One of Israel; for he hath glorified thee." The pronouns shift from "him" (David) in verse 4 to "thou" (Israel) in verse 5. The role David played toward the nations is now Israel's role. Nations Israel does not know will run to it, not because of Israel's power but because of the God who glorified it.

These pronoun shifts trace a pattern seen throughout this part of Isaiah. Roles move from an individual to the people and back. The servant becomes the servants (54:17). David's mercies become the people's covenant.

### Isaiah 55:6–7 — Seek, call, forsake, return

> "Seek ye the LORD while he may be found, call ye upon him while he is near: Let the wicked forsake his way, and the unrighteous man his thoughts: and let him return unto the LORD, and he will have mercy upon him; and to our God, for he will abundantly pardon."

**Verse 6** gives two parallel commands: "seek ye the LORD" and "call ye upon him." The time phrases, "while he may be found" and "while he is near," create urgency without suggesting God will hide. In context, God has come near in the announcement of redemption. Now is the time to respond.

**Verse 7** spells out what seeking means. The wicked forsake their way. The unrighteous forsake their thoughts. Both actions and inner thinking are involved. Then comes the positive move: "let him return unto the LORD." "Return" in Hebrew is the main word for repentance, turning back.

The promise is generous: "he will have mercy upon him; and to our God, for he will abundantly pardon." "Abundantly" means multiplied, more than enough. Return does not meet a reluctant God. It meets mercy that overflows.

Doctrine and Covenants 88:62–63 echoes this invitation: "Draw near unto me and I will draw near unto you; seek me diligently and ye shall find me."

### Isaiah 55:8–9 — Higher ways, in the context of mercy

> "For my thoughts are not your thoughts, neither are your ways my ways, saith the LORD. For as the heavens are higher than the earth, so are my ways higher than your ways, and my thoughts than your thoughts."

These verses are often quoted on their own to explain why God's actions are beyond human understanding. That use is not entirely wrong. But the context changes their emphasis.

Verse 8 begins with "For," connecting it to verse 7. God's thoughts are higher than human thoughts in the specific matter just discussed: abundant pardon. Humans tend to forgive grudgingly and partially. God forgives abundantly. God's ways are higher in mercy.

Read this way, the verses are not primarily a conversation-stopper, a way to close down hard questions. They are an expansion of hope. Where human calculation would say a person has gone too far, God's thoughts reach higher.

This does not mean hard questions are forbidden. Isaiah itself is full of honest questions and complaints (49:14; 51:9; 40:27). Psalms of lament ask God directly why. The distance between God's thoughts and ours is a reason for humility and for hope, not a ban on sincere inquiry.

### Isaiah 55:10–11 — Rain, snow, and a word that does not come back empty

> "For as the rain cometh down, and the snow from heaven, and returneth not thither, but watereth the earth, and maketh it bring forth and bud, that it may give seed to the sower, and bread to the eater: So shall my word be that goeth forth out of my mouth: it shall not return unto me void, but it shall accomplish that which I please, and it shall prosper in the thing whereto I sent it."

**Verse 10** describes the water cycle from an ancient observer's perspective. Rain and snow fall and don't go back up until they have watered the earth and made it fruitful. The verse ends with "seed to the sower, and bread to the eater," both future harvest and present food.

**Verse 11** applies the image to God's word. Like rain, it goes out and does not return "void," empty. It accomplishes God's purpose and "prosper[s]" in its mission. The verb "prosper" also described the servant's success in 53:10 ("the pleasure of the LORD shall prosper in his hand").

The section of Isaiah that began with "the word of our God shall stand for ever" (40:8) ends with that word's guaranteed effectiveness. The image connects with the food of verses 1–2. God's word is rain that produces bread. Listening is eating, and the word feeds.

The image also implies process. Rain does not produce bread overnight. There is planting, growing, and harvest. God's word works on its own timeline. Christian readers often connect this "word" with Christ, the Word made flesh (John 1:14), who was sent and accomplished his mission. That is a later theological connection, not the verse's explicit claim.

### Isaiah 55:12–13 — Joyful exodus and renewed creation

**Verse 12**: "For ye shall go out with joy, and be led forth with peace: the mountains and the hills shall break forth before you into singing, and all the trees of the field shall clap their hands." The exiles go out with joy and are led with peace. Creation joins in: mountains sing, trees clap. This echoes 52:12, where God guards the departing exiles, and 49:13, where heavens and earth sing. Creation itself participates in redemption.

Doctrine and Covenants 128:22–23 takes up this language: "Let the mountains shout for joy ... and let all the trees of the field praise the Lord." Joseph Smith applies Isaiah's joyful creation to the Restoration and the redemption of the dead.

**Verse 13**: "Instead of the thorn shall come up the fir tree, and instead of the brier shall come up the myrtle tree: and it shall be to the LORD for a name, for an everlasting sign that shall not be cut off." Thorns and briers are signs of the curse in Genesis 3:18. They are replaced by fir and myrtle, evergreens associated with beauty and festival. Isaiah 35:1–2 and 41:19 develop similar images of the desert blooming.

The transformed landscape will be "to the LORD for a name, for an everlasting sign that shall not be cut off." In 56:5 eunuchs receive "an everlasting name, that shall not be cut off." The repetition links the chapters. The transformed creation and the welcomed outsider are both signs of God's lasting name.

### Christ in Isaiah 55

The chapter's connections to Christ rest on:

- Acts 13:34, where Paul applies the "sure mercies of David" in verse 3 to the resurrection of Jesus;
- John 7:37, where Jesus issues an invitation that echoes verse 1;
- the Davidic witness and leader of verse 4, which Christian readers see fulfilled in Jesus as David's heir;
- Revelation 22:17, which near the close of scripture echoes verse 1's invitation as the Spirit and the bride call the thirsty to come and take the water of life freely;
- the Book of Mormon's use of this invitation in 2 Nephi 9 and 26 in explicitly Christ-centered sermons.

In Christian reading, the free feast is offered because the servant of chapter 53 has paid what the thirsty cannot.

### Questions for study

1. Verse 1 invites people to "buy ... without money." What does this paradox suggest about the relationship between grace and human response?
2. Verse 2 contrasts spending on what doesn't satisfy with listening to God. What makes listening the way to "eat"?
3. How do the pronoun shifts in verses 3–5 extend the Davidic promise? What does Acts 13:34 add?
4. How does reading verses 8–9 in the context of verse 7 change their meaning? Does that reading close or open space for hard questions?
5. What does the rain image in verses 10–11 suggest about how God's word works over time?
6. Verse 13 replaces thorns and briers. What does connecting this with Genesis 3:18 suggest about the scope of redemption?

### Cross-references

- 2 Nephi 9:50–51 and 26:25 use verse 1's invitation in Book of Mormon sermons on Christ.
- John 7:37 and Revelation 22:17 issue Christ's invitation to the thirsty, echoing verse 1.
- 2 Samuel 7:12–16 records the Davidic promise that verse 3 extends to the people.
- Acts 13:34 applies the "sure mercies of David" to Jesus's resurrection.
- Doctrine and Covenants 88:62–63 echoes verse 6's call to seek and draw near.
- Isaiah 40:8 begins the section with the enduring word that verses 10–11 complete.
- Genesis 3:18 introduces the thorns and thistles that verse 13 replaces.
- Doctrine and Covenants 128:22–23 applies verse 12's singing creation to the Restoration.

## Isaiah 56

### Chapter orientation

Chapter 56 begins a new section of Isaiah (chapters 56–66). The setting has shifted. The audience now seems to be the community back in the land, dealing with Sabbath, temple, and membership. The questions are practical: who belongs, and what does faithfulness look like in daily life?

The chapter has two contrasting halves. Verses 1–8 welcome foreigners and eunuchs who had feared exclusion. Verses 9–12 condemn leaders who neglect their responsibilities. Hilton's slides place this chapter under hope in the face of exclusion and link Deuteronomy 23:1, Isaiah 56, Mark 11:17, and the eunuch of Acts 8 ([Hilton, Class 40](https://johnhiltoniii.com/findingchrist/class-40-the-founder-of-our-peace/)). The discussion below reads those texts directly.

Parry's chapter page applies the temple language of this chapter to latter-day temples ([Parry, Isaiah 56](https://scripturecentral.org/archive/books/book-chapter/isaiah-56)). That is an explicitly Latter-day Saint application alongside the ancient context of the Jerusalem sanctuary, not a replacement for it.

### Isaiah 56:1–2 — Justice and Sabbath as signs of nearness

**Verse 1**: "Thus saith the LORD, Keep ye judgment, and do justice: for my salvation is near to come, and my righteousness to be revealed." The command is ethical: keep justice, do what is right. The motivation is the nearness of God's salvation.

The word pair is significant. In earlier chapters "righteousness" and "salvation" described what God would do (51:5–6, 8). Now people are called to "do justice" (the same Hebrew root as "righteousness") because God's righteousness is about to be revealed. God's coming salvation is not a reason for passivity. It calls people to live in its light.

**Verse 2**: "Blessed is the man that doeth this, and the son of man that layeth hold on it; that keepeth the sabbath from polluting it, and keepeth his hand from doing any evil." "Son of man" here means simply a human being. The blessing applies to anyone. Two practices are named together:

1. keeping the Sabbath;
2. keeping one's hand from evil.

Worship and ethics are inseparable. A person who keeps Sabbath while doing harm has missed the point. A person who avoids harm while neglecting worship has also missed something.

During and after the exile, Sabbath became an identity marker that could be kept anywhere, without a temple. Its prominence in this chapter reflects that.

### Isaiah 56:3–5 — Foreigners and eunuchs: a name that will not be cut off

> "Neither let the son of the stranger, that hath joined himself to the LORD, speak, saying, The LORD hath utterly separated me from his people: neither let the eunuch say, Behold, I am a dry tree." (56:3)

**Verse 3** names two groups who feared exclusion. The "son of the stranger" is a foreigner who has "joined himself to the LORD." He worries that God has separated him from the covenant people. The eunuch, a man castrated (often as a royal servant in foreign courts), worries that he is "a dry tree," without descendants or future.

These fears had a basis in the law. Deuteronomy 23:1 excluded men with certain genital injuries from the assembly of the Lord. Deuteronomy 23:3–8 excluded some foreign groups. Isaiah addresses both fears directly. He doesn't say the fears are imaginary. He says they should not be spoken as final, because God has answered them.

**Verse 4**: "For thus saith the LORD unto the eunuchs that keep my sabbaths, and choose the things that please me, and take hold of my covenant." The conditions are those of any faithful person: keeping Sabbath, choosing what pleases God, and holding on to the covenant. No additional requirement is placed on the eunuch.

**Verse 5**: "Even unto them will I give in mine house and within my walls a place and a name better than of sons and of daughters: I will give them an everlasting name, that shall not be cut off."

The word translated "place" literally means "hand." It was used for a monument or memorial. 2 Samuel 18:18 says Absalom set up a pillar, called "Absalom's place," because "I have no son to keep my name in remembrance." The eunuch who has no son receives exactly what Absalom tried to build for himself: a memorial and a name. And it is "better than of sons and of daughters." It is placed "in mine house and within my walls," inside the temple courts. It is "everlasting" and "shall not be cut off." The servant was "cut off out of the land of the living" (53:8). The eunuch receives a name that will not be cut off.

Acts 8:26–39 tells of an Ethiopian eunuch who had traveled to Jerusalem to worship and was reading Isaiah 53 on his way home. Philip explains the passage, preaches Jesus, and baptizes him. Acts does not quote Isaiah 56. The connection is that a eunuch, a foreigner, receives the gospel and enters the covenant community through baptism, fulfilling the welcome of verses 3–5 in a concrete way.

The verses should not be read as blaming anyone for childlessness or for any bodily condition. The point is that covenant belonging does not depend on lineage or biological fertility. Those who might feel disqualified from the future are given an everlasting name.

### Isaiah 56:6–8 — A house of prayer for all people

**Verse 6**: "Also the sons of the stranger, that join themselves to the LORD, to serve him, and to love the name of the LORD, to be his servants, every one that keepeth the sabbath from polluting it, and taketh hold of my covenant." Foreigners who join themselves to the Lord are described with the same language used for Israel. They serve, love God's name, become his "servants," keep Sabbath, and hold the covenant. The word "servants" connects them with 54:17's "servants of the LORD."

**Verse 7**: "Even them will I bring to my holy mountain, and make them joyful in my house of prayer: their burnt offerings and their sacrifices shall be accepted upon mine altar; for mine house shall be called an house of prayer for all people." Foreigners are not merely tolerated at the edges. They are brought to the holy mountain, made joyful in God's house, and their offerings are accepted on the altar. The temple belongs to "all people."

Jesus quotes this verse in Mark 11:17 when he clears the temple: "Is it not written, My house shall be called of all nations the house of prayer? but ye have made it a den of thieves." Mark's version keeps the phrase "of all nations," which Matthew and Luke omit in their parallel accounts. Jesus's action in the court where Gentiles could pray takes on added meaning. The space meant for all nations' prayer had been turned into a marketplace.

**Verse 8**: "The Lord GOD which gathereth the outcasts of Israel saith, Yet will I gather others to him, beside those that are gathered unto him." God already gathers Israel's outcasts. Now he promises to gather "others" beyond them. The gathering keeps widening. In John 10:16, Jesus says, "other sheep I have, which are not of this fold: them also I must bring." 3 Nephi 15:21 identifies the Nephites as some of those other sheep. 3 Nephi 16:1–3 speaks of still others.

### Isaiah 56:9–12 — Blind watchmen, mute dogs, selfish shepherds

**Verse 9**: "All ye beasts of the field, come to devour, yea, all ye beasts in the forest." The tone shifts abruptly. Wild animals are invited to devour. The image is of an unguarded flock, open to predators because its protectors have failed. It is a prophetic image of judgment's consequences, not an endorsement of violence.

**Verse 10**: "His watchmen are blind: they are all ignorant, they are all dumb dogs, they cannot bark; sleeping, lying down, loving to slumber." The leaders are watchmen who cannot see and watchdogs who cannot bark. "Dumb" here means mute, unable to give warning. The point is failed responsibility: those who should warn the people are asleep. Ezekiel 33:1–6 describes the watchman's duty to sound the alarm, and the guilt of a watchman who stays silent.

These images are metaphors for neglect of duty. They are not judgments on people who are blind, deaf, or unable to speak. Applying them to disability would invert the text, which condemns the powerful for failing the vulnerable.

**Verse 11**: "Yea, they are greedy dogs which can never have enough, and they are shepherds that cannot understand: they all look to their own way, every one for his gain, from his quarter." The leaders are both lazy (verse 10) and greedy (verse 11). As shepherds, they "look to their own way, every one for his gain." The phrase "their own way" echoes 53:6, where "we have turned every one to his own way." The leaders have turned to their own way in a specifically predatory form. Ezekiel 34:1–10 develops the same charge against shepherds who feed themselves instead of the flock.

**Verse 12**: "Come ye, say they, I will fetch wine, and we will fill ourselves with strong drink; and to morrow shall be as this day, and much more abundant." The leaders party, assuming tomorrow will be like today, only better. The contrast with 55:1–2 is pointed. God offered free wine and food that satisfies to the thirsty. These leaders hoard drink for themselves and assume nothing will ever change.

The two halves of the chapter belong together. Verses 1–8 describe a God who widens the circle to include outsiders. Verses 9–12 describe leaders who neglect the people they are already responsible for. A community can fail both ways, excluding those God welcomes and neglecting those God has entrusted to it.

### Christ in Isaiah 56

The connections to Christ rest on:

- Mark 11:17, where Jesus quotes verse 7 as he clears the temple, applying "all nations" to his own act;
- Acts 8:26–39, where a eunuch is baptized after hearing Jesus preached from Isaiah 53, embodying the welcome of verses 3–5;
- John 10:16 and 3 Nephi 15–16, where Jesus speaks of gathering other sheep, echoing verse 8;
- Jesus's role as the good shepherd (John 10:11), contrasting with the selfish shepherds of verses 10–11.

### Questions for study

1. Verse 2 joins Sabbath keeping with refraining from evil. What happens when one is practiced without the other?
2. Verse 3 names specific fears. Why might it matter that God addresses those fears rather than dismissing them?
3. How does 2 Samuel 18:18 illuminate the "place and a name" in verse 5?
4. In Mark 11:17, Jesus quotes verse 7 while clearing the temple. What does the phrase "all nations" add to the meaning of his action?
5. Verses 9–12 condemn leaders. What responsibilities do the images of watchmen, watchdogs, and shepherds suggest?
6. How do the two halves of the chapter, welcome and condemnation, relate to each other?

### Cross-references

- Deuteronomy 23:1–8 records the exclusions that verses 3–7 answer.
- 2 Samuel 18:18 shows Absalom's memorial "place," illuminating verse 5's monument and name.
- Acts 8:26–39 describes the baptism of an Ethiopian eunuch, embodying the welcome of verses 3–5.
- Mark 11:17 records Jesus quoting verse 7 while clearing the temple.
- John 10:16 and 3 Nephi 15:21; 16:1–3 speak of gathering other sheep, echoing verse 8.
- Ezekiel 33:1–6 describes the watchman's duty, the background of verse 10.
- Ezekiel 34:1–10 condemns shepherds who feed themselves, paralleling verse 11.

## Isaiah 57

### Chapter orientation

Chapter 57 is the most difficult chapter in this week's reading. It begins with a lament for righteous people who die unnoticed. It then launches a fierce attack on idolatry, using sexual metaphors and describing child sacrifice. It closes with one of scripture's most tender passages, in which the high and holy God dwells with the contrite, heals, and speaks peace to the far and near.

The chapter's harsh imagery should be read as imagery in its context. The sexual language is a prophetic metaphor for covenant unfaithfulness, used throughout the prophets (Hosea 2; Jeremiah 3; Ezekiel 16). It is not a literal description of anyone's sexual conduct, and the text gives no basis for reconstructing specific rituals. The child sacrifice it describes was a real practice condemned elsewhere in scripture (2 Kings 23:10; Jeremiah 7:31). The chapter's condemnation of it is a defense of children, not permission for any cruelty.

The chapter ends with the refrain "There is no peace, saith my God, to the wicked," which also closed 48:22. This marks a section boundary.

### Isaiah 57:1–2 — The righteous perish, and no one notices

> "The righteous perisheth, and no man layeth it to heart: and merciful men are taken away, none considering that the righteous is taken away from the evil to come." (57:1)

**Verse 1** laments righteous and merciful people dying while no one pays attention. The community does not lay it to heart. In 53:3–4 the "we" failed to understand the servant's suffering. Here the community fails to notice the death of the righteous.

The final clause, "the righteous is taken away from the evil to come," suggests one possibility: the righteous may be spared coming trouble by dying before it arrives. This is offered as something the community fails to consider, not as an explanation of every righteous person's death. It should not be used to tell grieving people why their loved one died. Scripture does not claim that every death has a discernible reason, and this verse does not claim that either.

**Verse 2**: "He shall enter into peace: they shall rest in their beds, each one walking in his uprightness." The righteous dead enter peace. "Rest in their beds" is a euphemism for the grave as a place of rest. "Each one walking in his uprightness" describes the lives they lived. The verse offers comfort without claiming to explain.

The contrast with the chapter's end is deliberate. The righteous "enter into peace" (verse 2). The wicked have "no peace" (verse 21).

### Isaiah 57:3–5 — Children of the sorceress: a sharp indictment

**Verse 3**: "But draw near hither, ye sons of the sorceress, the seed of the adulterer and the whore." The tone changes abruptly. The addressees are called to court, as it were, with insulting names. "Sons of the sorceress" and "seed of the adulterer" are not statements about anyone's literal ancestry or ethnicity. They describe conduct. Those who practice sorcery and covenant unfaithfulness are, metaphorically, children of those practices. Jesus uses similar language in John 8:39–44.

**Verse 4**: "Against whom do ye sport yourselves? against whom make ye a wide mouth, and draw out the tongue? are ye not children of transgression, a seed of falsehood." The addressees mock someone, making faces and sticking out their tongues. The text asks whom they are mocking, implying that their contempt is aimed at God or at the righteous. "Children of transgression, a seed of falsehood" again describes conduct.

**Verse 5**: "Enflaming yourselves with idols under every green tree, slaying the children in the valleys under the clifts of the rocks." Two practices are named. "Enflaming yourselves with idols under every green tree" refers to fertility worship at outdoor shrines, a practice condemned throughout the prophets (Jeremiah 2:20; Hosea 4:13). "Slaying the children in the valleys" refers to child sacrifice. 2 Kings 23:10 describes Josiah destroying Topheth in the valley of Hinnom, where children had been burned. Jeremiah 7:31 condemns the same practice.

The condemnation is fierce because the practice is horrific. The text defends the most vulnerable, children killed in the name of religion. Its harshness is aimed at those who harm them.

### Isaiah 57:6–8 — Smooth stones, high beds, and hidden memorials

**Verse 6**: "Among the smooth stones of the stream is thy portion; they, they are thy lot: even to them hast thou poured a drink offering, thou hast offered a meat offering. Should I receive comfort in these?" The "smooth stones of the stream" were probably objects of worship in a valley. The verse plays on the words "portion" and "lot," which are covenant terms for one's inheritance. In Psalm 16:5, "The LORD is the portion of mine inheritance." Here the people's portion is stones. They have poured out offerings to them. God asks whether he should be satisfied with this.

**Verse 7**: "Upon a lofty and high mountain hast thou set thy bed: even thither wentest thou up to offer sacrifice." The "high mountain" probably refers to a hilltop shrine (a "high place"). The "bed" is a sexual metaphor for unfaithful worship. The phrase "lofty and high" is ironic, since it closely resembles the description of God as "high and lofty" in verse 15. The people climbed high to worship other gods. The truly high God, verse 15 will say, dwells with the lowly.

**Verse 8**: "Behind the doors also and the posts hast thou set up thy remembrance: for thou hast discovered thyself to another than me, and art gone up; thou hast enlarged thy bed, and made thee a covenant with them; thou lovedst their bed where thou sawest it." "Thy remembrance" is a memorial or sign placed behind the doors and doorposts. Deuteronomy 6:9 commanded Israel to write God's words on the doorposts. This verse may be ironic: something other than God's words has been hidden behind the doors. That is one reasonable reading. The precise object is unclear.

The rest of the verse continues the marriage metaphor. The people have exposed themselves to "another than me," enlarged their bed, and made a covenant with other partners. The language depicts covenant betrayal as marital infidelity, as in Ezekiel 16 and Hosea 2. It should not be read as a description of specific sexual rituals. The text does not provide enough detail to reconstruct them, and doing so would go beyond what it says.

### Isaiah 57:9–10 — Gifts to the king, messengers far off, exhaustion without despair

**Verse 9**: "And thou wentest to the king with ointment, and didst increase thy perfumes, and didst send thy messengers far off, and didst debase thyself even unto hell."

The KJV reads "the king." The Hebrew consonants for "king" are the same as those for "Molech," a deity associated with child sacrifice (Leviticus 18:21; 2 Kings 23:10). Since ancient Hebrew was written without vowels, the identification depends on later vowel markings or interpretation. Parry's chapter page reads Molech here ([Parry, Isaiah 57](https://scripturecentral.org/archive/books/book-chapter/isaiah-57)), which is a defensible choice, but it is a choice, not an uncontested fact. The verse may refer to a foreign human king, to Molech, or deliberately to both.

Either way, the verse describes seeking help from something other than God. Ointment and perfumes are gifts, perhaps tribute. "Messengers far off" are envoys sent to foreign powers for alliances. Isaiah repeatedly condemned seeking help from Egypt and Assyria instead of from God (30:1–5; 31:1). "Debase thyself even unto hell" uses "hell" for the Hebrew Sheol, the realm of the dead. The people have sunk as low as one can go.

**Verse 10**: "Thou art wearied in the greatness of thy way; yet saidst thou not, There is no hope: thou hast found the life of thine hand; therefore thou wast not grieved." The people are exhausted from their many efforts. Yet they did not say, "There is no hope." They found enough renewed strength, "the life of thine hand," to keep going, so they never stopped to grieve.

This verse has a psychological subtlety. Sometimes a person must reach the end of their own strength before they turn elsewhere. The people here kept finding just enough strength to continue their futile path. They never reached the point of admitting it wasn't working. Compare 50:11's self-kindled fire, which also gives just enough light to continue in the wrong direction.

### Isaiah 57:11–13a — Fearing others, forgetting God, collections that blow away

**Verse 11**: "And of whom hast thou been afraid or feared, that thou hast lied, and hast not remembered me, nor laid it to thy heart? have not I held my peace even of old, and thou fearest me not?" The question is about misplaced fear. Afraid of whom, the people lied and forgot God. The phrase "nor laid it to thy heart" echoes verse 1's "no man layeth it to heart." The community neither notices righteous deaths nor remembers God.

The second question is poignant: "have not I held my peace even of old, and thou fearest me not?" God has been silent, and his silence was taken as absence or weakness. Because God did not act immediately, the people stopped fearing him. Psalm 50:21 makes a similar point: "These things hast thou done, and I kept silence; thou thoughtest that I was altogether such an one as thyself."

**Verse 12**: "I will declare thy righteousness, and thy works; for they shall not profit thee." God's tone is ironic. He will expose the people's so-called righteousness and works, which will not help them.

**Verse 13a**: "When thou criest, let thy companies deliver thee; but the wind shall carry them all away; vanity shall take them." "Thy companies" are probably the collected idols or the assembled alliances. When trouble comes, they will be blown away by wind. "Vanity" means a breath, emptiness. Whatever the people trusted instead of God has no substance.

### Isaiah 57:13b–14 — Trust, inheritance, and a cleared road

**Verse 13b**: "But he that putteth his trust in me shall possess the land, and shall inherit my holy mountain." The contrast is sharp. Idols blow away, but the one who trusts God inherits land and the holy mountain. The "holy mountain" connects with 56:7, where foreigners are brought to it. Inheritance depends on trust, not lineage.

**Verse 14**: "And shall say, Cast ye up, cast ye up, prepare the way, take up the stumblingblock out of the way of my people." The command to build a road echoes 40:3–4, which opened the section with "Prepare ye the way of the LORD." There the road was for God coming to his people. Here it is a road for God's people, with stumbling blocks removed. The image may describe the road home from exile or, more broadly, the removal of whatever prevents return to God.

As a devotional application, the command is communal: "cast ye up," "prepare," "take up," all plural. Removing obstacles from others' path is part of the work.

### Isaiah 57:15–16 — The high and holy God dwells with the contrite

> "For thus saith the high and lofty One that inhabiteth eternity, whose name is Holy; I dwell in the high and holy place, with him also that is of a contrite and humble spirit, to revive the spirit of the humble, and to revive the heart of the contrite ones." (57:15)

**Verse 15** is the hinge of the chapter. God is described with the highest language in Isaiah: "high and lofty," "inhabiteth eternity," "whose name is Holy." This recalls 6:1, where Isaiah saw the Lord "high and lifted up," and 52:13, where the servant would be "exalted and extolled, and be very high."

Then comes the surprise: God dwells "in the high and holy place, with him also that is of a contrite and humble spirit." God has two addresses: the highest heaven and the crushed human heart. "Contrite" means crushed or broken. The same root appears in 53:5, where the servant was "bruised." God dwells with those who have been crushed.

The purpose is revival: "to revive the spirit of the humble, and to revive the heart of the contrite ones." The verb is repeated. God does not just visit the crushed. He restores life to them.

Psalm 34:18 says, "The LORD is nigh unto them that are of a broken heart; and saveth such as be of a contrite spirit." In 3 Nephi 9:20, the risen Christ asks for "a broken heart and a contrite spirit" as the offering that replaces animal sacrifice. The posture of verse 15 becomes, in the Book of Mormon, the central offering of discipleship.

The verse contrasts with verse 7. The people climbed a "lofty and high mountain" to worship false gods. The truly high and lofty One is found with the lowly.

**Verse 16**: "For I will not contend for ever, neither will I be always wroth: for the spirit should fail before me, and the souls which I have made." God limits his own anger. The reason is human fragility. If God contended forever, the human spirit would fail. "The souls which I have made" grounds God's restraint in his role as Creator. He knows how fragile his creatures are. Psalm 103:9, 14 makes the same point: "He will not always chide ... For he knoweth our frame; he remembereth that we are dust."

### Isaiah 57:17–19 — Covetousness, healing, and peace to the far and near

**Verse 17**: "For the iniquity of his covetousness was I wroth, and smote him: I hid me, and was wroth, and he went on frowardly in the way of his heart." The sin named is covetousness, greed. God responded in anger and hid himself, but the person continued "frowardly," stubbornly, "in the way of his heart." The judgment did not produce repentance.

The verse is honest that wrongdoing has consequences, and that consequences alone did not change the person.

**Verse 18**: "I have seen his ways, and will heal him: I will lead him also, and restore comforts unto him and to his mourners." This is one of the most surprising turns in Isaiah. "I have seen his ways," the stubborn, covetous ways of verse 17, "and will heal him." The healing is not a reward for repentance. It comes after God has seen the person's continued stubbornness.

God will "lead him also," providing direction. He will "restore comforts unto him and to his mourners." The word "comforts" returns to the theme of 40:1. "His mourners" may refer to those who grieve over the person's condition, or to those within the community who mourn. Comfort reaches them too.

The verse does not cancel accountability. Verse 17 named the sin. Verse 18 shows that God's final word to the sinner is healing. Grace and covenant accountability stand together.

**Verse 19**: "I create the fruit of the lips; Peace, peace to him that is far off, and to him that is near, saith the LORD; and I will heal him." "I create the fruit of the lips" may mean God creates praise on people's lips, or God creates the word of peace that is spoken. "Peace, peace," doubled for emphasis, is spoken to the far and the near. In context, "far" may mean exiles still scattered, and "near" those already in the land. The peace reaches both.

Ephesians 2:17 applies this verse to Christ: "And came and preached peace to you which were afar off, and to them that were nigh." Paul identifies the far as Gentiles and the near as Jews. Christ's peace reconciles both into one body. Hebrews 13:15 speaks of offering "the fruit of our lips giving thanks to his name," which resonates with this verse's phrase.

### Isaiah 57:20–21 — The restless sea

> "But the wicked are like the troubled sea, when it cannot rest, whose waters cast up mire and dirt. There is no peace, saith my God, to the wicked."

**Verse 20** uses the image of a storm-tossed sea that churns up mud. The "wicked" here are those who persist in the conduct the chapter has described: oppression, idolatry, child sacrifice, greed, refusal to turn. Their restlessness is the opposite of the righteous person's peace in verse 2.

**Verse 21** completes the refrain: "There is no peace, saith my God, to the wicked." The same words close 48:22, marking the end of a section.

This contrast is covenantal and ethical. It describes the inner and outer turmoil that accompanies a life organized around harm and self-interest. It must not be used to diagnose anxiety, depression, or any mental illness as wickedness. Faithful people experience restlessness, distress, and psychological suffering, and people with clinical conditions are not the "wicked" of this verse. Scripture is full of righteous people in turmoil: Job, Jeremiah, the psalmists, and the servant of chapter 53. The servant was "a man of sorrows, and acquainted with grief," and was not wicked.

The verse also follows directly after verse 19, which offered "Peace, peace to him that is far off." The far off include the wicked of verse 17, whom God has promised to heal. "No peace to the wicked" is not a permanent sentence. It describes the condition of those who refuse the peace God offers.

### Christ in Isaiah 57

The chapter's connections to Christ rest on:

- Ephesians 2:17, where Paul applies verse 19's "peace to him that is far off, and to him that is near" to Christ's preaching and reconciling work;
- 3 Nephi 9:20, where the risen Christ asks for the "broken heart and a contrite spirit" that verse 15 describes as God's dwelling place;
- the verbal link between the "contrite" (crushed) of verse 15 and the servant "bruised" (crushed) in 53:5, which suggests that God dwells with the crushed because the servant himself was crushed;
- the contrast between the righteous who perish unnoticed (verses 1–2) and the servant whom no one considered (53:3, 8).

### Questions for study

1. Verse 1 laments righteous deaths that no one notices. What does it mean to "lay it to heart"? Why might the text be cautious about explaining these deaths?
2. Verses 3–8 use harsh metaphors. What do the metaphors communicate about covenant unfaithfulness? What would be lost by reading them literally?
3. Verse 10 describes people who never said "There is no hope." How can that be a problem rather than a strength in context?
4. Verse 11 says God's silence was misread. How do you understand the relationship between divine silence and divine presence?
5. Verse 15 gives God two dwelling places. What does it mean that the "high and lofty One" dwells with the contrite?
6. Verse 18 promises healing after describing stubbornness. How does the chapter hold together accountability and grace?

### Cross-references

- 2 Kings 23:10 and Jeremiah 7:31 condemn the child sacrifice described in verse 5.
- Hosea 2 and Ezekiel 16 use the same marriage metaphor for covenant unfaithfulness as verses 6–8.
- Deuteronomy 6:9 commands words on doorposts, illuminating the possible irony of verse 8.
- Leviticus 18:21 names Molech, relevant to the translation choice at verse 9.
- Psalm 50:21 shows divine silence misread as indifference, as in verse 11.
- Isaiah 40:3–4 opens the section with the prepared way that verse 14 echoes.
- Psalm 34:18 and 3 Nephi 9:20 describe God's nearness to the broken and contrite, as in verse 15.
- Psalm 103:9, 14 grounds divine restraint in human fragility, as verse 16 does.
- Ephesians 2:17 applies verse 19's peace to far and near to Christ's reconciling work.
- Isaiah 48:22 contains the same refrain as verse 21, marking a section boundary.

## Synthesis

These eight chapters move from a courtroom to a free feast, from a barren mother to a welcomed eunuch, from the servant's flint-set face to God dwelling with the crushed.

Several threads run through them:

- The question of abandonment raised in 49:14 is answered from many angles. There is no final divorce decree (50:1). The servant is vindicated (52:13). Zion is restored in a covenant that will not be removed (54:10). The outsider receives an everlasting name (56:5).
- The servant's experience becomes the pattern for the community. The servant listens, suffers, and is vindicated (50:4–9; 52:13–53:12). His followers become "servants" (54:17; 56:6) who share his vindication.
- Suffering is never treated as proof of guilt. The speakers of 53:4 confess that they were wrong to assume the servant was "smitten of God." The righteous of 57:1 die without explanation, and the text laments rather than explains.
- Grace and accountability stand together. Sin is named throughout, but God's last word is consistently healing, pardon, and comfort (55:7; 57:18–19).
- The circle keeps widening. The barren woman's tent expands (54:2). Nations run to Israel (55:5). Foreigners and eunuchs are brought to the holy mountain (56:7). God gathers "others" beyond those already gathered (56:8).

Christian and Latter-day Saint readers find the center of these chapters in the servant of 52:13–53:12, whom the New Testament and the Book of Mormon identify as Jesus Christ. That identification rests on explicit later scripture, including Jesus's own words in Luke 22:37. It does not require erasing the poem's ancient setting or dismissing Jewish readings. It sees in one innocent life the fullest expression of a pattern Israel had long known: the one who bears others' griefs, is misjudged, dies, and is vindicated.

The chapters do not promise that faith prevents suffering. They promise that suffering does not have the last word, that the high and holy God is found with the crushed, and that a covenant of peace stands firmer than the mountains.
