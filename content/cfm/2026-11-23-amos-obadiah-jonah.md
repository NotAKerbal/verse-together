# Seek the Lord, and Ye Shall Live

This guide covers the *Come, Follow Me* reading for **November 23–29, 2026**: Amos, Obadiah, and Jonah ([official lesson](https://www.churchofjesuschrist.org/study/manual/come-follow-me-for-home-and-church-old-testament-2026/48?lang=eng)). It is a personal Latter-day Saint study guide, not an official publication of The Church of Jesus Christ of Latter-day Saints. Scripture quotations come from the King James Version and the other standard works of the Church. The guide covers all 215 assigned verses in order, in small labeled groups: the 146 verses of Amos 1–9, the 21 verses of Obadiah, and the 48 verses of Jonah 1–4. Each chapter opens with a short note on its context. Each verse group gets an explanation, cross-references, labeled Christ connections where they fit, and a question for personal study or discussion. A day-by-day study plan and a class outline come near the end, before the list of sources.

## Historical and Literary Context

**Three prophets, three settings.** These three short books come from different times and speak to different audiences, but they share one concern: how God deals with nations, including nations that are not Israel.

- **Amos** spoke to the northern kingdom of Israel in a time of wealth and confidence.
- **Obadiah** spoke against Edom, Judah's neighbor and kin, after Edom joined in Jerusalem's ruin.
- **Jonah** is a story about a prophet sent to Nineveh, a great city of Assyria, the empire that would later destroy the northern kingdom.

**Amos: a shepherd from Judah in the north.** The book opens with "The words of Amos, who was among the herdmen of Tekoa" ([Amos 1:1](https://www.churchofjesuschrist.org/study/scriptures/ot/amos/1?lang=eng&id=p1#p1)). The Bible Dictionary places Tekoa 12 miles south of Jerusalem and says that although Amos was a shepherd from Judah, his ministry was in the northern kingdom ([Bible Dictionary, "Amos"](https://www.churchofjesuschrist.org/study/scriptures/bd/amos?lang=eng)). He prophesied "in the days of Uzziah king of Judah, and in the days of Jeroboam the son of Joash king of Israel," that is, Jeroboam II. The Bible Dictionary dates Jeroboam II's death to about 750 B.C. and Uzziah's to about 740 B.C. *Scripture Helps* places Amos in the eighth century B.C. and notes that Israel was conquered by Assyria in 722 B.C. ([Scripture Helps: Amos; Obadiah; Jonah](https://www.churchofjesuschrist.org/study/manual/scripture-helps-old-testament/46-amos-obadiah-jonah?lang=eng)).

Jeroboam II was a strong king. He "restored the coast of Israel from the entering of Hamath unto the sea of the plain" ([2 Kings 14:25](https://www.churchofjesuschrist.org/study/scriptures/ot/2-kgs/14?lang=eng&id=p25#p25)). Israel was rich and secure. Amos describes winter houses and summer houses, "houses of ivory" ([Amos 3:15](https://www.churchofjesuschrist.org/study/scriptures/ot/amos/3?lang=eng&id=p15#p15)), beds of ivory, choice lambs and calves, and wine drunk "in bowls" ([Amos 6:4–6](https://www.churchofjesuschrist.org/study/scriptures/ot/amos/6?lang=eng&id=p4-p6#p4)). He also describes who paid for that comfort: the poor, who were sold "for a pair of shoes" ([Amos 2:6](https://www.churchofjesuschrist.org/study/scriptures/ot/amos/2?lang=eng&id=p6#p6)), cheated with false weights ([Amos 8:5](https://www.churchofjesuschrist.org/study/scriptures/ot/amos/8?lang=eng&id=p5#p5)), and turned away from justice "in the gate" ([Amos 5:12](https://www.churchofjesuschrist.org/study/scriptures/ot/amos/5?lang=eng&id=p12#p12)). Worship was busy at the royal sanctuary at Bethel and at Gilgal ([Amos 4:4–5](https://www.churchofjesuschrist.org/study/scriptures/ot/amos/4?lang=eng&id=p4-p5#p4)), but the Lord refused it ([Amos 5:21–24](https://www.churchofjesuschrist.org/study/scriptures/ot/amos/5?lang=eng&id=p21-p24#p21)). The Bible Dictionary sums up the book's message: Jehovah is the righteous ruler of all nations, and the offering he most wants is a righteous life. Sacrifices lose their meaning when they are offered as a substitute for personal righteousness.

**How Amos is arranged.** *Scripture Helps* outlines the book in three parts: judgments on the nations, ending with Judah and Israel (chapters 1–2); warnings and a call to repent (chapters 3–6); and visions of judgment followed by a promise of future restoration (chapters 7–9). The only story told about Amos himself is his confrontation with Amaziah, the priest of Bethel ([Amos 7:10–17](https://www.churchofjesuschrist.org/study/scriptures/ot/amos/7?lang=eng&id=p10-p17#p10)).

**Obadiah: the shortest book.** Obadiah is the shortest book in the Old Testament, a single chapter of 21 verses. Nothing is known of the prophet's life. The Bible Dictionary says the prophecy was given just after some capture of Jerusalem, during which the Edomites showed hostility to Judah. It names two possibilities: a capture by the Philistines and Arabians in the reign of Jehoram (848–844 B.C.), or, more probably, the Babylonian (Chaldean) capture of 586 B.C. ([Bible Dictionary, "Obadiah"](https://www.churchofjesuschrist.org/study/scriptures/bd/obadiah?lang=eng)). *Scripture Helps* also says the book was probably written after the Babylonians captured Jerusalem in 586 B.C. A psalm remembers the Edomites' cry that day: "Rase it, rase it, even to the foundation thereof" ([Psalm 137:7](https://www.churchofjesuschrist.org/study/scriptures/ot/ps/137?lang=eng&id=p7#p7)).

Edom was the nation descended from Esau, Jacob's twin brother ([Genesis 36:1](https://www.churchofjesuschrist.org/study/scriptures/ot/gen/36?lang=eng&id=p1#p1)). Its land lay south of the Dead Sea in Mount Seir ([Bible Dictionary, "Edom"](https://www.churchofjesuschrist.org/study/scriptures/bd/edom?lang=eng)). Israel and Edom were kin, but their history was full of war. Scripture Central's KnoWhy #877 argues that this kinship explains the "raw pain" of Obadiah: betrayal by a brother hurts more than an attack by a stranger ([Scripture Central, "Why Was Obadiah So Upset with the Edomites?"](https://scripturecentral.org/knowhy/why-was-obadiah-so-upset-with-the-edomites)).

**Jonah: a prophet's story.** Unlike Amos and Obadiah, the book of Jonah is mostly a narrative about the prophet, not a collection of his sermons. Jonah son of Amittai was from Gath-hepher in Zebulun. He lived under Jeroboam II and is named in 2 Kings as the prophet who foretold that king's success in restoring Israel's borders ([2 Kings 14:25](https://www.churchofjesuschrist.org/study/scriptures/ot/2-kgs/14?lang=eng&id=p25#p25)). So Jonah and Amos were near contemporaries. The 2026 Institute manual notes that Amos and Hosea also prophesied to the northern kingdom in Jeroboam II's reign ([Old Testament Institute Teacher Manual, "Amos; Obadiah; Jonah"](https://www.churchofjesuschrist.org/study/manual/old-testament-institute-teacher-manual-2026/46-amos-obadiah-jonah?lang=eng)).

The Bible Dictionary observes that the book does not claim to come from Jonah's own hand. It tells an episode in his life and is the work of some later writer ([Bible Dictionary, "Jonah"](https://www.churchofjesuschrist.org/study/scriptures/bd/jonah?lang=eng)). The same entry says the key to the book is in [Jonah 3:10](https://www.churchofjesuschrist.org/study/scriptures/ot/jonah/3?lang=eng&id=p10#p10) through [Jonah 4:11](https://www.churchofjesuschrist.org/study/scriptures/ot/jonah/4?lang=eng&id=p11#p11), where Jonah explains why he fled. It says the book opposes a narrowmindedness that would confine God's love to one nation. It also notes that Jesus twice gave "the sign of the prophet Jonas" and that the event foreshadowed his own death and resurrection ([Matthew 12:39–41](https://www.churchofjesuschrist.org/study/scriptures/nt/matt/12?lang=eng&id=p39-p41#p39); [Matthew 16:4](https://www.churchofjesuschrist.org/study/scriptures/nt/matt/16?lang=eng&id=p4#p4); [Luke 11:29–30](https://www.churchofjesuschrist.org/study/scriptures/nt/luke/11?lang=eng&id=p29-p30#p29)).

**Nineveh and Assyria.** Nineveh stood on the east bank of the Tigris. It later became the capital of Assyria under Sennacherib, and was for centuries a great commercial center ([Bible Dictionary, "Nineveh"](https://www.churchofjesuschrist.org/study/scriptures/bd/nineveh?lang=eng)). The sources differ on when the city fell. The Bible Dictionary dates Nineveh's fall to 606 B.C., at the downfall of the Assyrian empire. A Babylonian chronicle records that the Babylonians and Medes besieged Nineveh and turned it into "a ruin heap" in the fourteenth year of the Babylonian king Nabopolassar. The Livius edition converts that year to 612–611 B.C. and dates the city's fall to 612 B.C. The same chronicle records that the Babylonians later captured Harran, where an Assyrian king had taken the throne (610–609 B.C. in Livius's conversion), and that an attempt to retake Harran failed (609 B.C.). (["ABC 3 (Fall of Nineveh Chronicle)," Livius](https://www.livius.org/sources/content/mesopotamian-chronicles-content/abc-3-fall-of-nineveh-chronicle)). The official lesson describes Nineveh as an enemy of Israel known for its violence and cruelty. Assyria later "took Samaria, and carried Israel away into Assyria" ([2 Kings 17:6](https://www.churchofjesuschrist.org/study/scriptures/ot/2-kgs/17?lang=eng&id=p6#p6)). Readers who know that history can feel the weight of Jonah's assignment. In an essay in *Interpreter*, Duane Boyce cautions readers who are quick to condemn Jonah's flight to remember how brutally Assyria treated its enemies. He writes that "this is the culture Jonah was commanded to visit and openly condemn" ([Boyce, "A Lengthening Shadow," Part Three](https://journal.interpreterfoundation.org/a-lengthening-shadow-is-quality-of-thought-deteriorating-in-lds-scholarly-discourse-regarding-prophets-and-revelation-part-three/), 97–98).

**Did the brass plates include these books?** Nephi's brass plates contained "the prophecies of the holy prophets" down to the reign of Zedekiah, including prophecies of Jeremiah ([1 Nephi 5:13](https://www.churchofjesuschrist.org/study/scriptures/bofm/1-ne/5?lang=eng&id=p13#p13)). A. Keith Thompson, writing in *Interpreter*, considers which Old Testament books they may have held. In his table he judges that the brass plates "probably not" included Amos, did not include Obadiah (likely written after Lehi left Jerusalem), and "probably not" Jonah, explaining that Jonah did not prophesy to Israel. (The book of Jonah tells of his mission to Nineveh, though 2 Kings records a prophecy of his about Israel's borders, [2 Kings 14:25](https://www.churchofjesuschrist.org/study/scriptures/ot/2-kgs/14?lang=eng&id=p25#p25).) Thompson notes that the Book of Mormon never quotes Amos directly, though a later Nephi named his son Amos ([4 Nephi 1:19](https://www.churchofjesuschrist.org/study/scriptures/bofm/4-ne/1?lang=eng&id=p19#p19)) ([Thompson, "The Brass Plates," *Interpreter* 45](https://cdn.interpreterfoundation.org/jnlpdf/thompson-v45-2021-pp81-114-PDF.pdf), 106–107 and n. 65). These are one scholar's judgments, not settled facts.

**Joseph Smith Translation notes.** Two kinds of passages in these books raise the question of whether God "repents." The Joseph Smith Translation (JST) changes several of them. In [Amos 7:3](https://www.churchofjesuschrist.org/study/scriptures/ot/amos/7?lang=eng&id=p3#p3) and [Amos 7:6](https://www.churchofjesuschrist.org/study/scriptures/ot/amos/7?lang=eng&id=p6#p6), where the KJV says "The Lord repented for this," the JST footnotes read that the Lord said "concerning Jacob, Jacob shall repent," and therefore he would not utterly destroy him. The JST also revises [Jonah 3:9–10](https://www.churchofjesuschrist.org/study/scriptures/ot/jonah/3?lang=eng&id=p9-p10#p9), and it changes "done" to "known" in [Amos 3:6](https://www.churchofjesuschrist.org/study/scriptures/ot/amos/3?lang=eng&id=p6#p6) and "but" to "until" in [Amos 3:7](https://www.churchofjesuschrist.org/study/scriptures/ot/amos/3?lang=eng&id=p7#p7). The guide notes these changes where they appear.

**Reading in layers.** This guide tries to keep four kinds of statement apart: what the ancient text says; what official Church sources teach about it; what individual scholars and teachers propose; and devotional applications, including readings that see Jesus Christ in a passage. These layers can support one another, but they are not the same, and the guide labels them as it goes.

## Significant Insights

1. **Seek the Lord, and live.** The lesson's title comes from Amos: "Seek ye me, and ye shall live" ([Amos 5:4](https://www.churchofjesuschrist.org/study/scriptures/ot/amos/5?lang=eng&id=p4#p4)), "Seek the Lord, and ye shall live" ([Amos 5:6](https://www.churchofjesuschrist.org/study/scriptures/ot/amos/5?lang=eng&id=p6#p6)), and "Seek good, and not evil, that ye may live" ([Amos 5:14](https://www.churchofjesuschrist.org/study/scriptures/ot/amos/5?lang=eng&id=p14#p14)). In Amos, seeking the Lord does not mean going to more shrines. It means turning to him and doing justice. Jesus Christ said he came "that they might have life" ([John 10:10](https://www.churchofjesuschrist.org/study/scriptures/nt/john/10?lang=eng&id=p10#p10)).

2. **God reveals his purposes through prophets.** "Surely the Lord God will do nothing, but he revealeth his secret unto his servants the prophets" ([Amos 3:7](https://www.churchofjesuschrist.org/study/scriptures/ot/amos/3?lang=eng&id=p7#p7)). The official lesson says that among the most precious of the "secrets" God reveals is that it is not too late to change. Elder Ulisses Soares, quoted there, taught that "having prophets is a sign of God's love." Amos also shows that prophets are often unwelcome: Israel told them, "Prophesy not" ([Amos 2:12](https://www.churchofjesuschrist.org/study/scriptures/ot/amos/2?lang=eng&id=p12#p12)).

3. **Worship and justice belong together.** "I hate, I despise your feast days ... But let judgment run down as waters, and righteousness as a mighty stream" ([Amos 5:21–24](https://www.churchofjesuschrist.org/study/scriptures/ot/amos/5?lang=eng&id=p21-p24#p21)). Amos does not reject worship. He rejects worship by people who crush the poor. Elsewhere the Lord says he desires "mercy, and not sacrifice" ([Hosea 6:6](https://www.churchofjesuschrist.org/study/scriptures/ot/hosea/6?lang=eng&id=p6#p6); [Matthew 12:7](https://www.churchofjesuschrist.org/study/scriptures/nt/matt/12?lang=eng&id=p7#p7)).

4. **Comfort can deafen us.** "Woe to them that are at ease in Zion" ([Amos 6:1](https://www.churchofjesuschrist.org/study/scriptures/ot/amos/6?lang=eng&id=p1#p1)). The people Amos condemned were "not grieved for the affliction of Joseph" ([Amos 6:6](https://www.churchofjesuschrist.org/study/scriptures/ot/amos/6?lang=eng&id=p6#p6)). The Book of Mormon repeats the warning to a latter-day people who say "All is well in Zion" ([2 Nephi 28:21–24](https://www.churchofjesuschrist.org/study/scriptures/bofm/2-ne/28?lang=eng&id=p21-p24#p21)).

5. **Without the word of God, souls starve.** Amos foresaw "a famine ... of hearing the words of the Lord" ([Amos 8:11–12](https://www.churchofjesuschrist.org/study/scriptures/ot/amos/8?lang=eng&id=p11-p12#p11)). Church sources see fulfillments in the centuries after Malachi and in the Great Apostasy, and they see the Restoration as the end of that famine. Jesus said, "I am the bread of life" ([John 6:35](https://www.churchofjesuschrist.org/study/scriptures/nt/john/6?lang=eng&id=p35#p35)).

6. **God is merciful, even when we would rather he were not.** Jonah fled because he knew God was "gracious ... and merciful, slow to anger, and of great kindness" ([Jonah 4:2](https://www.churchofjesuschrist.org/study/scriptures/ot/jonah/4?lang=eng&id=p2#p2)). When Nineveh repented, God spared it, and Jonah was angry. The book ends with God's question: "Should not I spare Nineveh, that great city?" ([Jonah 4:11](https://www.churchofjesuschrist.org/study/scriptures/ot/jonah/4?lang=eng&id=p11#p11)). The official lesson's title for this section states the theme: the Lord is merciful to all who turn to him.

7. **Betraying a brother is serious.** Obadiah condemns Edom for standing by, gloating, looting, and handing over refugees when Jerusalem fell ([Obadiah 1:10–14](https://www.churchofjesuschrist.org/study/scriptures/ot/obad/1?lang=eng&id=p10-p14#p10)). The sin of the bystander is still a sin. "As thou hast done, it shall be done unto thee" ([Obadiah 1:15](https://www.churchofjesuschrist.org/study/scriptures/ot/obad/1?lang=eng&id=p15#p15)).

8. **Saviors on Mount Zion.** Obadiah ends with "saviours" coming up on Mount Zion ([Obadiah 1:21](https://www.churchofjesuschrist.org/study/scriptures/ot/obad/1?lang=eng&id=p21#p21)). The Bible Dictionary and Latter-day Saint leaders connect this verse to the work of salvation for the dead, in which the living receive ordinances in temples on behalf of the dead.

9. **The sign of Jonah points to Jesus Christ.** Jesus said that "as Jonas was three days and three nights in the whale's belly; so shall the Son of man be three days and three nights in the heart of the earth" ([Matthew 12:40](https://www.churchofjesuschrist.org/study/scriptures/nt/matt/12?lang=eng&id=p40#p40)). Jonah went down into the deep and was brought up alive. Christ went down into death and rose. "Behold, a greater than Jonas is here" ([Matthew 12:41](https://www.churchofjesuschrist.org/study/scriptures/nt/matt/12?lang=eng&id=p41#p41)).

## Amos 1: The Lord Will Roar from Zion

Chapter 1 opens Amos's first great speech. After a short introduction (verses 1–2), the Lord pronounces judgment on five of Israel's neighbors: Syria (Damascus), the Philistines (Gaza), Tyre, Edom, and Ammon. Each oracle follows the same pattern. Israel's first hearers may have cheered as the judgments fell on their enemies, not yet knowing where the speech would end.

### Amos 1:1 — "Two Years Before the Earthquake"

"The words of Amos, who was among the herdmen of Tekoa, which he saw concerning Israel." The word "saw" is worth noticing. Amos's words came by seeing, by revelation, and later chapters describe five visions (7:1–9; 8:1–3; 9:1–4).

Amos was "among the herdmen." He later told the priest at Bethel, "I was no prophet, neither was I a prophet's son; but I was an herdman, and a gatherer of sycomore fruit" ([Amos 7:14](https://www.churchofjesuschrist.org/study/scriptures/ot/amos/7?lang=eng&id=p14#p14)). By his own account he was not a prophet or a prophet's son before the Lord called him. One common reading is that he did not belong to a group of professional prophets. The Lord called a working man from the south to preach to the north.

The date is unusual: "two years before the earthquake." The earthquake must have been well known, since the book expects its readers to know which one it means. Centuries later Zechariah still remembered it: "ye fled from before the earthquake in the days of Uzziah king of Judah" ([Zechariah 14:5](https://www.churchofjesuschrist.org/study/scriptures/ot/zech/14?lang=eng&id=p5#p5)). Amos's book later speaks of the Lord touching the land "and it shall melt" ([Amos 9:5](https://www.churchofjesuschrist.org/study/scriptures/ot/amos/9?lang=eng&id=p5#p5)) and of the Lord smiting the altar so "that the posts may shake" ([Amos 9:1](https://www.churchofjesuschrist.org/study/scriptures/ot/amos/9?lang=eng&id=p1#p1)). To later readers, the earthquake may have seemed a first sign that Amos's words were true.

**Question:** Amos was a herdsman and a gatherer of sycomore fruit when the Lord called him. What does his call suggest about the kinds of people the Lord can use?

### Amos 1:2 — "The Lord Will Roar from Zion"

"The Lord will roar from Zion, and utter his voice from Jerusalem; and the habitations of the shepherds shall mourn, and the top of Carmel shall wither." The Lord speaks from Jerusalem, the site of his temple, even though Amos's message is mostly for the northern kingdom, whose kings had set up rival sanctuaries at Bethel and Dan ([1 Kings 12:29](https://www.churchofjesuschrist.org/study/scriptures/ot/1-kgs/12?lang=eng&id=p29#p29)).

The roaring lion is a key image in Amos. In chapter 3 Amos argues that a lion roars only when it has prey ([Amos 3:4](https://www.churchofjesuschrist.org/study/scriptures/ot/amos/3?lang=eng&id=p4#p4)), and then asks, "The lion hath roared, who will not fear?" ([Amos 3:8](https://www.churchofjesuschrist.org/study/scriptures/ot/amos/3?lang=eng&id=p8#p8)). Joel, from last week's reading, uses almost the same words: "The Lord also shall roar out of Zion, and utter his voice from Jerusalem" ([Joel 3:16](https://www.churchofjesuschrist.org/study/scriptures/ot/joel/3?lang=eng&id=p16#p16)). In Joel the next words are hopeful: "the Lord will be the hope of his people." In Amos they announce drought. The footnote to "habitations" gives "pastures." When the Lord speaks, the pastures mourn, and even Carmel, the green, wooded ridge on Israel's coast, withers.

**Question:** The Lord's voice is compared to a lion's roar. What might it mean to "hear" that voice in time, before the judgment it warns of arrives?

### Amos 1:3–5 — Damascus: "For Three Transgressions ... and for Four"

"For three transgressions of Damascus, and for four, I will not turn away the punishment thereof." This formula opens each of the eight oracles in chapters 1–2. The pattern of "three ... and four" is a Hebrew way of saying many, and then one more: the measure is full and overflowing. Proverbs uses a similar pattern: "These six things doth the Lord hate: yea, seven are an abomination unto him" ([Proverbs 6:16](https://www.churchofjesuschrist.org/study/scriptures/ot/prov/6?lang=eng&id=p16#p16)). For each of the foreign nations Amos names one representative offense, as if it were the last one that filled the measure. For Israel he names several.

Damascus was the capital of Syria (Aram). Its crime: "they have threshed Gilead with threshing instruments of iron." Gilead was Israelite land east of the Jordan. Threshing sledges were heavy boards studded with iron or stone, dragged over grain. The image describes the crushing of a conquered people. Hazael and Ben-hadad were kings of Syria who fought Israel ([2 Kings 13:3](https://www.churchofjesuschrist.org/study/scriptures/ot/2-kgs/13?lang=eng&id=p3#p3)). When the prophet Elisha foresaw Hazael's reign, he wept, because he knew "the evil that thou wilt do unto the children of Israel" ([2 Kings 8:12](https://www.churchofjesuschrist.org/study/scriptures/ot/2-kgs/8?lang=eng&id=p12#p12)).

The judgment: fire on the royal house, the "bar" (the gate bar of the city) broken, and "the people of Syria shall go into captivity unto Kir." This was fulfilled when "the king of Assyria went up against Damascus, and took it, and carried the people of it captive to Kir" ([2 Kings 16:9](https://www.churchofjesuschrist.org/study/scriptures/ot/2-kgs/16?lang=eng&id=p9#p9)). Later the Lord reminds Israel that he once brought "the Syrians from Kir" ([Amos 9:7](https://www.churchofjesuschrist.org/study/scriptures/ot/amos/9?lang=eng&id=p7#p7)). Syria's exile reverses its history.

**Question:** The Lord holds Syria responsible for cruelty to the people it conquered. What does that teach about how God judges the use of power?

### Amos 1:6–8 — Gaza and the Philistines: Selling Whole Communities

Gaza was one of the five chief Philistine cities on the coast. Its crime: "they carried away captive the whole captivity, to deliver them up to Edom." The phrase seems to mean whole communities, everyone taken without distinction, sold to Edom as slaves. The judgment falls on Gaza, Ashdod, Ashkelon, and Ekron, four of the Philistine cities, until "the remnant of the Philistines shall perish."

The crime is human trafficking for profit. In Joel, last week, the Lord condemned the same thing: "they have ... sold a girl for wine" and sold the children of Judah to the Greeks ([Joel 3:3](https://www.churchofjesuschrist.org/study/scriptures/ot/joel/3?lang=eng&id=p3#p3), [6](https://www.churchofjesuschrist.org/study/scriptures/ot/joel/3?lang=eng&id=p6#p6)). The Lord notices when people are treated as goods.

**Question:** Where in the modern world are people still treated as things to be bought and sold? How can disciples of Christ respond?

### Amos 1:9–10 — Tyre: "The Brotherly Covenant"

Tyre, the great Phoenician port, committed a crime like Gaza's: "they delivered up the whole captivity to Edom." The added charge is that they "remembered not the brotherly covenant." The text does not say which covenant is meant. One possibility is the old alliance between Tyre and Israel. In Solomon's day "there was peace between Hiram and Solomon; and they two made a league together" ([1 Kings 5:12](https://www.churchofjesuschrist.org/study/scriptures/ot/1-kgs/5?lang=eng&id=p12#p12)), and Hiram called Solomon "my brother" ([1 Kings 9:13](https://www.churchofjesuschrist.org/study/scriptures/ot/1-kgs/9?lang=eng&id=p13#p13)). If that is the reference, Tyre's sin was not only cruelty but betrayal of a treaty partner.

**Question:** Why might breaking a covenant of friendship make a wrong worse in God's eyes?

### Amos 1:11–12 — Edom: "He Did Pursue His Brother with the Sword"

Edom's crime is that "he did pursue his brother with the sword, and did cast off all pity, and his anger did tear perpetually, and he kept his wrath for ever." The "brother" is Israel. Edom descended from Esau, and Israel from Jacob. When Moses asked Edom for passage, he began, "Thus saith thy brother Israel" ([Numbers 20:14](https://www.churchofjesuschrist.org/study/scriptures/ot/num/20?lang=eng&id=p14#p14)). Edom refused.

The phrase "cast off all pity" is striking. Edom's hatred did not cool. Ezekiel later condemned Edom in similar terms: "thou hast had a perpetual hatred, and hast shed the blood of the children of Israel" ([Ezekiel 35:5](https://www.churchofjesuschrist.org/study/scriptures/ot/ezek/35?lang=eng&id=p5#p5)). Teman and Bozrah were Edomite cities. This oracle prepares for Obadiah, which is entirely about Edom's hostility to its brother.

**Question:** Edom "kept his wrath for ever." What happens to a person or a people who will not let anger go?

### Amos 1:13–15 — Ammon: Violence Against the Defenseless

The Ammonites, east of the Jordan, committed an atrocity: "they have ripped up the women with child of Gilead, that they might enlarge their border." The motive is named plainly: land. Ammon killed pregnant women and their unborn children to gain territory. The same horror is foreseen in Elisha's grief over Hazael ([2 Kings 8:12](https://www.churchofjesuschrist.org/study/scriptures/ot/2-kgs/8?lang=eng&id=p12#p12)).

These verses are painful to read. They are in scripture because God sees such acts and condemns them. The Lord's judgment falls on Rabbah, Ammon's capital, "with a tempest in the day of the whirlwind," and "their king shall go into captivity, he and his princes together." The nation's leaders would share in its judgment.

**Question:** Each crime in Amos 1 involves cruelty to the weak in war. Why might God hold nations accountable even when they do not know his law?

## Amos 2: Judah and Israel Too

The pattern continues with Moab. Then it turns closer to home: Judah, and finally Israel itself. The oracle against Israel is the longest and the most detailed. Its charges are not war crimes against foreigners but injustice against the poor within Israel, sins committed by God's own covenant people.

### Amos 2:1–3 — Moab: Burning a King's Bones

Moab's crime: "he burned the bones of the king of Edom into lime." This was a crime against Edom, not against Israel. To burn a dead king's bones to powder was to desecrate his remains and deny him rest. The Lord judges it anyway. He cares about wrongs done between nations that have nothing to do with Israel, even wrongs against an enemy of Israel.

Kerioth was a Moabite city. The judgment again strikes the leaders: "I will cut off the judge from the midst thereof, and will slay all the princes thereof."

**Question:** The Lord condemns Moab for desecrating an Edomite king's remains. What does this suggest about God's concern for the dignity of every person, even in death?

### Amos 2:4–5 — Judah: "They Have Despised the Law of the Lord"

Judah's charge is different from those of the nations. Judah is not accused of war crimes but of covenant failure: "they have despised the law of the Lord, and have not kept his commandments, and their lies caused them to err, after the which their fathers have walked." "Lies" here likely means false gods or false teachings, which the fathers had followed before them. The nations were judged for cruelty that conscience should have forbidden. Judah was judged for rejecting the law it had been given. The judgment is the same: fire on "the palaces of Jerusalem."

**Question:** Judah inherited its errors from earlier generations. How can a person or family break a pattern that has been handed down?

### Amos 2:6–8 — Israel: Selling the Poor

Now the speech reaches its target. "For three transgressions of Israel, and for four ... because they sold the righteous for silver, and the poor for a pair of shoes." The "righteous" here are the innocent: people in the right who lost their cases or their freedom for money. The poor were sold into debt slavery for something as small as a pair of sandals. Amos repeats the charge later: "That we may buy the poor for silver, and the needy for a pair of shoes" ([Amos 8:6](https://www.churchofjesuschrist.org/study/scriptures/ot/amos/8?lang=eng&id=p6#p6)).

Verse 7: they "pant after the dust of the earth on the head of the poor, and turn aside the way of the meek." The image seems to be of people so greedy that they covet even the dust on the heads of the poor. "A man and his father will go in unto the same maid, to profane my holy name." The text describes a father and son having sexual relations with the same young woman. It does not say who she was. One possible reading is that she was a servant or otherwise vulnerable and was being exploited. The powerful used the vulnerable, and the Lord counts it as profaning his name.

Verse 8: "they lay themselves down upon clothes laid to pledge by every altar, and they drink the wine of the condemned in the house of their god." The law of Moses said that if a poor man gave his cloak as a pledge for a loan, "thou shalt deliver it unto him by that the sun goeth down: For that is his covering only ... wherein shall he sleep?" ([Exodus 22:26–27](https://www.churchofjesuschrist.org/study/scriptures/ot/ex/22?lang=eng&id=p26-p27#p26); see [Deuteronomy 24:12–13](https://www.churchofjesuschrist.org/study/scriptures/ot/deut/24?lang=eng&id=p12-p13#p12)). Israel's creditors kept the cloaks and lay on them at their feasts beside the altars. They drank wine paid for by fines taken from the poor. Their religion and their oppression happened in the same place.

John Hilton III notes that serving the financially poor is "one consistent theme found in Amos (and throughout scripture)," pointing to this verse and others ([Amos 4:1](https://www.churchofjesuschrist.org/study/scriptures/ot/amos/4?lang=eng&id=p1#p1); [Amos 5:11–12](https://www.churchofjesuschrist.org/study/scriptures/ot/amos/5?lang=eng&id=p11-p12#p11); [Amos 8:4–6](https://www.churchofjesuschrist.org/study/scriptures/ot/amos/8?lang=eng&id=p4-p6#p4)) ([Hilton, "Amos"](https://johnhiltoniii.com/amos/)).

*Christ connection (devotional reading):* Israel "sold the righteous for silver." Centuries later the Righteous One was sold for "thirty pieces of silver" ([Matthew 26:15](https://www.churchofjesuschrist.org/study/scriptures/nt/matt/26?lang=eng&id=p15#p15)). Amos was not speaking of that event, but the two moments show the same sin: pricing the innocent.

**Question:** Israel's leaders worshipped at altars while sleeping on cloaks taken from the poor. Where might we keep religious practices while overlooking how we treat others?

### Amos 2:9–12 — What the Lord Had Done for Israel

The Lord now reminds Israel of its history. "Yet destroyed I the Amorite before them, whose height was like the height of the cedars." He brought them out of Egypt, led them forty years in the wilderness, and gave them the land. Then: "I raised up of your sons for prophets, and of your young men for Nazarites."

*Scripture Helps* explains that "Nazarite" comes from a Hebrew word meaning "dedicated" or "withheld." Nazarites made a vow to dedicate themselves to the Lord's service for a time ([Numbers 6:2–3](https://www.churchofjesuschrist.org/study/scriptures/ot/num/6?lang=eng&id=p2-p3#p2)). Part of the vow was to abstain from wine. Samson was a Nazarite from birth ([Judges 13:5](https://www.churchofjesuschrist.org/study/scriptures/ot/judg/13?lang=eng&id=p5#p5)).

Verse 12 is the climax of the indictment: "But ye gave the Nazarites wine to drink; and commanded the prophets, saying, Prophesy not." Israel did not just ignore God's servants. It worked to corrupt the dedicated and silence the inspired. The Institute manual pairs this verse with Amaziah's command to Amos in [Amos 7:10–15](https://www.churchofjesuschrist.org/study/scriptures/ot/amos/7?lang=eng&id=p10-p15#p10) and asks, "Why do you think the people wanted Amos to stop prophesying?"

**Question:** Why might people today want prophets to stop speaking on certain subjects? What does a person lose by silencing the voices God sends?

### Amos 2:13–16 — No One Will Escape

"Behold, I am pressed under you, as a cart is pressed that is full of sheaves." In the KJV, the Lord is like a cart groaning under the weight of a heavy harvest: Israel's sins are a burden he carries. Modern translations differ on this verse; some read it as the Lord pressing Israel down. Either way, judgment is coming.

Verses 14–16 describe the day of defeat. The swift will not be able to run, the strong will not find strength, the archer will not stand, and the rider will not escape. "He that is courageous among the mighty shall flee away naked in that day." Israel trusted its military strength in Jeroboam's day of success. Amos says that strength would fail.

**Question:** Israel's sins are described as a heavy load on the Lord. How does that image help you think about what sin costs, and who carries it?

## Amos 3: The Lord Reveals His Secret unto His Prophets

Chapter 3 begins the second part of the book. It answers the people who told the prophets to be quiet ([Amos 2:12](https://www.churchofjesuschrist.org/study/scriptures/ot/amos/2?lang=eng&id=p12#p12)). Through a chain of questions, the Lord shows that a prophet speaks because God has spoken, just as a lion roars because it has prey. The chapter then calls witnesses to look at Samaria's injustice and announces the fall of its altars and great houses. The official lesson and the Institute manual both focus on this chapter.

### Amos 3:1–2 — "You Only Have I Known"

"You only have I known of all the families of the earth: therefore I will punish you for all your iniquities." To "know" here means to choose and enter into covenant. The Lord said of Abraham, "For I know him, that he will command his children ... to do justice and judgment" ([Genesis 18:19](https://www.churchofjesuschrist.org/study/scriptures/ot/gen/18?lang=eng&id=p19#p19)). Israel's chosen status did not exempt it from judgment. It made Israel more accountable.

That reverses what Israel expected. The people assumed that being chosen meant protection. The Lord says it means responsibility. Jesus taught the same: "unto whomsoever much is given, of him shall be much required" ([Luke 12:48](https://www.churchofjesuschrist.org/study/scriptures/nt/luke/12?lang=eng&id=p48#p48)). So does modern revelation: "he who sins against the greater light shall receive the greater condemnation" ([Doctrine and Covenants 82:3](https://www.churchofjesuschrist.org/study/scriptures/dc-testament/dc/82?lang=eng&id=p3#p3)).

**Question:** How does knowing the covenants you have made change what the Lord can expect of you?

### Amos 3:3–6 — Questions with Obvious Answers

Amos asks a series of questions, each with an obvious answer. "Can two walk together, except they be agreed?" Two people walking together have met by arrangement. "Will a lion roar in the forest, when he hath no prey?" A lion roars when it has caught something. "Can a bird fall in a snare upon the earth, where no gin is for him?" The footnote explains "gin" as "bait, or lure." A trap springs because something set it off. "Shall a trumpet be blown in the city, and the people not be afraid?" The alarm means danger.

Each question describes a cause and an effect. The official lesson summarizes the point: a lion's roar means there is prey; a bird in a trap means there was bait. The last question applies the pattern: "shall there be evil in a city, and the Lord hath not done it?" "Evil" here means disaster or calamity, not moral wickedness. The JST changes "done" to "known": the Lord knows of the disaster coming on a city. Either way, the chain leads to verses 7–8: there is a reason behind a prophet's warning.

**Question:** Amos's questions teach that effects have causes. When you hear a prophetic warning, what does that suggest about the reality behind it?

### Amos 3:7–8 — "He Revealeth His Secret unto His Servants the Prophets"

"Surely the Lord God will do nothing, but he revealeth his secret unto his servants the prophets." This is one of the best-known verses in Amos. The JST changes "but" to "until": the Lord will do nothing until he reveals his secret to his prophets.

*Scripture Helps* notes that the Hebrew word translated "secret" can also mean "council," "counsel," or "plan." The verse may picture prophets as those who are let into the Lord's council and learn his plans. Jeremiah used that idea: "who hath stood in the counsel of the Lord, and hath perceived and heard his word?" ([Jeremiah 23:18](https://www.churchofjesuschrist.org/study/scriptures/ot/jer/23?lang=eng&id=p18#p18); see [Jeremiah 23:22](https://www.churchofjesuschrist.org/study/scriptures/ot/jer/23?lang=eng&id=p22#p22)). The Lord said of Abraham, "Shall I hide from Abraham that thing which I do" ([Genesis 18:17](https://www.churchofjesuschrist.org/study/scriptures/ot/gen/18?lang=eng&id=p17#p17)). *Scripture Helps* explains that in context Amos was emphasizing that the Lord would warn his people through prophets before bringing destruction. It presents a statement by the Prophet Joseph Smith with the same principle: whenever God was about to bring a judgment on the world, he forewarned it through his servants, as he did with Noah and Lot.

Verse 8 completes the chain: "The lion hath roared, who will not fear? the Lord God hath spoken, who can but prophesy?" A prophet speaks because God has spoken. Asking him to be silent is like asking people not to fear when a lion roars.

The official lesson calls this one of the most precious "secrets" God reveals: that it is not too late to change. It quotes Elder Ulisses Soares: "having prophets is a sign of God's love." The Institute manual quotes President Russell M. Nelson: "Prophets are rarely popular." Then: "But we will *always* teach the truth!" Modern revelation teaches that the Lord's word is the same "whether by mine own voice or by the voice of my servants" ([Doctrine and Covenants 1:38](https://www.churchofjesuschrist.org/study/scriptures/dc-testament/dc/1?lang=eng&id=p38#p38)).

*Christ connection:* Prophets are messengers of Jesus Christ, the theme the official lesson gives for teaching children. Nephi taught that "by the Spirit are all things made known unto the prophets" ([1 Nephi 22:2](https://www.churchofjesuschrist.org/study/scriptures/bofm/1-ne/22?lang=eng&id=p2#p2)). The secret prophets reveal is finally the plan of salvation centered in Christ.

**Question:** The official lesson asks: Because the Lord reveals truth through prophets, what conclusions can we draw when a prophet speaks? What has the Lord revealed through his prophets that has helped you?

### Amos 3:9–12 — Witnesses Called to Samaria

The Lord calls foreign witnesses: "Publish in the palaces at Ashdod, and in the palaces in the land of Egypt, and say, Assemble yourselves upon the mountains of Samaria, and behold the great tumults in the midst thereof, and the oppressed." Even the Philistines and Egyptians would be shocked at what happened in Israel's capital. "They know not to do right, saith the Lord, who store up violence and robbery in their palaces." The palaces are full, but what fills them is stolen.

Verse 11 announces an adversary "round about the land." Verse 12 gives a bitter image: "As the shepherd taketh out of the mouth of the lion two legs, or a piece of an ear; so shall the children of Israel be taken out." Under the law, a shepherd whose sheep was killed by a beast brought back what was left as evidence: "If it be torn in pieces, then let him bring it for witness" ([Exodus 22:13](https://www.churchofjesuschrist.org/study/scriptures/ot/ex/22?lang=eng&id=p13#p13)). Amos, a shepherd himself, knew the practice. What would be "rescued" from Samaria would only be enough to prove that the rest had been destroyed: a corner of a bed, a piece of a couch.

**Question:** Samaria's palaces stored "violence and robbery." What do the things we accumulate say about how we have treated others?

### Amos 3:13–15 — The Altars of Bethel and the Houses of Ivory

"In the day that I shall visit the transgressions of Israel upon him I will also visit the altars of Beth-el: and the horns of the altar shall be cut off." Bethel was the royal sanctuary where Jeroboam I had set up a golden calf ([1 Kings 12:29](https://www.churchofjesuschrist.org/study/scriptures/ot/1-kgs/12?lang=eng&id=p29#p29)). An altar had horn-shaped projections at its corners. A person fleeing for his life might grasp them for refuge, as Joab did ([1 Kings 2:28](https://www.churchofjesuschrist.org/study/scriptures/ot/1-kgs/2?lang=eng&id=p28#p28)). Cutting off the horns meant no refuge would be left.

Then the houses: "I will smite the winter house with the summer house; and the houses of ivory shall perish." Only the very rich had separate homes for winter and summer. Houses decorated with ivory were the height of luxury. King Ahab had built "the ivory house" in Samaria a century earlier ([1 Kings 22:39](https://www.churchofjesuschrist.org/study/scriptures/ot/1-kgs/22?lang=eng&id=p39#p39)). The religion and the wealth of Israel would fall together.

**Question:** Israel looked for security in its altars and its fine houses. Where do people today look for security that cannot protect them?

## Amos 4: Yet Have Ye Not Returned unto Me

Chapter 4 begins with the wealthy women of Samaria, then mocks the busy worship at Bethel and Gilgal. Its center is a list of disasters the Lord sent to wake Israel up, each ending with the same sad refrain: "yet have ye not returned unto me." The chapter ends with a summons, "prepare to meet thy God," and a short hymn to the Creator.

### Amos 4:1–3 — "Ye Kine of Bashan"

"Hear this word, ye kine of Bashan, that are in the mountain of Samaria, which oppress the poor, which crush the needy, which say to their masters, Bring, and let us drink." The footnote explains "kine" as "cows." Bashan, east of the Sea of Galilee, was known for rich pasture and fat cattle ([Deuteronomy 32:14](https://www.churchofjesuschrist.org/study/scriptures/ot/deut/32?lang=eng&id=p14#p14)). Amos addresses wealthy women of Samaria, well fed, demanding drinks from their husbands ("masters"), while the poor are crushed to pay for it.

The image is harsh, and Amos means it to be. He uses it to describe a way of life, not to belittle women. The same book condemns the men who lounge on ivory beds ([Amos 6:4–6](https://www.churchofjesuschrist.org/study/scriptures/ot/amos/6?lang=eng&id=p4-p6#p4)). Both were living comfortably on what was taken from the poor.

The judgment: "he will take you away with hooks, and your posterity with fishhooks. And ye shall go out at the breaches." The footnote gives "broken places" for "breaches." The image is of captives led out single file through gaps in the broken city wall. The cows of Bashan would be driven out like cattle.

**Question:** The women of Samaria may never have seen the poor whose labor paid for their comfort. How can we become aware of the people affected by how we live?

### Amos 4:4–5 — "Come to Beth-el, and Transgress"

Amos imitates a priest's call to worship and twists it: "Come to Beth-el, and transgress; at Gilgal multiply transgression." Bethel and Gilgal were important sanctuaries. The people brought "sacrifices every morning, and your tithes after three years" (the footnote gives "days" for "years," as if they paid tithes every three days). They made thank offerings and announced their freewill offerings publicly. "For this liketh you, O ye children of Israel." They liked the religion, the activity, and the display, but it was not changing them.

**Question:** Israel loved religious activity that did not require a change of heart. What is the difference between being religiously busy and turning to the Lord?

### Amos 4:6–11 — Five Calls to Return

The Lord lists what he had already done to get Israel's attention:

- **Famine:** "cleanness of teeth in all your cities, and want of bread" (verse 6). Clean teeth meant there was nothing to eat.
- **Drought:** rain withheld "when there were yet three months to the harvest," falling on one city and not another, so that people "wandered unto one city, to drink water; but they were not satisfied" (verses 7–8).
- **Blight and locusts:** "blasting and mildew," with the palmerworm devouring gardens, vineyards, fig trees, and olive trees (verse 9).
- **Plague and war:** "pestilence after the manner of Egypt," young men slain, and the stench of the camps (verse 10).
- **Overthrow:** "as God overthrew Sodom and Gomorrah," leaving Israel "as a firebrand plucked out of the burning" (verse 11).

After each disaster comes the refrain: "yet have ye not returned unto me, saith the Lord." It appears five times. The disasters echo the warnings of the covenant in the law of Moses (see [Leviticus 26:14–26](https://www.churchofjesuschrist.org/study/scriptures/ot/lev/26?lang=eng&id=p14-p26#p14) and [Deuteronomy 28:15–24](https://www.churchofjesuschrist.org/study/scriptures/ot/deut/28?lang=eng&id=p15-p24#p15)). The point of each one was not to destroy but to turn Israel back. "A brand plucked out of the fire" appears later in Zechariah as an image of rescue ([Zechariah 3:2](https://www.churchofjesuschrist.org/study/scriptures/ot/zech/3?lang=eng&id=p2#p2)). Israel had been rescued like a stick pulled from a fire, and still did not come home.

The official lesson asks what the Lord hoped would happen after these trials. *Scripture Helps* notes that the physical famines of chapter 4 prepare for the spiritual famine of chapter 8.

**Question:** Not every hardship is a punishment, and scripture warns against assuming it is (see [John 9:2–3](https://www.churchofjesuschrist.org/study/scriptures/nt/john/9?lang=eng&id=p2-p3#p2)). But when trials come, how can they turn us toward the Lord rather than away from him?

### Amos 4:12–13 — "Prepare to Meet Thy God"

"Therefore thus will I do unto thee, O Israel: and because I will do this unto thee, prepare to meet thy God, O Israel." The Lord does not say what "this" is. The silence is ominous. Since five calls to return had failed, Israel must now face God himself.

Book of Mormon prophets use the same phrase with a hopeful sense: "this life is the time for men to prepare to meet God" ([Alma 34:32](https://www.churchofjesuschrist.org/study/scriptures/bofm/alma/34?lang=eng&id=p32#p32)). In Amos the meeting is near and dreadful. In Alma it is the purpose of life. Both mean that the meeting will come.

Verse 13 is a short hymn: "For, lo, he that formeth the mountains, and createth the wind, and declareth unto man what is his thought, that maketh the morning darkness, and treadeth upon the high places of the earth, The Lord, The God of hosts, is his name." The God Israel must meet is the Creator of the mountains and the wind. He also "declareth unto man what is his thought," which may mean that he reveals his own thoughts to people (compare [Amos 3:7](https://www.churchofjesuschrist.org/study/scriptures/ot/amos/3?lang=eng&id=p7#p7)) or that he shows people their own hidden thoughts. Similar hymns appear in [Amos 5:8–9](https://www.churchofjesuschrist.org/study/scriptures/ot/amos/5?lang=eng&id=p8-p9#p8) and [Amos 9:5–6](https://www.churchofjesuschrist.org/study/scriptures/ot/amos/9?lang=eng&id=p5-p6#p5).

*Christ connection:* Latter-day Saints understand Jehovah, the God of the Old Testament, to be Jesus Christ before his mortal birth. The God whom Israel must prepare to meet is the same Lord who later came to save.

**Question:** What does it mean for you to "prepare to meet thy God"? What would you want to change now?

## Amos 5: Seek the Lord, and Ye Shall Live

Chapter 5 is the heart of the book and gives this week's lesson its title. It begins as a funeral song for Israel, as if the nation had already died. Then, in the middle of the lament, comes a repeated invitation: "Seek ye me, and ye shall live." The chapter goes on to condemn injustice in the courts, warn those who long for "the day of the Lord," and reject worship without righteousness, ending with the call to "let judgment run down as waters."

### Amos 5:1–3 — A Funeral Song for Israel

"Hear ye this word which I take up against you, even a lamentation, O house of Israel. The virgin of Israel is fallen; she shall no more rise." A lamentation was a funeral song. Amos sings one over a nation that was still alive, prosperous, and confident. Calling Israel a "virgin" makes the lament sadder: she has died young, before her life was fulfilled.

Verse 3 shows the scale of loss: "The city that went out by a thousand shall leave an hundred, and that which went forth by an hundred shall leave ten." Nine out of ten soldiers would not come home.

**Question:** Why might the Lord speak of Israel's destruction as though it had already happened, while still calling the people to repent?

### Amos 5:4–6 — "Seek Ye Me, and Ye Shall Live"

"For thus saith the Lord unto the house of Israel, Seek ye me, and ye shall live: But seek not Beth-el, nor enter into Gilgal, and pass not to Beer-sheba." Israel was already traveling to sanctuaries, even as far south as Beer-sheba in Judah. The Lord tells them to stop. Seeking a holy place is not the same as seeking him. "Gilgal shall surely go into captivity, and Beth-el shall come to nought." The places Israel trusted would not survive.

Verse 6 repeats the call: "Seek the Lord, and ye shall live; lest he break out like fire in the house of Joseph, and devour it, and there be none to quench it in Beth-el." "The house of Joseph" means the northern tribes, led by Ephraim and Manasseh, Joseph's sons. The Lord's fire would come where Bethel's altars could not put it out.

The official lesson invites readers to ponder [Amos 5:4](https://www.churchofjesuschrist.org/study/scriptures/ot/amos/5?lang=eng&id=p4#p4), [Amos 5:14–15](https://www.churchofjesuschrist.org/study/scriptures/ot/amos/5?lang=eng&id=p14-p15#p14), and how seeking the Lord has brought life to them. The promise appears elsewhere: "ye shall seek me, and find me, when ye shall search for me with all your heart" ([Jeremiah 29:13](https://www.churchofjesuschrist.org/study/scriptures/ot/jer/29?lang=eng&id=p13#p13)); "seek me diligently and ye shall find me" ([Doctrine and Covenants 88:63](https://www.churchofjesuschrist.org/study/scriptures/dc-testament/dc/88?lang=eng&id=p63#p63)).

*Christ connection:* Jesus said, "I am the way, the truth, and the life" ([John 14:6](https://www.churchofjesuschrist.org/study/scriptures/nt/john/14?lang=eng&id=p6#p6)). For Christians, seeking the Lord and living are joined in him.

**Question:** What is the difference between seeking religious places or activities and seeking the Lord himself? How has seeking him brought life to you?

### Amos 5:7–9 — Wormwood and the Maker of the Stars

"Ye who turn judgment to wormwood, and leave off righteousness in the earth." Wormwood is a bitter plant. Justice, which should be sweet and healing, had been made bitter. Amos uses a similar image in [Amos 6:12](https://www.churchofjesuschrist.org/study/scriptures/ot/amos/6?lang=eng&id=p12#p12).

Verses 8–9 interrupt with a hymn: "Seek him that maketh the seven stars and Orion, and turneth the shadow of death into the morning ... The Lord is his name." The footnotes explain "the seven stars" as the Pleiades and give "Jehovah" for "The Lord." Job uses the same constellations to praise the Creator ([Job 9:9](https://www.churchofjesuschrist.org/study/scriptures/ot/job/9?lang=eng&id=p9#p9)). The God who orders the stars also "turneth the shadow of death into the morning." He "strengtheneth the spoiled against the strong, so that the spoiled shall come against the fortress." The oppressed are not helpless against the powerful, because God is on their side.

**Question:** The God who made the stars is also the God who defends the oppressed. How does remembering God's power affect how you think about injustice?

### Amos 5:10–13 — Injustice in the Gate

"They hate him that rebuketh in the gate, and they abhor him that speaketh uprightly." In ancient Israel, the city gate was where elders met to judge cases. A person who spoke up there for justice was hated.

Verses 11–12 name the injustice: "your treading is upon the poor, and ye take from him burdens of wheat." The wealthy took grain from poor farmers as rent or levy. "They afflict the just, they take a bribe, and they turn aside the poor in the gate from their right." The courts that should have protected the poor were for sale.

The judgment fits the crime: "ye have built houses of hewn stone, but ye shall not dwell in them; ye have planted pleasant vineyards, but ye shall not drink wine of them." This echoes the covenant curse in [Deuteronomy 28:30](https://www.churchofjesuschrist.org/study/scriptures/ot/deut/28?lang=eng&id=p30#p30). Wealth gained by injustice would not be enjoyed.

Verse 13 is hard to interpret: "Therefore the prudent shall keep silence in that time; for it is an evil time." It may be a sad observation (in such a time, wise people keep quiet because speaking is useless or dangerous) or a warning (the time will be so evil that silence is the only safe course). Amos himself did not keep silence.

**Question:** Amos says people hated "him that rebuketh in the gate." Why is it so hard to hear correction? How can we become people who welcome it?

### Amos 5:14–15 — "It May Be That the Lord ... Will Be Gracious"

"Seek good, and not evil, that ye may live: and so the Lord, the God of hosts, shall be with you, as ye have spoken." Israel claimed that the Lord was with them. Amos says he will be, if they seek good. "Hate the evil, and love the good, and establish judgment in the gate." Repentance would show itself in the courts, where justice had been sold.

"It may be that the Lord God of hosts will be gracious unto the remnant of Joseph." The word "may" is honest. Israel could not presume on God's grace. The same humble hope appears in Joel: "Who knoweth if he will return and repent" ([Joel 2:14](https://www.churchofjesuschrist.org/study/scriptures/ot/joel/2?lang=eng&id=p14#p14)), and in Nineveh: "Who can tell if God will turn and repent" ([Jonah 3:9](https://www.churchofjesuschrist.org/study/scriptures/ot/jonah/3?lang=eng&id=p9#p9)). In all three, people turn to God without claiming they have earned his mercy.

**Question:** What does it look like to "hate the evil, and love the good" in daily life, not just in feeling but in action?

### Amos 5:16–20 — "The Day of the Lord Is Darkness, and Not Light"

The lament returns: "Wailing shall be in all streets ... and in all vineyards shall be wailing: for I will pass through thee, saith the Lord." The phrase "I will pass through thee" recalls the Passover, when the Lord passed through Egypt ([Exodus 12:12](https://www.churchofjesuschrist.org/study/scriptures/ot/ex/12?lang=eng&id=p12#p12)). Now he would pass through Israel.

Verse 18: "Woe unto you that desire the day of the Lord! to what end is it for you? the day of the Lord is darkness, and not light." *Scripture Helps* explains that "the day of the Lord" is when the Lord judges the wicked and saves the righteous, and that many in Amos's day expected it to bring them victory over their enemies. The Lord said it would bring them darkness unless they repented.

Verse 19 is almost a dark joke: "As if a man did flee from a lion, and a bear met him; or went into the house, and leaned his hand on the wall, and a serpent bit him." A person escapes one danger only to meet another, and then, at home and feeling safe at last, is bitten. Joel described the day of the Lord in similar terms ([Joel 2:2](https://www.churchofjesuschrist.org/study/scriptures/ot/joel/2?lang=eng&id=p2#p2)), and so did Zephaniah ([Zephaniah 1:15](https://www.churchofjesuschrist.org/study/scriptures/ot/zeph/1?lang=eng&id=p15#p15)).

**Question:** Israel wanted the day of the Lord without being ready for it. How can a person look forward to the Savior's coming with hope rather than false confidence?

### Amos 5:21–24 — "Let Judgment Run Down as Waters"

"I hate, I despise your feast days, and I will not smell in your solemn assemblies." The footnote explains: "I will not take heed of your sacrifices, incense, etc." The Lord rejects their burnt offerings, meat offerings, and peace offerings. "Take thou away from me the noise of thy songs; for I will not hear the melody of thy viols."

Then the turning point: "But let judgment run down as waters, and righteousness as a mighty stream." "Judgment" here means justice. The image is of a river that never dries up, unlike the seasonal streams of the land that flow in the rainy season and vanish in summer. God wants justice that keeps flowing.

Other prophets said the same: "To what purpose is the multitude of your sacrifices unto me? ... seek judgment, relieve the oppressed, judge the fatherless, plead for the widow" ([Isaiah 1:11](https://www.churchofjesuschrist.org/study/scriptures/ot/isa/1?lang=eng&id=p11#p11); [Isaiah 1:17](https://www.churchofjesuschrist.org/study/scriptures/ot/isa/1?lang=eng&id=p17#p17)); "what doth the Lord require of thee, but to do justly, and to love mercy, and to walk humbly with thy God?" ([Micah 6:8](https://www.churchofjesuschrist.org/study/scriptures/ot/micah/6?lang=eng&id=p8#p8)). The Bible Dictionary summarizes this as Amos's key teaching: the offering the Lord most wants is a righteous life.

*Christ connection:* Jesus rebuked those who paid tithes on garden herbs but "omitted the weightier matters of the law, judgment, mercy, and faith" ([Matthew 23:23](https://www.churchofjesuschrist.org/study/scriptures/nt/matt/23?lang=eng&id=p23#p23)). He did not say tithing was wrong: "these ought ye to have done, and not to leave the other undone."

**Question:** God does not reject worship itself but worship cut off from justice. What would it look like for justice and righteousness to flow through your life "as a mighty stream"?

### Amos 5:25–27 — Idols Carried Since the Wilderness

"Have ye offered unto me sacrifices and offerings in the wilderness forty years, O house of Israel? But ye have borne the tabernacle of your Moloch and Chiun your images, the star of your god, which ye made to yourselves." The question is hard to interpret. It may mean that Israel did not depend on elaborate sacrifices in the wilderness, so sacrifice was never the heart of the covenant. Or it may mean that even then Israel was carrying other gods. Moloch and Chiun were the names of foreign gods, perhaps astral deities.

Stephen quoted this passage in his speech before the Sanhedrin ([Acts 7:42–43](https://www.churchofjesuschrist.org/study/scriptures/nt/acts/7?lang=eng&id=p42-p43#p42)). His wording differs from Amos's: he speaks of "the star of your god Remphan" and says, "I will carry you away beyond Babylon," while Amos says "beyond Damascus." Stephen's quotation seems to follow a different form of the text. The core message is the same in both: idolatry leads to exile.

"Therefore will I cause you to go into captivity beyond Damascus." Assyria lay beyond Damascus, and in 722 B.C. Israel was carried there.

**Question:** Israel carried idols alongside its worship of the Lord. What idols might people carry today without noticing them?

## Amos 6: Woe to Them That Are at Ease in Zion

Chapter 6 turns to the leaders of Israel and Judah who felt secure. It describes their banquets in vivid detail: ivory beds, choice meat, music, wine by the bowl, and fine oils. Their sin is not only luxury but indifference. They were "not grieved for the affliction of Joseph." The chapter ends by turning Jeroboam II's military success against Israel.

### Amos 6:1–3 — "At Ease in Zion"

"Woe to them that are at ease in Zion, and trust in the mountain of Samaria, which are named chief of the nations." The woe falls on the leaders in both capitals: Zion (Jerusalem) and Samaria. They were "named chief," the notable ones, the people to whom "the house of Israel came" for leadership.

Verse 2 invites a comparison: "Pass ye unto Calneh, and see; and from thence go ye to Hamath the great: then go down to Gath of the Philistines: be they better than these kingdoms?" The meaning is debated. The leaders may be told to look at other great cities that had fallen and ask whether Israel was any stronger. Either way, the point is that Israel's security was not guaranteed.

Verse 3: "Ye that put far away the evil day, and cause the seat of violence to come near." They told themselves the day of reckoning was far off, while their violence brought it nearer.

The Book of Mormon echoes the warning: "wo be unto him that is at ease in Zion!" ([2 Nephi 28:24](https://www.churchofjesuschrist.org/study/scriptures/bofm/2-ne/28?lang=eng&id=p24#p24)), describing those whom the devil lulls into "carnal security" by saying, "All is well in Zion" ([2 Nephi 28:21](https://www.churchofjesuschrist.org/study/scriptures/bofm/2-ne/28?lang=eng&id=p21#p21)).

**Question:** What makes people feel "at ease in Zion"? How can we tell the difference between peace that comes from God and false security?

### Amos 6:4–7 — Not Grieved for the Affliction of Joseph

Amos paints the banquet: "That lie upon beds of ivory, and stretch themselves upon their couches, and eat the lambs out of the flock, and the calves out of the midst of the stall; That chant to the sound of the viol, and invent to themselves instruments of musick, like David; That drink wine in bowls, and anoint themselves with the chief ointments."

None of these things is evil by itself. The charge comes at the end of verse 6: "but they are not grieved for the affliction of Joseph." "Joseph" means the northern tribes, the nation they led. Its people were suffering and its ruin was coming, and the leaders did not care.

The judgment is ironic: "Therefore now shall they go captive with the first that go captive." Those who were first in rank would be first into exile. "The banquet of them that stretched themselves shall be removed." The footnote gives "revelry" for "banquet."

At baptism, Latter-day Saints covenant to "mourn with those that mourn; yea, and comfort those that stand in need of comfort" ([Mosiah 18:9](https://www.churchofjesuschrist.org/study/scriptures/bofm/mosiah/18?lang=eng&id=p9#p9)). Amos condemns the opposite.

**Question:** The leaders' sin was indifference to suffering they could see. Whose affliction might we be tempted to overlook? What would it mean to be "grieved" for it?

### Amos 6:8–11 — The Lord Abhors the Pride of Jacob

"The Lord God hath sworn by himself ... I abhor the excellency of Jacob, and hate his palaces." The footnote gives "pride" for "excellency." The Lord swears by himself, since there is no one greater to swear by (compare [Hebrews 6:13](https://www.churchofjesuschrist.org/study/scriptures/nt/heb/6?lang=eng&id=p13#p13)). What he hates is the pride of Israel, embodied in its palaces.

Verses 9–10 describe a grim scene after disaster. "If there remain ten men in one house ... they shall die." A relative comes to carry out the bodies for burning. He calls to someone hiding in the house, "Is there yet any with thee?" The answer is "No." Then: "Hold thy tongue: for we may not make mention of the name of the Lord." The survivors are afraid even to say the Lord's name, perhaps for fear of drawing more judgment. The God they had once taken for granted had become a God they feared to name.

"The Lord commandeth, and he will smite the great house with breaches, and the little house with clefts." Great and small would fall together.

**Question:** The survivors in this scene are afraid to mention the Lord's name. How does pride in times of ease lead to fear in times of trouble?

### Amos 6:12–14 — Horses on Rock

"Shall horses run upon the rock? will one plow there with oxen? for ye have turned judgment into gall, and the fruit of righteousness into hemlock." Horses do not gallop on rock cliffs, and no one plows rock with oxen. Perverting justice is just as absurd and unnatural. Gall and hemlock are bitter poisons (compare [Amos 5:7](https://www.churchofjesuschrist.org/study/scriptures/ot/amos/5?lang=eng&id=p7#p7)).

"Ye which rejoice in a thing of nought, which say, Have we not taken to us horns by our own strength?" Horns are a symbol of strength. Israel boasted of military victories it believed it had won by itself.

Verse 14 announces the reversal: "I will raise up against you a nation, O house of Israel ... and they shall afflict you from the entering in of Hemath unto the river of the wilderness." Compare that boundary with the account of Jeroboam II, who "restored the coast of Israel from the entering of Hamath unto the sea of the plain" ([2 Kings 14:25](https://www.churchofjesuschrist.org/study/scriptures/ot/2-kgs/14?lang=eng&id=p25#p25)). Both passages begin at "the entering" of Hamath, though they describe the southern limit differently. Where Jeroboam's restored border began, Israel's affliction would begin. The verse in 2 Kings adds that Jeroboam's victories came "according to the word of the Lord ... which he spake by the hand of his servant Jonah." Israel's success had been a gift, not "our own strength."

**Question:** Israel credited its success to its own strength. What successes in your life might you be tempted to claim for yourself that were really gifts from God?

## Amos 7: Visions, Intercession, and a Prophet Confronted

Chapter 7 begins the third part of the book, a series of visions. In the first two, Amos sees disasters coming and pleads for Israel, and the Lord holds back. In the third, the Lord stands with a plumbline, and there is no more holding back. Then comes the only story in the book about Amos himself: the priest of Bethel orders him to leave, and Amos tells how the Lord called him. The official lesson and the Institute manual both use this account to teach about prophets.

### Amos 7:1–3 — The Vision of Locusts

"Thus hath the Lord God shewed unto me; and, behold, he formed grasshoppers in the beginning of the shooting up of the latter growth; and, lo, it was the latter growth after the king's mowings." The first cutting of the spring growth apparently went to the king. The second growth was what the people lived on. Locusts that came then would leave nothing.

Amos pleaded: "O Lord God, forgive, I beseech thee: by whom shall Jacob arise? for he is small." Israel was proud and thought itself great ([Amos 6:13](https://www.churchofjesuschrist.org/study/scriptures/ot/amos/6?lang=eng&id=p13#p13)). Amos saw it as small and helpless.

"The Lord repented for this: It shall not be, saith the Lord." *Scripture Helps* explains that the Hebrew word translated "repented" can mean "to regret," "to feel sorrow or sympathy," "to comfort," or "to relent or forebear." God does not repent of sin, since he has none. The JST footnote reads instead that the Lord said, "concerning Jacob, Jacob shall repent for this," and "therefore I will not utterly destroy him." On this reading, the Lord withholds judgment because Israel will repent. *Scripture Helps* points readers to Jonah for comparison ([Jonah 3:9–10](https://www.churchofjesuschrist.org/study/scriptures/ot/jonah/3?lang=eng&id=p9-p10#p9); [Jonah 4:2](https://www.churchofjesuschrist.org/study/scriptures/ot/jonah/4?lang=eng&id=p2#p2)).

Amos is an intercessor, like Abraham, who asked, "Shall not the Judge of all the earth do right?" ([Genesis 18:25](https://www.churchofjesuschrist.org/study/scriptures/ot/gen/18?lang=eng&id=p25#p25)), and Moses, who pleaded for Israel after the golden calf ([Exodus 32:11–14](https://www.churchofjesuschrist.org/study/scriptures/ot/ex/32?lang=eng&id=p11-p14#p11)). A prophet's role includes both warning the people and pleading for them.

*Christ connection:* Jesus Christ is the great intercessor. "He ever liveth to make intercession for them" ([Hebrews 7:25](https://www.churchofjesuschrist.org/study/scriptures/nt/heb/7?lang=eng&id=p25#p25)); "we have an advocate with the Father, Jesus Christ the righteous" ([1 John 2:1](https://www.churchofjesuschrist.org/study/scriptures/nt/1-jn/2?lang=eng&id=p1#p1)). Modern revelation records his plea: "Father, spare these my brethren that believe on my name" ([Doctrine and Covenants 45:3–5](https://www.churchofjesuschrist.org/study/scriptures/dc-testament/dc/45?lang=eng&id=p3-p5#p3)).

**Question:** Amos had harsh words for Israel, but he also pleaded for it. What does his example teach about how to feel toward people we must warn or correct?

### Amos 7:4–6 — The Vision of Fire

"The Lord God called to contend by fire, and it devoured the great deep, and did eat up a part." The fire was so great it dried up the "great deep," the waters beneath the earth, and began consuming the land. Amos pleaded again, this time not "forgive" but "cease": "O Lord God, cease, I beseech thee: by whom shall Jacob arise? for he is small."

Again: "The Lord repented for this: This also shall not be, saith the Lord God." The JST footnote reads that the Lord said, "concerning Jacob, Jacob shall repent of his wickedness; therefore I will not utterly destroy him." The phrase "not utterly destroy" also appears in Amos's last chapter: "saving that I will not utterly destroy the house of Jacob" ([Amos 9:8](https://www.churchofjesuschrist.org/study/scriptures/ot/amos/9?lang=eng&id=p8#p8)).

**Question:** Amos twice asked, "By whom shall Jacob arise? for he is small." How does recognizing our smallness open the way for God's mercy?

### Amos 7:7–9 — The Plumbline

"Behold, the Lord stood upon a wall made by a plumbline, with a plumbline in his hand." *Scripture Helps* explains that a plumbline is a cord with a weight on one end, used by builders to make sure a wall is vertical. In Amos's vision it is figurative: God measures his people, and the line shows how far they have strayed from true.

"Amos, what seest thou? And I said, A plumbline." The Lord explains: "Behold, I will set a plumbline in the midst of my people Israel: I will not again pass by them any more." This time Amos does not plead. A crooked wall cannot be saved by a request. It has to be taken down.

"The high places of Isaac shall be desolate, and the sanctuaries of Israel shall be laid waste; and I will rise against the house of Jeroboam with the sword." After Jeroboam II died, his son Zachariah reigned only six months before Shallum "smote him before the people, and slew him" ([2 Kings 15:8–10](https://www.churchofjesuschrist.org/study/scriptures/ot/2-kgs/15?lang=eng&id=p8-p10#p8)), ending Jeroboam's dynasty.

**Question:** A plumbline does not bend to fit a wall. What "plumbline" does the Lord use to measure our lives, and how can we let it straighten us rather than condemn us?

### Amos 7:10–13 — Amaziah: "Prophesy Not Again Any More at Beth-el"

"Then Amaziah the priest of Beth-el sent to Jeroboam king of Israel, saying, Amos hath conspired against thee ... the land is not able to bear all his words." Amaziah reports Amos's message to the king as political treason.

Notice how Amaziah summarizes Amos: "For thus Amos saith, Jeroboam shall die by the sword." What Amos had actually reported was that the Lord said, "I will rise against the house of Jeroboam with the sword" ([Amos 7:9](https://www.churchofjesuschrist.org/study/scriptures/ot/amos/7?lang=eng&id=p9#p9)). Amaziah turns a word from the Lord about the dynasty into a personal threat from Amos against the king. In fact Jeroboam II himself "slept with his fathers" ([2 Kings 14:29](https://www.churchofjesuschrist.org/study/scriptures/ot/2-kgs/14?lang=eng&id=p29#p29)), and the sword fell on his son.

Then Amaziah speaks to Amos directly: "O thou seer, go, flee thee away into the land of Judah, and there eat bread, and prophesy there: But prophesy not again any more at Beth-el: for it is the king's chapel, and it is the king's court." The footnote gives "sanctuary" for "chapel." Amaziah's words reveal the problem: Bethel had become the king's sanctuary, not the Lord's. "Eat bread" implies that Amos prophesied for a living, and should go earn it somewhere else.

The Institute manual pairs this passage with [Amos 2:11–12](https://www.churchofjesuschrist.org/study/scriptures/ot/amos/2?lang=eng&id=p11-p12#p11), where Israel told the prophets, "Prophesy not," and asks, "Why do you think God's prophets continue to teach even when some people reject their words?"

**Question:** Amaziah tried to remove Amos by calling his message treason. How do people sometimes reframe a prophet's message to avoid hearing it?

### Amos 7:14–15 — "I Was No Prophet ... but I Was an Herdman"

"Then answered Amos, and said to Amaziah, I was no prophet, neither was I a prophet's son; but I was an herdman, and a gatherer of sycomore fruit: And the Lord took me as I followed the flock, and the Lord said unto me, Go, prophesy unto my people Israel."

Amos answers Amaziah's charge that he prophesied for bread. He had a living: he was a herdman and "a gatherer of sycomore fruit." Many interpreters, including Hilton, read his words to mean that he was not a professional prophet. He spoke because the Lord "took me as I followed the flock." His authority came from the call, not from position. John Hilton III writes, "I love that Amos wasn't a professional prophet–he was just a shepherd who did what God called him to do–even in the face of opposition" ([Hilton, "Amos"](https://johnhiltoniii.com/amos/)). He applies this to anyone who feels unqualified for a calling.

The phrase "the Lord took me as I followed the flock" recalls David, whom the Lord "took ... from the sheepcote, from following the sheep" ([2 Samuel 7:8](https://www.churchofjesuschrist.org/study/scriptures/ot/2-sam/7?lang=eng&id=p8#p8)).

*A Book of Mormon parallel (interpretation):* John A. Tvedtnes compared Amaziah's dismissive "O thou seer" with Sariah's complaint that Lehi was "a visionary man" ([1 Nephi 5:2](https://www.churchofjesuschrist.org/study/scriptures/bofm/1-ne/5?lang=eng&id=p2#p2)) and Lehi's reply, "I know that I am a visionary man; for if I had not seen the things of God in a vision I should not have known the goodness of God" ([1 Nephi 5:4](https://www.churchofjesuschrist.org/study/scriptures/bofm/1-ne/5?lang=eng&id=p4#p4)). Tvedtnes proposed that the Hebrew word behind the KJV "seer" in Amos 7:12, *ḥôzeh*, is "likely" the term behind the Book of Mormon phrase "visionary man," and he called Amos's reply "similar to that of Lehi" ([Tvedtnes, "A Visionary Man"](https://scripturecentral.org/archive/books/book-chapter/visionary-man)). The parallel and the proposed Hebrew are Tvedtnes's suggestions, not something the text of either book establishes. Both passages show a prophet whose calling was questioned and who answered by pointing to what God had shown him.

**Question:** Amos said, "the Lord took me as I followed the flock." How has the Lord called you to serve while you were doing ordinary things? What gives you courage to act on that call?

### Amos 7:16–17 — The Word Against Amaziah

"Now therefore hear thou the word of the Lord: Thou sayest, Prophesy not against Israel, and drop not thy word against the house of Isaac." Amaziah had commanded silence. Amos answers with more prophecy.

The sentence on Amaziah is terrible: "Thy wife shall be an harlot in the city," a sentence that may describe a woman left destitute or abused after the city's conquest. His children would die by the sword, his land would be divided among others, and he himself would "die in a polluted land," that is, in an unclean foreign land in exile. These are the horrors that fell on families when a city was conquered. Amos describes them as the consequence that would fall on the priest who silenced the warning, along with all Israel. "Israel shall surely go into captivity forth of his land."

These words describe what war would bring. They are a prophecy of consequences, not a pattern for how anyone should treat an opponent.

**Question:** Amaziah's own family would suffer from the disaster he refused to hear about. How do leaders' choices affect the people closest to them?

## Amos 8: A Famine of Hearing the Words of the Lord

Chapter 8 begins with a fourth vision, a basket of summer fruit, which means that Israel's end has come. Then Amos returns to the merchants who cheat the poor and cannot wait for holy days to end. The chapter's most famous prophecy describes a famine not of bread but "of hearing the words of the Lord." Church sources connect this prophecy to periods without prophets, including the Great Apostasy.

### Amos 8:1–3 — A Basket of Summer Fruit

"Thus hath the Lord God shewed unto me: and behold a basket of summer fruit." The Lord asks, "Amos, what seest thou?" Amos answers, "A basket of summer fruit." Then: "The end is come upon my people of Israel; I will not again pass by them any more." Summer fruit is ripe fruit at the end of the harvest season. Israel is ripe, and its season is over. The words "I will not again pass by them any more" repeat [Amos 7:8](https://www.churchofjesuschrist.org/study/scriptures/ot/amos/7?lang=eng&id=p8#p8).

"The songs of the temple shall be howlings in that day ... there shall be many dead bodies in every place; they shall cast them forth with silence." The songs of worship that the Lord refused to hear ([Amos 5:23](https://www.churchofjesuschrist.org/study/scriptures/ot/amos/5?lang=eng&id=p23#p23)) would become wailing.

Samuel the Lamanite used a related image when he warned that a people who cast out the righteous would "be ripe for destruction" ([Helaman 13:14](https://www.churchofjesuschrist.org/study/scriptures/bofm/hel/13?lang=eng&id=p14#p14)).

**Question:** The image of ripe fruit suggests that there is a time when choices reach their full result. How can we act before our choices ripen into consequences we do not want?

### Amos 8:4–7 — Cheating the Poor

"Hear this, O ye that swallow up the needy, even to make the poor of the land to fail, Saying, When will the new moon be gone, that we may sell corn? and the sabbath, that we may set forth wheat." The merchants kept the new moon festival and the Sabbath, when trade was forbidden, but they resented them. Their minds were on business.

They cheated in three ways: "making the ephah small" (selling short measure), "and the shekel great" (using a heavy weight to take more silver), "and falsifying the balances by deceit" (rigging the scales). The law forbade all of this: "Thou shalt not have in thy bag divers weights, a great and a small ... a perfect and just weight ... shalt thou have" ([Deuteronomy 25:13–15](https://www.churchofjesuschrist.org/study/scriptures/ot/deut/25?lang=eng&id=p13-p15#p13)). They also sold "the refuse of the wheat," the sweepings, and bought the poor "for silver, and the needy for a pair of shoes" (compare [Amos 2:6](https://www.churchofjesuschrist.org/study/scriptures/ot/amos/2?lang=eng&id=p6#p6)).

"The Lord hath sworn by the excellency of Jacob, Surely I will never forget any of their works." The excellency (pride) of Jacob was mentioned in [Amos 6:8](https://www.churchofjesuschrist.org/study/scriptures/ot/amos/6?lang=eng&id=p8#p8). The Lord swears that he will remember every unjust deal.

Hilton's written article quotes this passage as the clearest statement of Amos's concern for the poor and suggests helping families or classes experience what it is like to have nothing, then discuss sharing. King Benjamin taught the same duty: "ye will not suffer that the beggar putteth up his petition to you in vain" ([Mosiah 4:16](https://www.churchofjesuschrist.org/study/scriptures/bofm/mosiah/4?lang=eng&id=p16#p16)).

**Question:** The merchants kept the Sabbath outwardly while their hearts were in their business. How can Sabbath observance reach our hearts and change how we treat others the rest of the week?

### Amos 8:8–10 — The Sun Going Down at Noon

"Shall not the land tremble for this ... and it shall rise up wholly as a flood; and it shall be cast out and drowned, as by the flood of Egypt." The footnote explains: "overflow and recede like the river of Egypt." The Nile rose and fell every year. The land of Israel would heave like the Nile in flood.

"I will cause the sun to go down at noon, and I will darken the earth in the clear day: And I will turn your feasts into mourning ... and I will make it as the mourning of an only son." The loss of an only son is used in scripture as a picture of the deepest grief (compare [Zechariah 12:10](https://www.churchofjesuschrist.org/study/scriptures/ot/zech/12?lang=eng&id=p10#p10)).

*Christ connection (devotional reading):* Amos is describing the day of Israel's judgment. Christian readers have also noticed that on the day of Christ's Crucifixion "there was darkness over all the land" from the sixth hour to the ninth ([Matthew 27:45](https://www.churchofjesuschrist.org/study/scriptures/nt/matt/27?lang=eng&id=p45#p45)), and Zechariah speaks of mourning for the one "whom they have pierced ... as one mourneth for his only son" ([Zechariah 12:10](https://www.churchofjesuschrist.org/study/scriptures/ot/zech/12?lang=eng&id=p10#p10)). Amos did not prophesy the Crucifixion here, but the images of darkness at noon and mourning for an only son point devotionally to the day when God's Only Begotten Son died ([John 3:16](https://www.churchofjesuschrist.org/study/scriptures/nt/john/3?lang=eng&id=p16#p16)).

**Question:** Amos describes feasts turned into mourning. How has the Savior turned mourning into joy in your life?

### Amos 8:11–12 — A Famine of Hearing the Words of the Lord

"Behold, the days come, saith the Lord God, that I will send a famine in the land, not a famine of bread, nor a thirst for water, but of hearing the words of the Lord: And they shall wander from sea to sea, and from the north even to the east, they shall run to and fro to seek the word of the Lord, and shall not find it."

In chapter 4 the Lord sent famine and drought to call Israel back. Now he describes a worse famine. Israel had told the prophets to stop speaking ([Amos 2:12](https://www.churchofjesuschrist.org/study/scriptures/ot/amos/2?lang=eng&id=p12#p12); [Amos 7:13](https://www.churchofjesuschrist.org/study/scriptures/ot/amos/7?lang=eng&id=p13#p13)). The Institute manual states the principle: "When people reject the Lord's prophets, they lose the blessings of the word of the Lord." People would search everywhere and not find what they had once refused.

*Scripture Helps* describes two fulfillments. One was the more than 400 years after Malachi when there were no called prophets in Israel. Another was the apostasy after the deaths of Christ's Apostles, until the Restoration through Joseph Smith. It notes that a similar apostasy occurred in the Americas a few centuries later (see [4 Nephi 1:45–49](https://www.churchofjesuschrist.org/study/scriptures/bofm/4-ne/1?lang=eng&id=p45-p49#p45)). The Institute manual cites Paul's prophecy of "a falling away" ([2 Thessalonians 2:3](https://www.churchofjesuschrist.org/study/scriptures/nt/2-thes/2?lang=eng&id=p3#p3)). The official lesson asks how the Restoration satisfies spiritual hunger.

The Institute manual also applies the prophecy personally: we can experience a personal spiritual famine if we do not nourish our spirits with God's word. It quotes President D. Todd Christofferson's warning about "growing scriptural illiteracy" and Elder Stephen W. Owen's image of "the spiritual equivalent of feeding hay to deer."

*Christ connection:* Moses taught that "man doth not live by bread only, but by every word that proceedeth out of the mouth of the Lord doth man live" ([Deuteronomy 8:3](https://www.churchofjesuschrist.org/study/scriptures/ot/deut/8?lang=eng&id=p3#p3)), and Jesus quoted it when he was hungry in the wilderness. Jesus also said, "I am the bread of life: he that cometh to me shall never hunger; and he that believeth on me shall never thirst" ([John 6:35](https://www.churchofjesuschrist.org/study/scriptures/nt/john/6?lang=eng&id=p35#p35)). The cure for spiritual famine is Christ himself and his word.

**Question:** The official lesson asks why famine is a good comparison for living without the word of God. When have you felt spiritually hungry, and what satisfied that hunger?

### Amos 8:13–14 — Fainting for Thirst

"In that day shall the fair virgins and young men faint for thirst." Even the young and strong would fail, both from physical thirst and from the spiritual famine just described.

"They that swear by the sin of Samaria, and say, Thy god, O Dan, liveth; and, The manner of Beer-sheba liveth; even they shall fall, and never rise up again." People swore oaths by the gods of the northern shrines. "The sin of Samaria" may be an idol worshipped there. Dan was the site of one of Jeroboam I's golden calves ([1 Kings 12:29](https://www.churchofjesuschrist.org/study/scriptures/ot/1-kgs/12?lang=eng&id=p29#p29)). Those who swore by false gods would find that those gods could not hold them up. "They shall fall, and never rise up again" echoes the lament for "the virgin of Israel" who "shall no more rise" ([Amos 5:2](https://www.churchofjesuschrist.org/study/scriptures/ot/amos/5?lang=eng&id=p2#p2)).

**Question:** People in Amos's day swore by gods that could not save them. What do people today rely on as if it were living and powerful, when it cannot give life?

## Amos 9: No Escape, and the Tabernacle of David Raised Up

The final chapter begins with the fifth and most terrifying vision: the Lord standing by the altar, commanding its destruction, with no hiding place for those who flee. A third hymn praises the Creator. Then Israel is reminded that it has no special claim apart from obedience. But the book does not end in destruction. The Lord will sift Israel without losing a grain, raise up the fallen house of David, and plant his people in their land again.

### Amos 9:1–4 — "Though They Dig into Hell"

"I saw the Lord standing upon the altar: and he said, Smite the lintel of the door, that the posts may shake." The altar was where Israel came to meet God. Now the Lord stands there to judge, and the sanctuary itself shakes.

Verses 2–4 describe the impossibility of escape: "Though they dig into hell, thence shall mine hand take them; though they climb up to heaven, thence will I bring them down." Not the top of Carmel, not the bottom of the sea ("thence will I command the serpent"), not even captivity would hide them. "I will set mine eyes upon them for evil, and not for good."

Psalm 139 uses the same images to say the opposite: "If I ascend up into heaven, thou art there: if I make my bed in hell, behold, thou art there ... Even there shall thy hand lead me" ([Psalm 139:7–10](https://www.churchofjesuschrist.org/study/scriptures/ot/ps/139?lang=eng&id=p7-p10#p7)). God's presence everywhere is terrifying to those who flee him and comforting to those who trust him. Jonah, in this week's reading, learns the same thing when he tries to flee "from the presence of the Lord" by sea ([Jonah 1:3](https://www.churchofjesuschrist.org/study/scriptures/ot/jonah/1?lang=eng&id=p3#p3)).

**Question:** For the psalmist, God's presence everywhere was a comfort. For those in Amos 9, it was a terror. What makes the difference?

### Amos 9:5–6 — The Lord Who Touches the Land

"And the Lord God of hosts is he that toucheth the land, and it shall melt, and all that dwell therein shall mourn ... It is he that buildeth his stories in the heaven ... he that calleth for the waters of the sea, and poureth them out upon the face of the earth: The Lord is his name." This is the third hymn in Amos (see [Amos 4:13](https://www.churchofjesuschrist.org/study/scriptures/ot/amos/4?lang=eng&id=p13#p13); [Amos 5:8–9](https://www.churchofjesuschrist.org/study/scriptures/ot/amos/5?lang=eng&id=p8-p9#p8)). It repeats the image of the land rising and falling like the Nile ([Amos 8:8](https://www.churchofjesuschrist.org/study/scriptures/ot/amos/8?lang=eng&id=p8#p8)). The God who built the heavens like a house with upper stories can make the earth melt with a touch.

**Question:** Each of Amos's three hymns ends with God's name. Why might Amos keep returning to praise even in a book of judgment?

### Amos 9:7–10 — Sifted, but Not a Grain Lost

"Are ye not as children of the Ethiopians unto me, O children of Israel? saith the Lord. Have not I brought up Israel out of the land of Egypt? and the Philistines from Caphtor, and the Syrians from Kir?" This is one of the most surprising verses in the Old Testament. The Lord had led other nations in their migrations too. The Exodus did not make Israel his only concern. Israel's privilege came with responsibility, not exemption ([Amos 3:2](https://www.churchofjesuschrist.org/study/scriptures/ot/amos/3?lang=eng&id=p2#p2)).

The Book of Mormon teaches the same breadth of concern: "Know ye not that there are more nations than one? Know ye not that I, the Lord your God, have created all men?" ([2 Nephi 29:7](https://www.churchofjesuschrist.org/study/scriptures/bofm/2-ne/29?lang=eng&id=p7#p7)); "the Lord esteemeth all flesh in one; he that is righteous is favored of God" ([1 Nephi 17:35](https://www.churchofjesuschrist.org/study/scriptures/bofm/1-ne/17?lang=eng&id=p35#p35)).

Verse 8: "the eyes of the Lord God are upon the sinful kingdom, and I will destroy it from off the face of the earth; saving that I will not utterly destroy the house of Jacob." The kingdom would end, but the people would not.

Verse 9: "For, lo, I will command, and I will sift the house of Israel among all nations, like as corn is sifted in a sieve, yet shall not the least grain fall upon the earth." Israel would be scattered and shaken, but the Lord would not lose a single grain. *Restoration reading:* Latter-day Saints read the scattering and gathering of Israel as continuing into the last days. Lehi compared Israel to an olive tree "whose branches should be broken off and should be scattered upon all the face of the earth" ([1 Nephi 10:12](https://www.churchofjesuschrist.org/study/scriptures/bofm/1-ne/10?lang=eng&id=p12#p12)), and he also prophesied its gathering.

Verse 10 limits the promise: "All the sinners of my people shall die by the sword, which say, The evil shall not overtake nor prevent us." The footnote gives "confront" for "prevent." Those who were sure that disaster would never reach them were the ones it would reach.

**Question:** The Lord sifts Israel "yet shall not the least grain fall upon the earth." What does this promise suggest about God's care for each individual, even in a time of scattering?

### Amos 9:11–12 — "I Will Raise Up the Tabernacle of David"

"In that day will I raise up the tabernacle of David that is fallen, and close up the breaches thereof; and I will raise up his ruins, and I will build it as in the days of old: That they may possess the remnant of Edom, and of all the heathen, which are called by my name, saith the Lord that doeth this."

The "tabernacle" (booth or shelter) of David is the royal house of David, pictured as a fallen hut. The Lord promises to rebuild it. In its setting, the promise means a restored kingdom under a son of David, reunited and extending its rule over neighboring nations, including Edom, "which are called by my name."

**James's use of the passage.** At the council in Jerusalem, the Apostle James quoted this prophecy to support admitting Gentiles to the Church without requiring them to be circumcised ([Acts 15:13–19](https://www.churchofjesuschrist.org/study/scriptures/nt/acts/15?lang=eng&id=p13-p19#p13)). His quotation reads: "After this I will return, and will build again the tabernacle of David, which is fallen down ... That the residue of men might seek after the Lord, and all the Gentiles, upon whom my name is called" ([Acts 15:16–17](https://www.churchofjesuschrist.org/study/scriptures/nt/acts/15?lang=eng&id=p16-p17#p16)). Compare the two wordings: where Amos says Israel will "possess the remnant of Edom, and of all the heathen," James says "the residue of men might seek after the Lord." James's wording seems to follow a different form of the text. The difference matters. Amos's own words describe a restored kingdom with authority over the nations. James, inspired by the conversion of Gentiles, saw in it the nations coming to seek the Lord. Both readings center on the phrase "called by my name." This guide keeps both: what Amos said to his hearers, and how an Apostle applied it to the Church.

*Christ connection:* The angel promised Mary that her son would receive "the throne of his father David" and that "of his kingdom there shall be no end" ([Luke 1:32–33](https://www.churchofjesuschrist.org/study/scriptures/nt/luke/1?lang=eng&id=p32-p33#p32); see [Isaiah 9:7](https://www.churchofjesuschrist.org/study/scriptures/ot/isa/9?lang=eng&id=p7#p7)). Christians see the fallen house of David raised up in Jesus Christ, the Son of David.

**Question:** James saw an ancient promise about David's house fulfilled in Gentiles joining the Church. How can studying how the Apostles read the Old Testament help us read it?

### Amos 9:13–15 — "The Plowman Shall Overtake the Reaper"

"Behold, the days come, saith the Lord, that the plowman shall overtake the reaper, and the treader of grapes him that soweth seed." The harvest would be so abundant that the reapers would still be harvesting when it was time to plow again. The law of Moses promised the same blessing: "your threshing shall reach unto the vintage, and the vintage shall reach unto the sowing time" ([Leviticus 26:5](https://www.churchofjesuschrist.org/study/scriptures/ot/lev/26?lang=eng&id=p5#p5)). "The mountains shall drop sweet wine" echoes Joel's promise that "the mountains shall drop down new wine" ([Joel 3:18](https://www.churchofjesuschrist.org/study/scriptures/ot/joel/3?lang=eng&id=p18#p18)).

Verse 14 reverses the curses of chapter 5. There, they built houses and planted vineyards they would not enjoy ([Amos 5:11](https://www.churchofjesuschrist.org/study/scriptures/ot/amos/5?lang=eng&id=p11#p11)). Here: "they shall build the waste cities, and inhabit them; and they shall plant vineyards, and drink the wine thereof."

Verse 15 ends the book: "And I will plant them upon their land, and they shall no more be pulled up out of their land which I have given them, saith the Lord thy God." The book that began with the Lord roaring in judgment ends with the Lord planting his people. The last words are "the Lord thy God": the covenant still stands.

**Question:** Amos's book is mostly judgment, but it ends with restoration. What does that teach about God's ultimate purpose, even in his warnings?

## Obadiah 1: Saviours on Mount Zion

Obadiah's single chapter has three movements. Verses 1–9 announce that proud Edom, secure in its mountain strongholds, will be brought down and betrayed by its own allies. Verses 10–14 give the reason: when Jerusalem fell, Edom stood by, gloated, looted, and handed over refugees. Verses 15–21 widen the view to "the day of the Lord" on all nations, ending with deliverance on Mount Zion, "saviours" who come up there, and the declaration that "the kingdom shall be the Lord's." Much of verses 1–9 closely resembles Jeremiah's prophecy against Edom (compare [Jeremiah 49:9](https://www.churchofjesuschrist.org/study/scriptures/ot/jer/49?lang=eng&id=p9#p9); [Jeremiah 49:14–16](https://www.churchofjesuschrist.org/study/scriptures/ot/jer/49?lang=eng&id=p14-p16#p14)).

### Obadiah 1:1–4 — "Though Thou Exalt Thyself as the Eagle"

"The vision of Obadiah. Thus saith the Lord God concerning Edom." The book calls itself a "vision," like Amos's "words ... which he saw" ([Amos 1:1](https://www.churchofjesuschrist.org/study/scriptures/ot/amos/1?lang=eng&id=p1#p1)). The prophet has "heard a rumour from the Lord": a messenger has gone among the nations to call them to war against Edom.

"The pride of thine heart hath deceived thee, thou that dwellest in the clefts of the rock, whose habitation is high; that saith in his heart, Who shall bring me down to the ground?" The Bible Dictionary places Edom in Mount Seir, south of the Dead Sea. Its people lived in rugged, rocky high country that seemed impossible to conquer. Their geography made them proud.

"Though thou exalt thyself as the eagle, and though thou set thy nest among the stars, thence will I bring thee down, saith the Lord." Isaiah used similar language for the king of Babylon, who said, "I will exalt my throne above the stars of God," and was told, "Yet thou shalt be brought down to hell" ([Isaiah 14:13–15](https://www.churchofjesuschrist.org/study/scriptures/ot/isa/14?lang=eng&id=p13-p15#p13)). Jeremiah's version of the Edom prophecy uses the same eagle's nest image ([Jeremiah 49:16](https://www.churchofjesuschrist.org/study/scriptures/ot/jer/49?lang=eng&id=p16#p16)).

*Christ connection:* Jesus taught, "whosoever exalteth himself shall be abased; and he that humbleth himself shall be exalted" ([Luke 14:11](https://www.churchofjesuschrist.org/study/scriptures/nt/luke/14?lang=eng&id=p11#p11)). Christ, who descended below all things, is the opposite of Edom's self-exaltation.

**Question:** Edom's pride "deceived" it into feeling safe. How can pride deceive us about our own security?

### Obadiah 1:5–9 — Stripped Bare and Betrayed by Allies

"If thieves came to thee, if robbers by night, (how art thou cut off!) would they not have stolen till they had enough? if the grapegatherers came to thee, would they not leave some grapes?" Even thieves take only what they want, and harvesters leave some grapes for gleaners. Edom's fall would be more complete than that. "How are the things of Esau searched out! how are his hidden things sought up!" Edom's hidden treasures would be found.

Verse 7 describes betrayal: "All the men of thy confederacy have brought thee even to the border: the men that were at peace with thee have deceived thee ... they that eat thy bread have laid a wound under thee." Edom's allies, the people who shared its table, would turn on it. Edom had betrayed its brother, and its own friends would betray it.

The phrase "they that eat thy bread" recalls the psalm, "mine own familiar friend, in whom I trusted, which did eat of my bread, hath lifted up his heel against me" ([Psalm 41:9](https://www.churchofjesuschrist.org/study/scriptures/ot/ps/41?lang=eng&id=p9#p9)), which Jesus applied to Judas ([John 13:18](https://www.churchofjesuschrist.org/study/scriptures/nt/john/13?lang=eng&id=p18#p18)).

Verses 8–9: "Shall I not in that day ... destroy the wise men out of Edom, and understanding out of the mount of Esau? And thy mighty men, O Teman, shall be dismayed." Edom was known for its wise men, and Teman was one of its chief places. Neither wisdom nor strength would save it.

**Question:** Edom was betrayed by the allies it trusted. What does this suggest about the kind of relationships that last, and the kind that do not?

### Obadiah 1:10–14 — "Thou Shouldest Not Have Looked On"

"For thy violence against thy brother Jacob shame shall cover thee, and thou shalt be cut off for ever." This is the heart of the book, and Scripture Central's KnoWhy #877 uses it as its theme verse. The word "brother" carries the charge. Esau and Jacob were twins ([Genesis 25:23–26](https://www.churchofjesuschrist.org/study/scriptures/ot/gen/25?lang=eng&id=p23-p26#p23)), and the two nations remembered it. The KnoWhy argues that because the Edomites were seen as kin, "the sense of betrayal would surely have run deeper."

Verse 11 names Edom's first sin: standing aside. "In the day that thou stoodest on the other side, in the day that the strangers carried away captive his forces, and foreigners entered into his gates, and cast lots upon Jerusalem, even thou wast as one of them." By doing nothing while strangers attacked its brother, Edom became one of the attackers.

Verses 12–14 list eight things Edom "should not" have done, each set "in the day" of Judah's disaster:

- looked on the day of his brother (verse 12)
- rejoiced over the children of Judah in the day of their destruction (verse 12)
- spoken proudly in the day of distress (verse 12)
- entered into the gate of my people in the day of their calamity (verse 13)
- looked on their affliction in the day of their calamity (verse 13)
- laid hands on their substance in the day of their calamity (verse 13)
- stood in the crossway, to cut off those of his that did escape (verse 14)
- delivered up those of his that did remain (verse 14)

The list moves from watching, to gloating, to looting, to capturing refugees at the crossroads and handing them over. Each step makes the next one easier. A psalm of the exile remembers the Edomites shouting, "Rase it, rase it" ([Psalm 137:7](https://www.churchofjesuschrist.org/study/scriptures/ot/ps/137?lang=eng&id=p7#p7)). Lamentations also addresses Edom ([Lamentations 4:21–22](https://www.churchofjesuschrist.org/study/scriptures/ot/lam/4?lang=eng&id=p21-p22#p21)).

*Christ connection:* In Jesus's parable, a priest and a Levite saw a wounded man and "passed by on the other side" ([Luke 10:31–32](https://www.churchofjesuschrist.org/study/scriptures/nt/luke/10?lang=eng&id=p31-p32#p31)). The Samaritan, from a people Jews considered enemies, stopped and helped. Edom is the opposite of the good Samaritan: a brother who saw and stood "on the other side."

The KnoWhy also points to the moment when Jacob and Esau were reconciled: "Esau ran to meet him, and embraced him, and fell on his neck, and kissed him: and they wept" ([Genesis 33:4](https://www.churchofjesuschrist.org/study/scriptures/ot/gen/33?lang=eng&id=p4#p4)). It concludes that Christ's Atonement can heal terrible wounds and help people find real forgiveness. The brothers had been reconciled once, and the nations that came from them could have been.

**Question:** Edom's sin began with standing by and watching. When is it wrong to do nothing? What helps you move from watching to helping?

### Obadiah 1:15–16 — "As Thou Hast Done, It Shall Be Done unto Thee"

"For the day of the Lord is near upon all the heathen: as thou hast done, it shall be done unto thee: thy reward shall return upon thine own head." The judgment on Edom becomes part of the day of the Lord on all nations. The principle is simple: what a person does comes back on them. Jesus taught, "with what measure ye mete, it shall be measured to you again" ([Matthew 7:2](https://www.churchofjesuschrist.org/study/scriptures/nt/matt/7?lang=eng&id=p2#p2)), and Paul, "whatsoever a man soweth, that shall he also reap" ([Galatians 6:7](https://www.churchofjesuschrist.org/study/scriptures/nt/gal/6?lang=eng&id=p7#p7)).

Verse 16: "For as ye have drunk upon my holy mountain, so shall all the heathen drink continually." The image is of drinking a cup. One reading is that Edom (the "ye" of the verse) drank in celebration on Jerusalem's holy mountain; another is that "ye" means Judah, which had drunk the cup of judgment. Either way, now the nations would drink the cup of God's judgment. Jeremiah used the image of the "wine cup of this fury" given to the nations ([Jeremiah 25:15](https://www.churchofjesuschrist.org/study/scriptures/ot/jer/25?lang=eng&id=p15#p15)), and Lamentations said to Edom, "the cup also shall pass through unto thee" ([Lamentations 4:21](https://www.churchofjesuschrist.org/study/scriptures/ot/lam/4?lang=eng&id=p21#p21)).

*Christ connection (devotional reading):* In Gethsemane, Jesus prayed that "this cup" might pass from him, "nevertheless not as I will, but as thou wilt" ([Matthew 26:39](https://www.churchofjesuschrist.org/study/scriptures/nt/matt/26?lang=eng&id=p39#p39)). Soon after, he said, "the cup which my Father hath given me, shall I not drink it?" ([John 18:11](https://www.churchofjesuschrist.org/study/scriptures/nt/john/18?lang=eng&id=p11#p11)). The cup of judgment that the nations deserved, he took on himself.

**Question:** "As thou hast done, it shall be done unto thee." How does the Savior's Atonement change what that principle means for those who repent?

### Obadiah 1:17–18 — "Upon Mount Zion Shall Be Deliverance"

"But upon mount Zion shall be deliverance, and there shall be holiness; and the house of Jacob shall possess their possessions." The fallen city will become a place of rescue and holiness. Joel, last week, said almost the same: "in mount Zion and in Jerusalem shall be deliverance, as the Lord hath said" ([Joel 2:32](https://www.churchofjesuschrist.org/study/scriptures/ot/joel/2?lang=eng&id=p32#p32)). Joel's phrase "as the Lord hath said" may point to this very promise, or both prophets may draw on an earlier one.

Verse 18: "the house of Jacob shall be a fire, and the house of Joseph a flame, and the house of Esau for stubble." Jacob (Judah) and Joseph (the northern tribes) appear together, as if the divided kingdoms are reunited. Malachi uses the image of the wicked as stubble in the day that "shall burn as an oven" ([Malachi 4:1](https://www.churchofjesuschrist.org/study/scriptures/ot/mal/4?lang=eng&id=p1#p1)).

**Question:** The place of Jerusalem's humiliation becomes a place of "deliverance" and "holiness." Where have you seen the Lord bring holiness out of a place of loss?

### Obadiah 1:19–20 — The Land Restored

These verses map out the restoration. People from the south (the Negev) would possess the mount of Esau. People from the lowland would possess the Philistine country. Israel would again hold the fields of Ephraim and Samaria, and Benjamin would possess Gilead. Exiles from the northern kingdom would possess Phoenicia "even unto Zarephath," the town where Elijah had been fed by a widow (see [1 Kings 17:9](https://www.churchofjesuschrist.org/study/scriptures/ot/1-kgs/17?lang=eng&id=p9#p9)). Exiles from Jerusalem "in Sepharad" would possess the cities of the south. The location of Sepharad is uncertain.

The details are local, but the point is broad: every part of the land that had been lost would be restored, and the exiles would come home.

**Question:** Obadiah names specific places that would be restored. What specific losses in your life or family do you hope the Lord will one day restore?

### Obadiah 1:21 — "Saviours Shall Come Up on Mount Zion"

"And saviours shall come up on mount Zion to judge the mount of Esau; and the kingdom shall be the Lord's." This is the most quoted verse of Obadiah among Latter-day Saints.

**In its ancient setting.** *Scripture Helps* explains that the Hebrew word for "saviour" refers to someone who saves, rescues, or delivers, and that in the Old Testament "mount Zion" usually means Jerusalem, especially the temple mount. In Israel's history, God sent such deliverers in time of need. In the days of the judges, "the Lord raised up a deliverer to the children of Israel" ([Judges 3:9](https://www.churchofjesuschrist.org/study/scriptures/ot/judg/3?lang=eng&id=p9#p9)). Nehemiah later recalled, "according to thy manifold mercies thou gavest them saviours, who saved them out of the hand of their enemies" ([Nehemiah 9:27](https://www.churchofjesuschrist.org/study/scriptures/ot/neh/9?lang=eng&id=p27#p27)). In Obadiah, the deliverers come up to Mount Zion and rule over Edom, and the final authority is the Lord's: "the kingdom shall be the Lord's."

**In Latter-day Saint teaching.** The Bible Dictionary says this verse refers to the doctrine of salvation for the dead. *Scripture Helps* presents the Prophet Joseph Smith's question of how the Saints become saviors on Mount Zion, and his answer, which begins, "By building their temples, erecting their baptismal fonts," and goes on to receiving ordinances on behalf of the dead. It quotes Elder Quentin L. Cook, who taught that we can "perform vicarious ordinances and truly become saviors on Mount Zion for our own families." The official lesson cites President Gordon B. Hinckley's teaching that we become saviors on Mount Zion when we receive ordinances in the temple for those who have died, and suggests the hymn "Holy Temples on Mount Zion" (*Hymns*, no. 289). It asks how this work is like the Savior's work.

Modern revelation teaches that the salvation of the dead "is necessary and essential to our salvation" because "they without us cannot be made perfect—neither can we without our dead be made perfect" ([Doctrine and Covenants 128:15](https://www.churchofjesuschrist.org/study/scriptures/dc-testament/dc/128?lang=eng&id=p15#p15)). The New Testament also teaches that "for this cause was the gospel preached also to them that are dead" ([1 Peter 4:6](https://www.churchofjesuschrist.org/study/scriptures/nt/1-pet/4?lang=eng&id=p6#p6)).

**Holding the layers together.** Obadiah's first hearers would likely have heard a promise of deliverers in a restored Jerusalem. Latter-day Saint leaders have taught that the verse also describes the work of the living for the dead in temples. Both readings share the core of the verse: people delivering others on God's holy mountain, under the Lord's kingdom.

*Christ connection:* Jesus Christ is the Savior. Anyone who acts as a "saviour" does so only by sharing in his work. In temples, the living do for others what those others cannot do for themselves, as Christ did for all.

"And the kingdom shall be the Lord's." John heard the same announcement in vision: "The kingdoms of this world are become the kingdoms of our Lord, and of his Christ; and he shall reign for ever and ever" ([Revelation 11:15](https://www.churchofjesuschrist.org/study/scriptures/nt/rev/11?lang=eng&id=p15#p15)).

**Question:** The official lesson asks how temple and family history work is like the Savior's work. When have you felt that you were helping someone receive what they could not receive alone?

## Jonah 1: Fleeing from the Presence of the Lord

The story of Jonah begins with a clear command and an immediate refusal. The Lord tells Jonah to go east to Nineveh, and Jonah sets sail west for Tarshish. A storm follows. While Jonah sleeps, the pagan sailors pray. Before the chapter ends, the sailors have turned to the Lord, Jonah has been thrown into the sea, and a great fish has swallowed him. The official lesson notes that even in this story of flight, the Lord had not given up on Jonah.

### Jonah 1:1–3 — "Jonah Rose Up to Flee unto Tarshish"

"Now the word of the Lord came unto Jonah the son of Amittai, saying, Arise, go to Nineveh, that great city, and cry against it; for their wickedness is come up before me." Jonah son of Amittai is the same prophet who foretold Jeroboam II's success ([2 Kings 14:25](https://www.churchofjesuschrist.org/study/scriptures/ot/2-kgs/14?lang=eng&id=p25#p25)). He had delivered good news to Israel before. Now he was sent with a warning to Israel's enemy.

"But Jonah rose up to flee unto Tarshish from the presence of the Lord." *Scripture Helps* notes that the location of Tarshish is unknown, that many scholars connect it with Tartessus in southern Spain, and that it lay in the opposite direction from Nineveh. Jonah was going as far as possible the other way. *Scripture Helps* also says Jonah's hometown, Gath-hepher, was more than 500 miles from Nineveh.

Notice a word the narrator repeats: Jonah "went down to Joppa," then "went down into" the ship ([Jonah 1:3](https://www.churchofjesuschrist.org/study/scriptures/ot/jonah/1?lang=eng&id=p3#p3)), then "was gone down into the sides of the ship" ([Jonah 1:5](https://www.churchofjesuschrist.org/study/scriptures/ot/jonah/1?lang=eng&id=p5#p5)). Later Jonah prays, "I went down to the bottoms of the mountains" ([Jonah 2:6](https://www.churchofjesuschrist.org/study/scriptures/ot/jonah/2?lang=eng&id=p6#p6)). Every step away from the Lord took him lower.

"So he paid the fare thereof." Running from the Lord cost him something.

The book does not yet tell us why Jonah fled. We learn that only in chapter 4. Duane Boyce, writing in *Interpreter*, cautions against hastily criticizing Jonah, given the cruelty of the Assyrians he was sent to condemn.

**Question:** The Institute manual asks, "Have you ever struggled to accept a calling or assignment from the Lord?" What makes some assignments hard to accept?

### Jonah 1:4–6 — "What Meanest Thou, O Sleeper?"

"But the Lord sent out a great wind into the sea, and there was a mighty tempest in the sea, so that the ship was like to be broken." Jonah had chosen the sea to flee the Lord. But the Lord was Lord of the sea too.

"Then the mariners were afraid, and cried every man unto his god, and cast forth the wares that were in the ship into the sea." The sailors, each with his own god, prayed and worked. Jonah, the prophet of the true God, slept below.

"So the shipmaster came to him, and said unto him, What meanest thou, O sleeper? arise, call upon thy God, if so be that God will think upon us, that we perish not." The captain's words "arise, call" echo the Lord's command "Arise, go ... and cry" ([Jonah 1:2](https://www.churchofjesuschrist.org/study/scriptures/ot/jonah/1?lang=eng&id=p2#p2)). A pagan captain now gave Jonah the same call he was running from. The captain's humble hope, "if so be that God will think upon us," resembles the king of Nineveh's "Who can tell" in [Jonah 3:9](https://www.churchofjesuschrist.org/study/scriptures/ot/jonah/3?lang=eng&id=p9#p9).

*Christ connection:* The Gospels tell of another sleeper in a storm. Jesus was "asleep on a pillow," and his disciples woke him, saying, "Master, carest thou not that we perish?" ([Mark 4:38](https://www.churchofjesuschrist.org/study/scriptures/nt/mark/4?lang=eng&id=p38#p38)). Jonah slept in flight from God. Jesus slept in perfect trust. Jonah's flight had brought the storm on the ship. Jesus "arose, and rebuked the wind, and said unto the sea, Peace, be still" ([Mark 4:39](https://www.churchofjesuschrist.org/study/scriptures/nt/mark/4?lang=eng&id=p39#p39)).

**Question:** The pagan captain called the prophet to prayer. Have you ever been reminded of your duty by someone you didn't expect?

### Jonah 1:7–10 — "I Fear the Lord, the God of Heaven"

The sailors cast lots to learn who had brought the storm, "and the lot fell upon Jonah." They asked a rush of questions: "What is thine occupation? and whence comest thou? what is thy country? and of what people art thou?"

Jonah answered: "I am an Hebrew; and I fear the Lord, the God of heaven, which hath made the sea and the dry land." His confession is true and full of irony. He claimed to fear the God who made the sea, while trying to escape him by sea.

"Then were the men exceedingly afraid, and said unto him, Why hast thou done this? For the men knew that he fled from the presence of the Lord, because he had told them." The sailors understood the seriousness of fleeing God better than the prophet seemed to.

Psalm 139 had already given the answer to Jonah's plan: "If I take the wings of the morning, and dwell in the uttermost parts of the sea; Even there shall thy hand lead me" ([Psalm 139:9–10](https://www.churchofjesuschrist.org/study/scriptures/ot/ps/139?lang=eng&id=p9-p10#p9)).

**Question:** Jonah could state true doctrine about God while running from him. How can a person know truth and still not live by it? What closes that gap?

### Jonah 1:11–16 — The Sailors Turn to the Lord

"What shall we do unto thee, that the sea may be calm unto us?" Jonah answered, "Take me up, and cast me forth into the sea; so shall the sea be calm unto you: for I know that for my sake this great tempest is upon you." Jonah accepted responsibility. He did not, however, offer to repent and go to Nineveh.

The sailors did not want to throw him overboard. "Nevertheless the men rowed hard to bring it to the land; but they could not." These men, who had no covenant with Israel's God, tried to save the man whose disobedience had endangered them.

Then they prayed, this time to the Lord by his name: "We beseech thee, O Lord, we beseech thee, let us not perish for this man's life, and lay not upon us innocent blood: for thou, O Lord, hast done as it pleased thee." They threw Jonah into the sea, "and the sea ceased from her raging."

Follow the sailors' fear through the chapter: they "were afraid" and each prayed to his own god (verse 5); they "were ... exceedingly afraid" when they learned Jonah had fled the Lord (verse 10); and finally "the men feared the Lord exceedingly, and offered a sacrifice unto the Lord, and made vows" (verse 16). Their fear moved from terror to reverence. The disobedient prophet's flight became the means by which a ship of foreigners came to worship the Lord.

*Christ connection:* When Jesus calmed the storm, the disciples "feared exceedingly, and said one to another, What manner of man is this, that even the wind and the sea obey him?" ([Mark 4:41](https://www.churchofjesuschrist.org/study/scriptures/nt/mark/4?lang=eng&id=p41#p41)). In both stories, people who saw the sea obey God were filled with reverent awe.

**Question:** The sailors show compassion, prayer, and reverence, even though they had not known the Lord. What can we learn from people outside our faith who act with integrity?

### Jonah 1:17 — "The Lord Had Prepared a Great Fish"

"Now the Lord had prepared a great fish to swallow up Jonah. And Jonah was in the belly of the fish three days and three nights."

The word "prepared" matters. The Lord "prepared" the fish here, and later he "prepared" a gourd ([Jonah 4:6](https://www.churchofjesuschrist.org/study/scriptures/ot/jonah/4?lang=eng&id=p6#p6)), a worm ([Jonah 4:7](https://www.churchofjesuschrist.org/study/scriptures/ot/jonah/4?lang=eng&id=p7#p7)), and an east wind ([Jonah 4:8](https://www.churchofjesuschrist.org/study/scriptures/ot/jonah/4?lang=eng&id=p8#p8)). In every case God arranges something to teach and save Jonah. The fish was not a punishment but a rescue: a drowning man was kept alive.

The text says "a great fish." In Matthew's Gospel the KJV reads "whale's belly" ([Matthew 12:40](https://www.churchofjesuschrist.org/study/scriptures/nt/matt/12?lang=eng&id=p40#p40)). The Bible does not name a species. The point of the story is not the animal but the God who prepared it.

*Christ connection:* Jesus used this verse as the sign of his own death and Resurrection: "For as Jonas was three days and three nights in the whale's belly; so shall the Son of man be three days and three nights in the heart of the earth" ([Matthew 12:40](https://www.churchofjesuschrist.org/study/scriptures/nt/matt/12?lang=eng&id=p40#p40); see [Matthew 16:4](https://www.churchofjesuschrist.org/study/scriptures/nt/matt/16?lang=eng&id=p4#p4); [Luke 11:30](https://www.churchofjesuschrist.org/study/scriptures/nt/luke/11?lang=eng&id=p30#p30)). The Bible Dictionary and *Scripture Helps* both point to this as a foreshadowing of the Savior's death, burial, and Resurrection. The comparison has limits: Jonah went down because he fled God's will, and Christ went down because he did it.

**Question:** The fish that swallowed Jonah was "prepared" by the Lord to save him. Has something that felt like being swallowed up turned out to be part of the Lord's rescue in your life?

## Jonah 2: Jonah's Prayer from the Deep

Chapter 2 is almost entirely a prayer. *Scripture Helps* describes it as a psalm of thanksgiving with many echoes of the Psalms, in which Jonah describes his sinking into the sea as a descent into death. Remarkably, the prayer is a thanksgiving, offered before Jonah reaches dry land. He gives thanks while he is still inside the fish, because he has been saved from drowning.

### Jonah 2:1–2 — "Out of the Belly of Hell Cried I"

"Then Jonah prayed unto the Lord his God out of the fish's belly, And said, I cried by reason of mine affliction unto the Lord, and he heard me; out of the belly of hell cried I, and thou heardest my voice." For the first time in the book, Jonah prays. The sailors prayed in chapter 1. Now Jonah does.

*Scripture Helps* explains that "hell" here translates the Hebrew *Sheol*, the realm of the dead. Jonah means that he was as good as dead, already in the grave's grasp. His words echo a psalm: "In my distress I cried unto the Lord, and he heard me" ([Psalm 120:1](https://www.churchofjesuschrist.org/study/scriptures/ot/ps/120?lang=eng&id=p1#p1)).

*Christ connection:* The psalmist said, "thou wilt not leave my soul in hell" ([Psalm 16:10](https://www.churchofjesuschrist.org/study/scriptures/ot/ps/16?lang=eng&id=p10#p10)), and Peter applied the verse to "the resurrection of Christ, that his soul was not left in hell" ([Acts 2:31](https://www.churchofjesuschrist.org/study/scriptures/nt/acts/2?lang=eng&id=p31#p31)). Jonah's "belly of hell" points toward the Savior, who went down into death and was not left there.

**Question:** Jonah prayed from a place where he could do nothing else. When has prayer been your only resource? What happened?

### Jonah 2:3–6 — "Yet I Will Look Again Toward Thy Holy Temple"

"For thou hadst cast me into the deep, in the midst of the seas; and the floods compassed me about: all thy billows and thy waves passed over me." Jonah saw the Lord's hand even in the sailors' act of throwing him overboard. "All thy billows and thy waves" repeats a psalm of distress almost word for word ([Psalm 42:7](https://www.churchofjesuschrist.org/study/scriptures/ot/ps/42?lang=eng&id=p7#p7)).

"Then I said, I am cast out of thy sight; yet I will look again toward thy holy temple." Jonah had fled "from the presence of the Lord." Now, feeling cast out of God's sight, he wants to look back toward the temple, the place of God's presence. Solomon's dedicatory prayer had asked the Lord to hear anyone who would "spread forth his hands toward this house" ([1 Kings 8:38](https://www.churchofjesuschrist.org/study/scriptures/ot/1-kgs/8?lang=eng&id=p38#p38)).

"The waters compassed me about, even to the soul." The footnote explains "even to the soul" as "to the point of death." "The weeds were wrapped about my head. I went down to the bottoms of the mountains; the earth with her bars was about me for ever." This is the lowest point of Jonah's going "down." The earth is pictured as a prison with bars.

"Yet hast thou brought up my life from corruption, O Lord my God." The turn comes with one word: "yet." Peter used the same language of "corruption" for Christ's Resurrection ([Acts 2:31](https://www.churchofjesuschrist.org/study/scriptures/nt/acts/2?lang=eng&id=p31#p31)).

**Question:** At his lowest point, Jonah said, "yet I will look again toward thy holy temple." What does it mean to "look again" toward the Lord after feeling cast out?

### Jonah 2:7–9 — "Salvation Is of the Lord"

"When my soul fainted within me I remembered the Lord: and my prayer came in unto thee, into thine holy temple." Remembering the Lord was the turning point.

Verse 8: "They that observe lying vanities forsake their own mercy." "Lying vanities" are idols, empty things that cannot save. Those who cling to them give up the mercy that could have been theirs. Matthew S. Holland's 2025 talk "Forsake Not Your Own Mercy," cited by the official lesson and *Scripture Helps*, takes its title from this verse.

There is irony here. Jonah contrasts himself with idol worshippers. Yet in chapter 1 the idol-worshipping sailors had already turned to the Lord and made vows, while Jonah fled. And in chapter 4 Jonah himself resents the mercy shown to Nineveh. The lesson of verse 8 applies to Jonah too.

Verse 9: "But I will sacrifice unto thee with the voice of thanksgiving; I will pay that that I have vowed. Salvation is of the Lord." Like the sailors, who "offered a sacrifice ... and made vows" ([Jonah 1:16](https://www.churchofjesuschrist.org/study/scriptures/ot/jonah/1?lang=eng&id=p16#p16)), Jonah now promises sacrifice and vows. "Salvation is of the Lord" is the center of the prayer and perhaps of the whole book. Compare "Salvation belongeth unto the Lord" ([Psalm 3:8](https://www.churchofjesuschrist.org/study/scriptures/ot/ps/3?lang=eng&id=p8#p8)).

*Christ connection:* "Salvation is of the Lord" points to Jesus Christ, through whom salvation comes. Jonah could not save himself from the sea; no one can save himself from death and sin.

**Question:** Jonah warns against forsaking "their own mercy." What "lying vanities" might lead people to give up the mercy God offers them?

### Jonah 2:10 — Dry Land

"And the Lord spake unto the fish, and it vomited out Jonah upon the dry land." The Lord speaks, and the fish obeys at once, as the sea, the wind, and the fish have obeyed all along. Only the prophet has been slow to obey. The image is not dignified. Jonah's rescue is complete, but it is not glorious.

Jonah had confessed the Lord as the God "which hath made the sea and the dry land" ([Jonah 1:9](https://www.churchofjesuschrist.org/study/scriptures/ot/jonah/1?lang=eng&id=p9#p9)). He is now back on that dry land, alive, and ready to hear the Lord's call again.

**Question:** Jonah's rescue was undignified but complete. Why might the Lord's deliverance sometimes humble us even as it saves us?

## Jonah 3: The Second Time, and a City Repents

Chapter 3 mirrors chapter 1. The word of the Lord comes again with nearly the same command, and this time Jonah obeys. His message is short and contains no offer of mercy. Yet the people of the city, from the greatest to the least, believe God and turn from their evil way, the king's fasting decree reaches even the animals, and God spares the city. The official lesson and *Scripture Helps* both see in this chapter the Lord's mercy to all who turn to him.

### Jonah 3:1–4 — "Yet Forty Days, and Nineveh Shall Be Overthrown"

"And the word of the Lord came unto Jonah the second time." The command is repeated almost word for word: "Arise, go unto Nineveh, that great city, and preach unto it the preaching that I bid thee." The Lord gave Jonah a second chance. *Scripture Helps* lists an *Ensign* article on this theme, Juan Uceda's "Jonah and the Second Chance."

"So Jonah arose, and went unto Nineveh, according to the word of the Lord. Now Nineveh was an exceeding great city of three days' journey." The footnotes give the Hebrew as "great city to God" and explain the three days' journey as "through greater Nineveh with its environs." The city was great in size, and perhaps also great in God's sight.

"And Jonah began to enter into the city a day's journey, and he cried, and said, Yet forty days, and Nineveh shall be overthrown." Jonah's whole recorded sermon is one sentence. It contains no call to repent and no promise of mercy. In scripture, forty often marks a period of testing: the rain of the Flood ([Genesis 7:12](https://www.churchofjesuschrist.org/study/scriptures/ot/gen/7?lang=eng&id=p12#p12)), Israel's years in the wilderness ([Amos 2:10](https://www.churchofjesuschrist.org/study/scriptures/ot/amos/2?lang=eng&id=p10#p10)), and the Savior's fast ([Matthew 4:2](https://www.churchofjesuschrist.org/study/scriptures/nt/matt/4?lang=eng&id=p2#p2)). Forty days gave Nineveh time to respond.

Jeremiah later explained the principle behind such warnings: "If that nation, against whom I have pronounced, turn from their evil, I will repent of the evil that I thought to do unto them" ([Jeremiah 18:7–8](https://www.churchofjesuschrist.org/study/scriptures/ot/jer/18?lang=eng&id=p7-p8#p7)). A prophecy of destruction can be a call to repent, even when it does not say so.

**Question:** The Lord gave Jonah a second chance and gave Nineveh forty days. What does this suggest about how the Lord gives people time to change?

### Jonah 3:5–9 — "Who Can Tell If God Will Turn?"

"So the people of Nineveh believed God, and proclaimed a fast, and put on sackcloth, from the greatest of them even to the least of them." The people responded before the king did. Their repentance began with belief.

When word reached the king, "he arose from his throne, and he laid his robe from him, and covered him with sackcloth, and sat in ashes." The most powerful man in the city came down from his throne to sit in ashes. He issued a decree: no one, "neither man nor beast, herd nor flock," was to eat or drink. "But let man and beast be covered with sackcloth, and cry mightily unto God: yea, let them turn every one from his evil way, and from the violence that is in their hands." The decree reached even the animals, but the turning it called for was from the people's "evil way" and "violence." The king named the city's sin. That fits what the official lesson says of Nineveh's reputation.

"Who can tell if God will turn and repent, and turn away from his fierce anger, that we perish not?" The king did not presume on God's mercy. The JST footnote rephrases the verse so that the people are the ones who repent: "we will repent, and turn unto God, but he will turn away from us his fierce anger." Joel's call to repentance used nearly the same words: "Who knoweth if he will return and repent" ([Joel 2:14](https://www.churchofjesuschrist.org/study/scriptures/ot/joel/2?lang=eng&id=p14#p14)).

*Christ connection:* Jesus told his generation that "the men of Nineveh shall rise in judgment with this generation, and shall condemn it: because they repented at the preaching of Jonas; and, behold, a greater than Jonas is here" ([Matthew 12:41](https://www.churchofjesuschrist.org/study/scriptures/nt/matt/12?lang=eng&id=p41#p41); see [Luke 11:32](https://www.churchofjesuschrist.org/study/scriptures/nt/luke/11?lang=eng&id=p32#p32)). Nineveh repented at a one-sentence warning from a reluctant prophet. Jesus's hearers had the Son of God in person.

**Question:** Nineveh repented "from the greatest of them even to the least of them." What does the king's example teach about humility and leadership in repentance?

### Jonah 3:10 — "God Saw Their Works"

"And God saw their works, that they turned from their evil way; and God repented of the evil, that he had said that he would do unto them; and he did it not." God responded not to their sackcloth alone but to their "works": they "turned from their evil way."

As in Amos 7, the KJV says God "repented." In its entry on Amos 7, *Scripture Helps* explains that the Hebrew word can mean to relent or forbear, and it points readers to this verse for comparison ([Amos 7:1–6](https://www.churchofjesuschrist.org/study/scriptures/ot/amos/7?lang=eng&id=p1-p6#p1)). The JST footnote rephrases the verse: the people turned from their "evil way and repented; and God turned away the evil that he had said he would bring upon them." "Evil" here means disaster, not sin. Scripture also says God "is not a man, that he should repent" ([1 Samuel 15:29](https://www.churchofjesuschrist.org/study/scriptures/ot/1-sam/15?lang=eng&id=p29#p29)). The JST readings make the same point: God's character does not change. Because God is consistent and merciful, he responds to people who change.

**Question:** God "saw their works." What "works" show that a person's repentance is real?

## Jonah 4: Should Not I Spare Nineveh?

The book's last chapter reveals why Jonah fled. He was not afraid that Nineveh would reject him. He was afraid that God would forgive it. Jonah is angry, the Lord asks him questions, and through a plant, a worm, and a hot wind, the Lord teaches him about mercy. The book ends with the Lord's question, unanswered. The Bible Dictionary says the key to the whole book is here.

### Jonah 4:1–3 — "I Knew That Thou Art a Gracious God"

"But it displeased Jonah exceedingly, and he was very angry." The official lesson asks why Jonah was "displeased" and "very angry" at the Lord's mercy.

Jonah tells us: "I pray thee, O Lord, was not this my saying, when I was yet in my country? Therefore I fled before unto Tarshish: for I knew that thou art a gracious God, and merciful, slow to anger, and of great kindness, and repentest thee of the evil." Jonah is quoting Israel's great confession of who God is. When the Lord passed before Moses, he proclaimed himself "merciful and gracious, longsuffering, and abundant in goodness and truth" ([Exodus 34:6](https://www.churchofjesuschrist.org/study/scriptures/ot/ex/34?lang=eng&id=p6#p6)). Joel used nearly the same words as Jonah: "he is gracious and merciful, slow to anger, and of great kindness, and repenteth him of the evil" ([Joel 2:13](https://www.churchofjesuschrist.org/study/scriptures/ot/joel/2?lang=eng&id=p13#p13)). For Joel, those words were good news. For Jonah, when applied to Nineveh, they were a complaint.

The footnote to "repentest" gives "relentest" and adds: "Jonah knew that God could revoke the calamity decreed, but expected He would do so even without the repentance of the people."

"Therefore now, O Lord, take, I beseech thee, my life from me; for it is better for me to die than to live." Elijah once made a similar request in despair ([1 Kings 19:4](https://www.churchofjesuschrist.org/study/scriptures/ot/1-kgs/19?lang=eng&id=p4#p4)). Here Jonah's words come in his anger over Nineveh. The Lord does not grant the request. He keeps teaching.

*A note of care:* Jonah's words "it is better for me to die than to live" are part of a story, but some readers may know feelings like these firsthand. If you have thoughts of ending your life, please reach out now to someone you trust, a Church leader, a medical professional, or a local crisis line or emergency service. The Lord did not leave Jonah alone in that moment, and you do not have to be alone either.

**Question:** Jonah knew God was merciful and was angry about it. Why can it be hard to rejoice when God shows mercy to people we think do not deserve it?

### Jonah 4:4–5 — "Doest Thou Well to Be Angry?"

"Then said the Lord, Doest thou well to be angry?" The Lord answers Jonah's anger with a question, not a rebuke. The Lord once asked Cain, "Why art thou wroth? and why is thy countenance fallen?" ([Genesis 4:6](https://www.churchofjesuschrist.org/study/scriptures/ot/gen/4?lang=eng&id=p6#p6)). Both questions invite the angry person to look at his own heart.

Jonah does not answer. "So Jonah went out of the city, and sat on the east side of the city, and there made him a booth, and sat under it in the shadow, till he might see what would become of the city." The footnote gives "shelter" for "booth." Jonah, it seems, still hoped the city would be destroyed. He sat outside to watch.

**Question:** The Lord asked Jonah, "Doest thou well to be angry?" How might asking yourself that question change the way you handle anger?

### Jonah 4:6–9 — The Gourd, the Worm, and the East Wind

"And the Lord God prepared a gourd, and made it to come up over Jonah, that it might be a shadow over his head, to deliver him from his grief. So Jonah was exceeding glad of the gourd." The footnote gives "castor bean plant" for "gourd." Jonah had been "displeased ... exceedingly" at Nineveh's rescue ([Jonah 4:1](https://www.churchofjesuschrist.org/study/scriptures/ot/jonah/4?lang=eng&id=p1#p1)). Now he is "exceeding glad" over a plant that gave him shade.

"But God prepared a worm when the morning rose the next day, and it smote the gourd that it withered." Then "God prepared a vehement east wind; and the sun beat upon the head of Jonah, that he fainted, and wished in himself to die." This second wish to die comes as Jonah faints under the sun and the hot wind, in physical distress as well as anger.

The Lord asks his question a second time, now about the plant: "Doest thou well to be angry for the gourd?" This time Jonah answers: "I do well to be angry, even unto death."

*Scripture Helps* explains that Jonah's reaction shows misplaced priorities: he cared more about a plant that gave him temporary comfort than about a city full of people. Through the plant, the Lord shows that he cares for Nineveh even if Jonah does not.

**Question:** Jonah grieved more for a plant than for a city. What comforts might we care about more than people?

### Jonah 4:10–11 — "Should Not I Spare Nineveh?"

"Then said the Lord, Thou hast had pity on the gourd, for the which thou hast not laboured, neither madest it grow; which came up in a night, and perished in a night: And should not I spare Nineveh, that great city, wherein are more than sixscore thousand persons that cannot discern between their right hand and their left hand; and also much cattle?"

The Lord's argument moves from lesser to greater. Jonah pitied a plant he had not planted or tended, which lived for a day. Should not God pity a city of people he had made? "Sixscore thousand" is 120,000. The phrase "cannot discern between their right hand and their left hand" may refer to young children, or to people who lacked moral understanding. Either way, they were people God saw as needing his patience. And "much cattle": God even cares about the animals.

The book ends with that question. We never hear Jonah's answer. The Bible Dictionary describes God's tenderness toward the prophet and toward the children of Nineveh, and says the book opposes a narrowmindedness that would confine God's love to one nation. The question is left open for the reader to answer.

*Christ connection:* Jesus's parable of the prodigal son also ends with an angry son and an open question. When the father welcomed the younger son home, the older brother "was angry, and would not go in: therefore came his father out, and entreated him" ([Luke 15:28](https://www.churchofjesuschrist.org/study/scriptures/nt/luke/15?lang=eng&id=p28#p28)). The father said, "It was meet that we should make merry, and be glad: for this thy brother was dead, and is alive again" ([Luke 15:32](https://www.churchofjesuschrist.org/study/scriptures/nt/luke/15?lang=eng&id=p32#p32)). We are not told whether the older son went in. Like Jonah, the older brother is invited to share the Father's joy over mercy. Jesus taught, "Be ye therefore merciful, as your Father also is merciful" ([Luke 6:36](https://www.churchofjesuschrist.org/study/scriptures/nt/luke/6?lang=eng&id=p36#p36)). The Lord is "not willing that any should perish, but that all should come to repentance" ([2 Peter 3:9](https://www.churchofjesuschrist.org/study/scriptures/nt/2-pet/3?lang=eng&id=p9#p9)).

The official lesson compares Jonah with the sons of Mosiah, who went to preach to the Lamanites, a people long seen as enemies, because "they could not bear that any human soul should perish" ([Mosiah 28:3](https://www.churchofjesuschrist.org/study/scriptures/bofm/mosiah/28?lang=eng&id=p3#p3)). Ammon later rejoiced that "God is mindful of every people, whatsoever land they may be in" ([Alma 26:37](https://www.churchofjesuschrist.org/study/scriptures/bofm/alma/26?lang=eng&id=p37#p37)).

**Question:** The Lord's last question to Jonah is also a question to us. Is there a person or group you find it hard to want God to forgive? How would you answer the Lord's question?

Ponder the official lesson's question: Because the Lord reveals truth through prophets, what conclusions can we draw when a prophet speaks?

**Tuesday, November 24 — Amos 4–5: Seek the Lord, and live.** Mark the five times "yet have ye not returned unto me" appears in chapter 4. Read [Amos 5:4–15](https://www.churchofjesuschrist.org/study/scriptures/ot/amos/5?lang=eng&id=p4-p15#p4) and underline every form of "seek." Read [Amos 5:21–24](https://www.churchofjesuschrist.org/study/scriptures/ot/amos/5?lang=eng&id=p21-p24#p21) and consider what "judgment ... as waters" would look like in your home or community.

**Wednesday, November 25 — Amos 6–7: Ease, intercession, and a prophet's call.** Read [Amos 6:1–7](https://www.churchofjesuschrist.org/study/scriptures/ot/amos/6?lang=eng&id=p1-p7#p1) and list what the leaders enjoyed and what they ignored. Read Amos's two prayers in [Amos 7:1–6](https://www.churchofjesuschrist.org/study/scriptures/ot/amos/7?lang=eng&id=p1-p6#p1) and his answer to Amaziah in [Amos 7:14–15](https://www.churchofjesuschrist.org/study/scriptures/ot/amos/7?lang=eng&id=p14-p15#p14). Ponder how the Lord has called you.

**Thursday, November 26 — Amos 8–9: Famine and restoration.** Read [Amos 8:11–12](https://www.churchofjesuschrist.org/study/scriptures/ot/amos/8?lang=eng&id=p11-p12#p11) with *Scripture Helps*' notes on the apostasy and the Restoration. Read [Amos 9:11–15](https://www.churchofjesuschrist.org/study/scriptures/ot/amos/9?lang=eng&id=p11-p15#p11) and then [Acts 15:13–19](https://www.churchofjesuschrist.org/study/scriptures/nt/acts/15?lang=eng&id=p13-p19#p13). Notice the difference between Amos's words and James's quotation, and what James saw in the passage.

**Friday, November 27 — Obadiah: Brothers and bystanders.** Read the whole book (it takes only a few minutes). List the eight things Edom "should not" have done in verses 12–14. Then read [Obadiah 1:21](https://www.churchofjesuschrist.org/study/scriptures/ot/obad/1?lang=eng&id=p21#p21) and the Latter-day Saint teachings about saviors on Mount Zion. If possible, plan a time for temple or family history work.

**Saturday, November 28 — Jonah 1–2: Fleeing and praying.** Trace the word "down" through chapters 1–2. Notice how the sailors' fear changes in chapter 1. Read Jonah's prayer in chapter 2 and [Matthew 12:39–41](https://www.churchofjesuschrist.org/study/scriptures/nt/matt/12?lang=eng&id=p39-p41#p39).

**Sunday, November 29 — Jonah 3–4: Mercy to all.** Compare [Jonah 4:2](https://www.churchofjesuschrist.org/study/scriptures/ot/jonah/4?lang=eng&id=p2#p2) with [Exodus 34:6](https://www.churchofjesuschrist.org/study/scriptures/ot/ex/34?lang=eng&id=p6#p6) and [Joel 2:13](https://www.churchofjesuschrist.org/study/scriptures/ot/joel/2?lang=eng&id=p13#p13). Read the Lord's last question ([Jonah 4:10–11](https://www.churchofjesuschrist.org/study/scriptures/ot/jonah/4?lang=eng&id=p10-p11#p10)) and the end of the parable of the prodigal son ([Luke 15:25–32](https://www.churchofjesuschrist.org/study/scriptures/nt/luke/15?lang=eng&id=p25-p32#p25)). Ponder the official lesson's question: How can you be more merciful?

**For a 40-minute class or family discussion:**

1. *Prophets and the roaring lion (10 minutes):* Read [Amos 3:3–8](https://www.churchofjesuschrist.org/study/scriptures/ot/amos/3?lang=eng&id=p3-p8#p3). Ask the class to explain each question's cause and effect, then discuss verse 7. Share Elder Soares's words, quoted in the official lesson: "having prophets is a sign of God's love."
2. *Seek the Lord, and live (8 minutes):* Read [Amos 5:4–6](https://www.churchofjesuschrist.org/study/scriptures/ot/amos/5?lang=eng&id=p4-p6#p4), [Amos 5:14–15](https://www.churchofjesuschrist.org/study/scriptures/ot/amos/5?lang=eng&id=p14-p15#p14), and [Amos 5:21–24](https://www.churchofjesuschrist.org/study/scriptures/ot/amos/5?lang=eng&id=p21-p24#p21). Discuss the difference between religious activity and seeking the Lord.
3. *Famine of the word (7 minutes):* Read [Amos 8:11–12](https://www.churchofjesuschrist.org/study/scriptures/ot/amos/8?lang=eng&id=p11-p12#p11). Discuss the official lesson's question: why is famine a good comparison for living without the word of God?
4. *Saviors on Mount Zion (5 minutes):* Read [Obadiah 1:21](https://www.churchofjesuschrist.org/study/scriptures/ot/obad/1?lang=eng&id=p21#p21). Share how temple and family history work lets us help others receive what they cannot receive alone.
5. *Jonah and the Lord's mercy (10 minutes):* Briefly retell Jonah 1–3. Read [Jonah 4:1–4](https://www.churchofjesuschrist.org/study/scriptures/ot/jonah/4?lang=eng&id=p1-p4#p1) and [Jonah 4:10–11](https://www.churchofjesuschrist.org/study/scriptures/ot/jonah/4?lang=eng&id=p10-p11#p10). Invite people to answer the Lord's question privately. Close with testimony of the Savior, whose sign was "the sign of the prophet Jonas" ([Matthew 12:39](https://www.churchofjesuschrist.org/study/scriptures/nt/matt/12?lang=eng&id=p39#p39)).
