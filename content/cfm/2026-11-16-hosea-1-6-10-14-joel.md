# I Will Love Them Freely

This guide covers the *Come, Follow Me* reading for **November 16–22, 2026**: Hosea 1–6; 10–14 and Joel 1–3 ([official lesson](https://www.churchofjesuschrist.org/study/manual/come-follow-me-for-home-and-church-old-testament-2026/47?lang=eng)). It is a personal Latter-day Saint study guide. It is not an official publication of The Church of Jesus Christ of Latter-day Saints. Scripture quotations come from the King James Version and the other standard works of the Church. The guide goes through all 223 assigned verses in order, in small groups: 150 verses of Hosea and all 73 verses of Joel. Each group gets an explanation, cross-references, labeled Christ connections where they fit, and a question for personal study or discussion.

## Historical and Literary Context

**A prophet to the northern kingdom.** After Solomon's death the Israelite kingdom split in two. The northern kingdom, called Israel and often "Ephraim" after its leading tribe, had its capital at Samaria. The southern kingdom, Judah, kept Jerusalem and its temple. The Bible Dictionary calls Hosea "the only prophet of the northern kingdom who has left written prophecies" ([Bible Dictionary, "Hosea, or Hoshea"](https://www.churchofjesuschrist.org/study/scriptures/bd/hosea?lang=eng)). His name means salvation, or "Jehovah saves" ([Scripture Helps: Hosea 1–6; 10–14; Joel](https://www.churchofjesuschrist.org/study/manual/scripture-helps-old-testament/45-hosea-1-6-10-14-joel?lang=eng)). When Hosea calls the nation "Ephraim," he is speaking to the same people as when he says "Israel." "Judah" means the southern kingdom, which he mentions more briefly.

**When Hosea preached.** [Hosea 1:1](https://www.churchofjesuschrist.org/study/scriptures/ot/hosea/1?lang=eng&id=p1#p1) dates his work to the days of Jeroboam (Jeroboam II) in Israel and of four kings of Judah, from Uzziah to Hezekiah. Jeroboam II reigned forty-one years and restored Israel's borders ([2 Kings 14:23–25](https://www.churchofjesuschrist.org/study/scriptures/ot/2-kgs/14?lang=eng&id=p23-p25#p23)). It was a time of prosperity and confidence. After his death the kingdom fell apart quickly: his son Zachariah reigned six months before he was murdered ([2 Kings 15:8–10](https://www.churchofjesuschrist.org/study/scriptures/ot/2-kgs/15?lang=eng&id=p8-p10#p8)), and several more kings followed in a short span, most of them by assassination. Kings bought off Assyria with tribute ([2 Kings 15:19–20](https://www.churchofjesuschrist.org/study/scriptures/ot/2-kgs/15?lang=eng&id=p19-p20#p19)), lost territory to it ([2 Kings 15:29](https://www.churchofjesuschrist.org/study/scriptures/ot/2-kgs/15?lang=eng&id=p29#p29)), and then looked to Egypt for help ([2 Kings 17:3–4](https://www.churchofjesuschrist.org/study/scriptures/ot/2-kgs/17?lang=eng&id=p3-p4#p3)). Finally "the king of Assyria took Samaria, and carried Israel away into Assyria" ([2 Kings 17:6](https://www.churchofjesuschrist.org/study/scriptures/ot/2-kgs/17?lang=eng&id=p6#p6)). The Bible Dictionary says Hosea began late in Jeroboam II's reign and "probably died before the accession of Pekah, 736 B.C.," because he never mentions the later wars; *Scripture Helps* places him in the latter half of the eighth century B.C., when Assyria was close to conquering Israel. Wally Breitenstein's *Old Testament Minute* commentary gives about 760 to 720 B.C. and notes that Hosea was a contemporary of Isaiah ([Breitenstein, *Old Testament Minute: Hosea*](https://scripturecentral.org/archive/books/book/old-testament-minute-hosea)). The sources differ on how long his ministry lasted, but they agree on its setting: a prosperous nation sliding toward collapse.

**What went wrong in Israel.** Two problems run through the book. The first is worship. Jeroboam I, the first northern king, set up golden calves at Bethel and Dan and told the people, "behold thy gods, O Israel, which brought thee up out of the land of Egypt" ([1 Kings 12:28–29](https://www.churchofjesuschrist.org/study/scriptures/ot/1-kgs/12?lang=eng&id=p28-p29#p28)). Over time Israel also turned to Baal, the Canaanite storm god credited with rain and harvests. The second problem is how people treated one another: lying, violence, stealing, adultery, corrupt priests, dishonest trade ([Hosea 4:1–2](https://www.churchofjesuschrist.org/study/scriptures/ot/hosea/4?lang=eng&id=p1-p2#p1); [Hosea 12:7](https://www.churchofjesuschrist.org/study/scriptures/ot/hosea/12?lang=eng&id=p7#p7)). Hosea treats the two as one sickness. People who forgot the Lord also forgot mercy. A later summary of Israel's fall makes the same diagnosis: they "had sinned against the Lord their God ... and had feared other gods," though the Lord had warned them "by all the prophets, and by all the seers" ([2 Kings 17:7](https://www.churchofjesuschrist.org/study/scriptures/ot/2-kgs/17?lang=eng&id=p7#p7), [13](https://www.churchofjesuschrist.org/study/scriptures/ot/2-kgs/17?lang=eng&id=p13#p13)).

**A marriage as a message.** Hosea's prophecy begins with his own family. The Lord commanded him to marry, and the unfaithfulness of his wife, Gomer, became a picture of Israel's unfaithfulness to the Lord. The official lesson explains that Israel's covenant "was meant to be so deep and meaningful that the Lord compared it to a marriage" ([official lesson](https://www.churchofjesuschrist.org/study/manual/come-follow-me-for-home-and-church-old-testament-2026/47?lang=eng)). Readers have long asked whether Hosea's marriage was a real event or a symbolic story. *Scripture Helps* notes that scholars debate the question and says the central message is the covenant the marriage represents, not the marriage itself. This guide follows the text as written and does not try to settle the question.

**Reading the marriage imagery with care.** Some verses in Hosea 2 are hard to read. They describe a husband threatening to strip, expose, and punish his unfaithful wife. These lines need a careful reading:

- **The wife is a nation, and the threats are judgments on that nation.** Hosea says so in his first command: "the land hath committed great whoredom, departing from the Lord" ([Hosea 1:2](https://www.churchofjesuschrist.org/study/scriptures/ot/hosea/1?lang=eng&id=p2#p2)). The "lovers" are false gods and foreign alliances. The stripping and the dry land of chapter 2 describe what would happen to Israel when crops failed and armies came. The "wife" includes the kings, priests, and men of the nation; in fact Hosea blames the men most sharply ([Hosea 4:14](https://www.churchofjesuschrist.org/study/scriptures/ot/hosea/4?lang=eng&id=p14#p14)).
- **The imagery reflects its ancient world.** The prophet used the language of marriage and public shame that his hearers knew to show how serious covenant betrayal was. The text describes God's judgment through a metaphor. It does not give husbands a pattern to follow.
- **No one may use these verses to justify abuse.** Restoration scripture teaches that no power or influence "can or ought to be maintained" except "by persuasion, by long-suffering, by gentleness and meekness, and by love unfeigned" ([Doctrine and Covenants 121:41](https://www.churchofjesuschrist.org/study/scriptures/dc-testament/dc/121?lang=eng&id=p41#p41)). The New Testament tells husbands to love their wives "even as Christ also loved the church, and gave himself for it" ([Ephesians 5:25](https://www.churchofjesuschrist.org/study/scriptures/nt/eph/5?lang=eng&id=p25#p25)). Nothing in Hosea permits anyone to harm, shame, isolate, or control a spouse. It does not ask a person who is being harmed to stay in danger, and it does not teach that someone who is abused must have earned it. Anyone being harmed should seek safety and help from trusted people and from local support and emergency resources.
- **The chapter moves toward tenderness.** The same chapter that warns of judgment turns to "I will allure her ... and speak comfortably unto her" and "I will betroth thee unto me for ever" ([Hosea 2:14](https://www.churchofjesuschrist.org/study/scriptures/ot/hosea/2?lang=eng&id=p14#p14), [19](https://www.churchofjesuschrist.org/study/scriptures/ot/hosea/2?lang=eng&id=p19#p19)). The marriage picture is ultimately about a God who keeps his covenant when his people do not.

**Hesed: covenant love.** One Hebrew word helps unlock the book. *Scripture Helps* explains that *hesed*, translated "lovingkindness" in [Hosea 2:19](https://www.churchofjesuschrist.org/study/scriptures/ot/hosea/2?lang=eng&id=p19#p19) and "mercy" in [Hosea 4:1](https://www.churchofjesuschrist.org/study/scriptures/ot/hosea/4?lang=eng&id=p1#p1) and [Hosea 6:6](https://www.churchofjesuschrist.org/study/scriptures/ot/hosea/6?lang=eng&id=p6#p6), is the Lord's covenant love: loyal, steady, and merciful. It also cites President Russell M. Nelson's teaching that because God has *hesed* for those who make covenants with him, he will keep loving them and working with them, forgive them when they repent, and help them return when they stray. Hosea's charge against Israel is that "there is no truth, nor mercy [*hesed*], nor knowledge of God in the land" ([Hosea 4:1](https://www.churchofjesuschrist.org/study/scriptures/ot/hosea/4?lang=eng&id=p1#p1)). His hope is that God's *hesed* will outlast Israel's betrayal.

**The shape of Hosea.** *Scripture Helps* divides the book into two parts. Chapters 1–3 tell the story of Hosea's marriage and children and its meaning. Chapters 4–14 are a long series of warnings and promises. This week's reading skips chapters 7–9, which continue the same charges of false worship, foreign alliances, and coming exile. Throughout the book judgment and mercy alternate, often abruptly. A threat is followed by a promise, and the book ends with an invitation: "O Israel, return unto the Lord thy God" ([Hosea 14:1](https://www.churchofjesuschrist.org/study/scriptures/ot/hosea/14?lang=eng&id=p1#p1)). The Bible Dictionary sums up the message this way: "Hosea's fundamental idea is the love of God for His people."

**Joel: a prophet of Judah, date unknown.** Joel addressed the southern kingdom of Judah, but no king is named in his book. The Bible Dictionary says his date "is uncertain; it may have been spoken as early as the reign of Joash, before 850 B.C., or even so late as after the Return" from Babylon ([Bible Dictionary, "Joel"](https://www.churchofjesuschrist.org/study/scriptures/bd/joel?lang=eng)). *Scripture Helps* lists his date as uncertain, probably somewhere between the ninth and fifth centuries B.C. Because his book is not tied to a particular reign, it reads easily as a message for any generation, including the last days.

**What Joel is about.** "The occasion of the prophecy," says the Bible Dictionary, "was a severe visitation of drought and locusts." Joel treats that disaster as a warning of "the day of the Lord," a day of judgment for the wicked and deliverance for the faithful. He calls the people to fast, gather, and "rend your heart, and not your garments" ([Joel 2:13](https://www.churchofjesuschrist.org/study/scriptures/ot/joel/2?lang=eng&id=p13#p13)). Then the book turns to promises: restored harvests, the Spirit poured out "upon all flesh," judgment on the nations, and the Lord dwelling in Zion. Joel's prophecy of the Spirit was quoted twice in pivotal moments: by Peter on the day of Pentecost ([Acts 2:16–21](https://www.churchofjesuschrist.org/study/scriptures/nt/acts/2?lang=eng&id=p16-p21#p16)) and by the angel Moroni to Joseph Smith in 1823 ([Joseph Smith—History 1:41](https://www.churchofjesuschrist.org/study/scriptures/pgp/js-h/1?lang=eng&id=p41#p41)).

**Two kinds of "return."** Both prophets use the language of turning and returning. In Hosea, unfaithful Israel says, "I will go and return to my first husband" ([Hosea 2:7](https://www.churchofjesuschrist.org/study/scriptures/ot/hosea/2?lang=eng&id=p7#p7)); the people say, "Come, and let us return unto the Lord" ([Hosea 6:1](https://www.churchofjesuschrist.org/study/scriptures/ot/hosea/6?lang=eng&id=p1#p1)); and the book ends with "O Israel, return" ([Hosea 14:1](https://www.churchofjesuschrist.org/study/scriptures/ot/hosea/14?lang=eng&id=p1#p1)). Joel calls, "turn ye even to me with all your heart" ([Joel 2:12](https://www.churchofjesuschrist.org/study/scriptures/ot/joel/2?lang=eng&id=p12#p12)), and wonders whether the Lord himself "will return and repent, and leave a blessing behind him" ([Joel 2:14](https://www.churchofjesuschrist.org/study/scriptures/ot/joel/2?lang=eng&id=p14#p14)). John Hilton III's class for this week gathers the two books around one question: when the day of decision comes, will we turn toward God or away from him? He takes his title from Joel's "Multitudes, multitudes in the valley of decision" ([Joel 3:14](https://www.churchofjesuschrist.org/study/scriptures/ot/joel/3?lang=eng&id=p14#p14)) ([Hilton, Class 46](https://johnhiltoniii.com/findingchrist/class-46-your-day-of-decision-hosea-1-6-10-14/)).

**Threads worth tracking through the week:**

- **"Know" and "knowledge."** Israel lacks "knowledge of God" ([Hosea 4:1](https://www.churchofjesuschrist.org/study/scriptures/ot/hosea/4?lang=eng&id=p1#p1), [6](https://www.churchofjesuschrist.org/study/scriptures/ot/hosea/4?lang=eng&id=p6#p6)); the Lord wants "the knowledge of God more than burnt offerings" ([Hosea 6:6](https://www.churchofjesuschrist.org/study/scriptures/ot/hosea/6?lang=eng&id=p6#p6)); the promise is "thou shalt know the Lord" ([Hosea 2:20](https://www.churchofjesuschrist.org/study/scriptures/ot/hosea/2?lang=eng&id=p20#p20)). Joel ends with "So shall ye know that I am the Lord your God" ([Joel 3:17](https://www.churchofjesuschrist.org/study/scriptures/ot/joel/3?lang=eng&id=p17#p17)).
- **Names that change.** Jezreel, Lo-ruhamah, and Lo-ammi become Ammi and Ruhamah ([Hosea 1:4–9](https://www.churchofjesuschrist.org/study/scriptures/ot/hosea/1?lang=eng&id=p4-p9#p4); [Hosea 2:1](https://www.churchofjesuschrist.org/study/scriptures/ot/hosea/2?lang=eng&id=p1#p1), [23](https://www.churchofjesuschrist.org/study/scriptures/ot/hosea/2?lang=eng&id=p23#p23)). "Baali" becomes "Ishi" ([Hosea 2:16](https://www.churchofjesuschrist.org/study/scriptures/ot/hosea/2?lang=eng&id=p16#p16)).
- **Dew, rain, and harvest.** Israel's goodness vanishes "as the early dew" ([Hosea 6:4](https://www.churchofjesuschrist.org/study/scriptures/ot/hosea/6?lang=eng&id=p4#p4)), but the Lord promises, "I will be as the dew unto Israel" ([Hosea 14:5](https://www.churchofjesuschrist.org/study/scriptures/ot/hosea/14?lang=eng&id=p5#p5)). Hosea's "latter and former rain" ([Hosea 6:3](https://www.churchofjesuschrist.org/study/scriptures/ot/hosea/6?lang=eng&id=p3#p3)) returns in Joel ([Joel 2:23](https://www.churchofjesuschrist.org/study/scriptures/ot/joel/2?lang=eng&id=p23#p23)). Sowing and reaping run through [Hosea 8:7](https://www.churchofjesuschrist.org/study/scriptures/ot/hosea/8?lang=eng&id=p7#p7) (outside this week's reading), [Hosea 10:12–13](https://www.churchofjesuschrist.org/study/scriptures/ot/hosea/10?lang=eng&id=p12-p13#p12), and [Joel 3:13](https://www.churchofjesuschrist.org/study/scriptures/ot/joel/3?lang=eng&id=p13#p13).
- **Lions.** The Lord is a lion who tears in judgment ([Hosea 5:14](https://www.churchofjesuschrist.org/study/scriptures/ot/hosea/5?lang=eng&id=p14#p14); [Hosea 13:7–8](https://www.churchofjesuschrist.org/study/scriptures/ot/hosea/13?lang=eng&id=p7-p8#p7)), a lion whose roar calls his children home ([Hosea 11:10](https://www.churchofjesuschrist.org/study/scriptures/ot/hosea/11?lang=eng&id=p10#p10)), and a lion who roars out of Zion ([Joel 3:16](https://www.churchofjesuschrist.org/study/scriptures/ot/joel/3?lang=eng&id=p16#p16)).
- **Egypt and Assyria.** Israel looks to both empires for rescue ([Hosea 7:11](https://www.churchofjesuschrist.org/study/scriptures/ot/hosea/7?lang=eng&id=p11#p11) outside this week's reading; [Hosea 12:1](https://www.churchofjesuschrist.org/study/scriptures/ot/hosea/12?lang=eng&id=p1#p1)), but "Asshur shall not save us" ([Hosea 14:3](https://www.churchofjesuschrist.org/study/scriptures/ot/hosea/14?lang=eng&id=p3#p3)).
- **"The day of the Lord."** Joel uses the phrase five times ([Joel 1:15](https://www.churchofjesuschrist.org/study/scriptures/ot/joel/1?lang=eng&id=p15#p15); [Joel 2:1](https://www.churchofjesuschrist.org/study/scriptures/ot/joel/2?lang=eng&id=p1#p1), [11](https://www.churchofjesuschrist.org/study/scriptures/ot/joel/2?lang=eng&id=p11#p11), [31](https://www.churchofjesuschrist.org/study/scriptures/ot/joel/2?lang=eng&id=p31#p31); [Joel 3:14](https://www.churchofjesuschrist.org/study/scriptures/ot/joel/3?lang=eng&id=p14#p14)).

**Reading in layers.** This guide tries to keep four kinds of statement apart: what the ancient text says to its first audience; what official Latter-day Saint sources teach about it; what individual scholars or teachers propose; and devotional applications, including readings that see Jesus Christ in the passage. These layers can support each other, but they are not the same thing, and the guide labels them as it goes.

## Significant Insights

1. **God's love is covenant love, and it is freely given.** "I will heal their backsliding, I will love them freely" ([Hosea 14:4](https://www.churchofjesuschrist.org/study/scriptures/ot/hosea/14?lang=eng&id=p4#p4)). Israel did not earn that love and could not buy it back. The whole book of Hosea shows a God who keeps seeking a people who keep leaving. The Hebrew word for this kind of steady covenant love is *hesed*. President Russell M. Nelson has taught that *hesed* "is a special kind of love and mercy that God feels for and extends to those who have made a covenant with Him" ([Nelson, "The Everlasting Covenant"](https://www.churchofjesuschrist.org/study/liahona/2022/10/04-the-everlasting-covenant?lang=eng)). The gospel of Jesus Christ is its fullest expression ([1 John 4:10](https://www.churchofjesuschrist.org/study/scriptures/nt/1-jn/4?lang=eng&id=p10#p10)).

2. **Covenant faithfulness is personal, like a marriage.** The Lord does not describe Israel's sin as breaking a rule but as betraying a relationship. The promise in [Hosea 2:19–20](https://www.churchofjesuschrist.org/study/scriptures/ot/hosea/2?lang=eng&id=p19-p20#p19) is not "I will tolerate you" but "I will betroth thee unto me for ever ... and thou shalt know the Lord." Our covenants are relationships with God, not just lists of duties.

3. **Idolatry is trusting the wrong source for what we need.** Israel credited Baal and its "lovers" with "my bread and my water, my wool and my flax" ([Hosea 2:5](https://www.churchofjesuschrist.org/study/scriptures/ot/hosea/2?lang=eng&id=p5#p5)). "She did not know that I gave her corn, and wine, and oil" ([Hosea 2:8](https://www.churchofjesuschrist.org/study/scriptures/ot/hosea/2?lang=eng&id=p8#p8)). Much modern idolatry looks the same: giving credit and trust to money, power, or alliances for what only God gives.

4. **God values mercy and knowledge of him above outward performance.** "For I desired mercy, and not sacrifice; and the knowledge of God more than burnt offerings" ([Hosea 6:6](https://www.churchofjesuschrist.org/study/scriptures/ot/hosea/6?lang=eng&id=p6#p6)). Jesus quoted this verse twice to people who used religious observance to look down on others ([Matthew 9:13](https://www.churchofjesuschrist.org/study/scriptures/nt/matt/9?lang=eng&id=p13#p13); [Matthew 12:7](https://www.churchofjesuschrist.org/study/scriptures/nt/matt/12?lang=eng&id=p7#p7)). Joel says the same thing another way: "rend your heart, and not your garments" ([Joel 2:13](https://www.churchofjesuschrist.org/study/scriptures/ot/joel/2?lang=eng&id=p13#p13)).

5. **Judgment is real, and so is God's reluctance to destroy.** Hosea does not soften the consequences of sin. Yet at the center of his book God cries, "How shall I give thee up, Ephraim? ... mine heart is turned within me" ([Hosea 11:8](https://www.churchofjesuschrist.org/study/scriptures/ot/hosea/11?lang=eng&id=p8#p8)). God's warnings come from love, and his deepest desire is to heal.

6. **Repentance is returning, and God supplies the words.** "Take with you words, and turn to the Lord: say unto him, Take away all iniquity, and receive us graciously" ([Hosea 14:2](https://www.churchofjesuschrist.org/study/scriptures/ot/hosea/14?lang=eng&id=p2#p2)). God does not wait for his people to find their own way back. He teaches them what to say.

7. **Christ conquers death.** "I will ransom them from the power of the grave; I will redeem them from death: O death, I will be thy plagues; O grave, I will be thy destruction" ([Hosea 13:14](https://www.churchofjesuschrist.org/study/scriptures/ot/hosea/13?lang=eng&id=p14#p14)). Paul heard in these words the victory of the resurrected Christ ([1 Corinthians 15:54–57](https://www.churchofjesuschrist.org/study/scriptures/nt/1-cor/15?lang=eng&id=p54-p57#p54)).

8. **What the locusts took, the Lord can restore.** "I will restore to you the years that the locust hath eaten" ([Joel 2:25](https://www.churchofjesuschrist.org/study/scriptures/ot/joel/2?lang=eng&id=p25#p25)). Joel's promise reaches beyond crops to every kind of loss that repentance and the Lord's mercy can redeem.

9. **The Spirit is for everyone.** Joel saw a day when God would pour out his Spirit "upon all flesh," on sons and daughters, old and young, servants and handmaids ([Joel 2:28–29](https://www.churchofjesuschrist.org/study/scriptures/ot/joel/2?lang=eng&id=p28-p29#p28)). Peter said that day had begun at Pentecost; Moroni told Joseph Smith it was "soon to be" fulfilled ([Joseph Smith—History 1:41](https://www.churchofjesuschrist.org/study/scriptures/pgp/js-h/1?lang=eng&id=p41#p41)). Latter-day Saints see the Restoration as part of that fulfillment and the gift of the Holy Ghost as its personal center.

## Hosea 1: Children with Names That Preach

Chapter 1 introduces Hosea, his marriage, and three children whose names announce judgment on Israel. Then, without warning, the chapter turns to hope: the people called "not my people" will be called "the sons of the living God."

### Hosea 1:1 — The Word of the Lord in a Time of Kings

"The word of the Lord that came unto Hosea, the son of Beeri." Like other prophetic books, Hosea begins by naming its source: the message is the Lord's word, not Hosea's opinion. The verse then names kings. Four are kings of Judah, "Uzziah, Jotham, Ahaz, and Hezekiah," and one is king of Israel, "Jeroboam the son of Joash."

It is a little surprising that a northern prophet dates his work mostly by southern kings. One possibility is that his writings were kept and copied in Judah after Israel fell, so they were dated by Judah's reigns. Another is that after Jeroboam II the northern throne changed hands so quickly, often by murder, that its kings were not worth naming. The text does not say.

The opening also sets Hosea beside other prophets. Isaiah's book is dated by the same four kings of Judah ([Isaiah 1:1](https://www.churchofjesuschrist.org/study/scriptures/ot/isa/1?lang=eng&id=p1#p1)), and Amos preached "concerning Israel" in the days of Uzziah and Jeroboam ([Amos 1:1](https://www.churchofjesuschrist.org/study/scriptures/ot/amos/1?lang=eng&id=p1#p1)). The Lord sent several witnesses to the same generation.

**Question:** Hosea preached during a time of prosperity that came before a collapse. Why might prosperous times make it harder to hear a prophet's warning?

### Hosea 1:2–3 — "Go, Take unto Thee a Wife"

"The beginning of the word of the Lord by Hosea. And the Lord said to Hosea, Go, take unto thee a wife of whoredoms and children of whoredoms." The reason follows immediately: "for the land hath committed great whoredom, departing from the Lord." The command exists to illustrate the second half of the verse. Israel's worship of other gods was like a spouse's adultery.

"So he went and took Gomer the daughter of Diblaim; which conceived, and bare him a son" (verse 3). The text gives no details about Gomer's life before or during the marriage. The phrase "wife of whoredoms" could describe what she had been, what she would become, or simply her place in the symbolic drama. *Scripture Helps* notes that scholars have long debated whether the command was literal or symbolic, and that the page's focus is the covenant the marriage represents. The Church's footnote to this verse points to [Hosea 3:1–3](https://www.churchofjesuschrist.org/study/scriptures/ot/hosea/3?lang=eng&id=p1-p3#p1) and to the Topical Guide entries on idolatry.

Whichever way one reads it, Hosea's obedience was costly. Prophets sometimes taught with their own lives: Isaiah walked "naked and barefoot" as a sign ([Isaiah 20:2–3](https://www.churchofjesuschrist.org/study/scriptures/ot/isa/20?lang=eng&id=p2-p3#p2)), and Ezekiel was told not to mourn publicly for his wife ([Ezekiel 24:16–18](https://www.churchofjesuschrist.org/study/scriptures/ot/ezek/24?lang=eng&id=p16-p18#p16)). Hosea's household became a living sermon. The marriage is not offered as a model for anyone's choice of a spouse; it is a sign to a nation.

**Question:** Why might the Lord have chosen a marriage, the most personal of human covenants, to show how he feels about his covenant with Israel?

### Hosea 1:4–5 — Jezreel

"Call his name Jezreel; for yet a little while, and I will avenge the blood of Jezreel upon the house of Jehu." Jezreel was a valley and a royal city in the north. About a century earlier, Jehu had seized the throne there in a bloody purge of Ahab's family ([2 Kings 9–10](https://www.churchofjesuschrist.org/study/scriptures/ot/2-kgs/9?lang=eng)). The Lord had commissioned Jehu to end Ahab's house, and 2 Kings records the Lord's promise that Jehu's descendants would reign "of the fourth generation" ([2 Kings 10:30](https://www.churchofjesuschrist.org/study/scriptures/ot/2-kgs/10?lang=eng&id=p30#p30)). Jeroboam II was of that dynasty, and his son Zachariah was the fourth generation. Within months of Zachariah's accession he was killed ([2 Kings 15:10–12](https://www.churchofjesuschrist.org/study/scriptures/ot/2-kgs/15?lang=eng&id=p10-p12#p10)).

Why would the Lord "avenge the blood of Jezreel" when he had sent Jehu? The text does not explain. One common reading is that Jehu's violence went beyond what the Lord required, or that his dynasty went on doing the same sins it had condemned in Ahab's house ([2 Kings 10:31](https://www.churchofjesuschrist.org/study/scriptures/ot/2-kgs/10?lang=eng&id=p31#p31)). *Scripture Helps* cites Kent P. Jackson's explanation tying the name to Jehu's bloodshed at Jezreel and notes that the name means "God sows." Here the sowing is of judgment, scattering Israel like seed. Verse 5 adds, "I will break the bow of Israel in the valley of Jezreel": Israel's military strength would fail in the very valley where its dynasty began.

**Question:** What does it suggest that a name associated with violence and judgment, Jezreel, will later become a name of blessing ([Hosea 2:22–23](https://www.churchofjesuschrist.org/study/scriptures/ot/hosea/2?lang=eng&id=p22-p23#p22))?

### Hosea 1:6–7 — Lo-ruhamah: "No More Mercy"

Gomer "conceived again, and bare a daughter. And God said unto him, Call her name Lo-ruhamah." The Church's footnote explains the name as "Not having obtained mercy." The reason: "for I will no more have mercy upon the house of Israel; but I will utterly take them away."

The mercy withdrawn here is the compassion Israel had received from the Lord since the Exodus. To give a little girl that name was shocking. Every time her name was spoken, Israel heard what its choices had cost.

Verse 7 offers a contrast: "But I will have mercy upon the house of Judah, and will save them by the Lord their God, and will not save them by bow, nor by sword, nor by battle, by horses, nor by horsemen." Judah would be saved, but not by its own military strength. When the Assyrian army later surrounded Jerusalem in Hezekiah's day, "the angel of the Lord went out, and smote in the camp of the Assyrians" ([2 Kings 19:35](https://www.churchofjesuschrist.org/study/scriptures/ot/2-kgs/19?lang=eng&id=p35#p35)). The deliverance came from the Lord, as Hosea had said.

**Question:** Verse 7 says salvation would come "not ... by bow, nor by sword." Where are you tempted to trust your own strength for something only the Lord can do?

### Hosea 1:8–9 — Lo-ammi: "Not My People"

"Now when she had weaned Lo-ruhamah, she conceived, and bare a son. Then said God, Call his name Lo-ammi." The footnote gives the meaning, "Not my people." The reason follows: "for ye are not my people, and I will not be your God." These words reverse the heart of the covenant. Even before the Exodus, while Israel was still in Egypt, the Lord had promised through Moses, "I will take you to me for a people, and I will be to you a God" ([Exodus 6:7](https://www.churchofjesuschrist.org/study/scriptures/ot/ex/6?lang=eng&id=p7#p7); compare [Leviticus 26:12](https://www.churchofjesuschrist.org/study/scriptures/ot/lev/26?lang=eng&id=p12#p12)). The covenant at Sinai and the law that followed repeated the same promise. *Scripture Helps* points to the same covenant formula.

The three names form a sequence. Jezreel announces judgment. Lo-ruhamah announces the withdrawal of compassion. Lo-ammi announces the end of the relationship itself.

A recent Scripture Central KnoWhy suggests a further layer. It notes that the Hebrew phrase translated "I will not be your God" does not contain the usual word for God. Citing a standard reference grammar, it reads the phrase more literally as "I will not be for you," and connects it to the name the Lord revealed to Moses, "I Am That I Am" ([Exodus 3:14](https://www.churchofjesuschrist.org/study/scriptures/ot/ex/3?lang=eng&id=p14#p14)). In that reading, the God who revealed his name at the Exodus to affirm his covenant now withholds it ([Scripture Central, "Why Does Hosea Use an Unusual Name for God?"](https://scripturecentral.org/knowhy/why-does-hosea-use-an-unusual-name-for-god)). This is an interpretation, not a settled conclusion, but it fits the chapter: Lo-ammi undoes the Exodus covenant.

**Question:** What would it feel like to hear "Ye are not my people" from God? What does that show you about how much the covenant relationship is meant to matter?

### Hosea 1:10–11 — "Ye Are the Sons of the Living God"

Then, with no transition, hope breaks in: "Yet the number of the children of Israel shall be as the sand of the sea, which cannot be measured nor numbered." That is the promise God made to Abraham and Jacob ([Genesis 22:17](https://www.churchofjesuschrist.org/study/scriptures/ot/gen/22?lang=eng&id=p17#p17); [Genesis 32:12](https://www.churchofjesuschrist.org/study/scriptures/ot/gen/32?lang=eng&id=p12#p12)). "In the place where it was said unto them, Ye are not my people, there it shall be said unto them, Ye are the sons of the living God." The very place of rejection becomes the place of adoption.

Verse 11 adds, "Then shall the children of Judah and the children of Israel be gathered together, and appoint themselves one head ... for great shall be the day of Jezreel." The divided kingdoms will be reunited. "Jezreel," the name of judgment, now names a great day of gathering, because God "sows" his people again. The Church's footnote connects this gathering with [2 Nephi 29:8–9](https://www.churchofjesuschrist.org/study/scriptures/bofm/2-ne/29?lang=eng&id=p8-p9#p8) and the Topical Guide on the gathering of Israel. Ezekiel later prophesied the same reunion with two sticks joined into one ([Ezekiel 37:16–22](https://www.churchofjesuschrist.org/study/scriptures/ot/ezek/37?lang=eng&id=p16-p22#p16)).

*Christ connection (New Testament reading):* Paul quoted Hosea's promise to show that God was calling Gentiles as well as Jews into his family: "in the place where it was said unto them, Ye are not my people; there shall they be called the children of the living God" ([Romans 9:25–26](https://www.churchofjesuschrist.org/study/scriptures/nt/rom/9?lang=eng&id=p25-p26#p25)). Peter applied it to the Saints: they "in time past were not a people, but are now the people of God" ([1 Peter 2:10](https://www.churchofjesuschrist.org/study/scriptures/nt/1-pet/2?lang=eng&id=p10#p10)).

*Restoration reading:* *Scripture Helps* connects "sons of the living God" with King Benjamin's teaching that those who make covenants "shall be called the children of Christ, his sons, and his daughters" ([Mosiah 5:7](https://www.churchofjesuschrist.org/study/scriptures/bofm/mosiah/5?lang=eng&id=p7#p7)). It also quotes President Russell M. Nelson: "We are men and women of God quite precisely because we have made covenants with Him."

**Question:** Where in your own life might the Lord turn a place of failure or rejection into a place where you hear, "Ye are the sons [and daughters] of the living God"?

## Hosea 2: The Lord Will Betroth Israel Forever

Chapter 2 is the longest poem in Hosea's opening section. It begins with a court case against an unfaithful wife, describes how the Lord will block her path and take back his gifts, and then turns to one of the most tender passages in the Old Testament: the Lord leading Israel into the wilderness to win her heart again. Read the whole chapter with the cautions in the introduction in mind. The wife is the nation, and the judgments are national calamities.

### Hosea 2:1 — "Ammi" and "Ruhamah"

"Say ye unto your brethren, Ammi; and to your sisters, Ruhamah." The Church's footnotes explain the names: Ammi means "My people," and Ruhamah means "Having obtained mercy." The verse simply drops the negative "Lo-" from the children's names in chapter 1.

This verse continues the hope of 1:10–11. Before the judgment poem even begins, Hosea tells his hearers how the story ends. *Scripture Helps* explains that the renaming implies the covenant will not stay broken forever. Calling one another by the new names is itself an act of faith.

**Question:** What changes when you speak to others, and to yourself, by the names God intends ("my people," "having obtained mercy") instead of the names sin has earned?

### Hosea 2:2–5 — "Plead with Your Mother"

"Plead with your mother, plead: for she is not my wife, neither am I her husband." The language sounds like a legal proceeding. The children (the people of Israel) are asked to plead with their "mother" (the nation as a whole). The goal is not divorce but reform: "let her therefore put away her whoredoms out of her sight."

Verses 3–4 warn of what will happen if she does not: "Lest I strip her naked ... and make her as a wilderness, and set her like a dry land, and slay her with thirst." Notice how the image shifts in the middle of the sentence from a woman to a land. A stripped and thirsty woman becomes a land without rain. That is the judgment Hosea foresaw: drought, failed harvests, and invasion. Verse 4 extends the judgment to the "children of whoredoms," the people who had joined in the nation's idolatry.

Verse 5 explains the charge: "she said, I will go after my lovers, that give me my bread and my water, my wool and my flax, mine oil and my drink." Baal was worshipped as the giver of rain and fertility. Israel was not denying that it needed bread and water. It was crediting the wrong source.

These verses are among the hardest in the book. As explained in the introduction, they describe national judgment in the language of an ancient marriage. They are not a pattern for how anyone should treat a spouse, and they do not suggest that a person who is mistreated deserves it. Hosea's message is aimed at a nation that had abandoned its covenant, and the chapter's direction is toward restoration.

**Question:** Israel said its "lovers" gave it bread and water. What blessings in your life might you be tempted to credit to something other than God?

### Hosea 2:6–8 — A Hedge of Thorns

"Therefore, behold, I will hedge up thy way with thorns, and make a wall, that she shall not find her paths." The Lord's first response is not destruction but obstruction. He blocks the road to the false gods. Verse 7 explains the purpose: "she shall follow after her lovers, but she shall not overtake them ... then shall she say, I will go and return to my first husband; for then was it better with me than now."

This is the logic of the prodigal son, who "came to himself" only when his resources ran out ([Luke 15:17–18](https://www.churchofjesuschrist.org/study/scriptures/nt/luke/15?lang=eng&id=p17-p18#p17)). Disappointment can be a mercy when it turns us home.

Verse 8 names the root problem: "For she did not know that I gave her corn, and wine, and oil, and multiplied her silver and gold, which they prepared for Baal." (The footnote gives "grain" for "corn.") The Lord's own gifts were being melted down for idols. Ingratitude and idolatry are close neighbors.

A side note on the phrase "hedge up thy way": Nephi pleads, "hedge not up my way" ([2 Nephi 4:33](https://www.churchofjesuschrist.org/study/scriptures/bofm/2-ne/4?lang=eng&id=p33#p33)). A. Keith Thompson, writing in *Interpreter*, notes that some critics point to phrases like this one as evidence of borrowing. He argues that such shared idioms are explicable in other ways and are not an adequate basis for concluding that the brass plates contained Hosea's writings ([Thompson, "The Brass Plates," *Interpreter* 45 (2021): 106 n. 63](https://cdn.interpreterfoundation.org/jnlpdf/thompson-v45-2021-pp81-114-PDF.pdf)). The overlap is interesting, but it does not establish a direct connection either way.

**Question:** Can you think of a time when a closed door, a "hedge of thorns," turned out to be the Lord's way of bringing you back?

### Hosea 2:9–13 — The Lord Takes Back His Gifts

"Therefore will I return, and take away my corn in the time thereof, and my wine in the season thereof, and will recover my wool and my flax." Note the word "my." The grain, wine, wool, and flax had always been the Lord's. Israel thought they were "rewards that my lovers have given me" (verse 12). The Lord will take them back so Israel can see where they came from.

The losses spread. Verse 10 speaks of public exposure "in the sight of her lovers," meaning the nation's humiliation before the foreign powers it had courted, "and none shall deliver her out of mine hand." Verse 11 ends the celebrations: "her feast days, her new moons, and her sabbaths, and all her solemn feasts." Israel's festivals had become mixed with Baal worship and lost their meaning. Verse 12 turns cultivated vines and fig trees into "a forest" for wild beasts. Verse 13 gives the reason: "I will visit upon her the days of Baalim, wherein she burned incense to them ... and she went after her lovers, and forgat me, saith the Lord."

"And forgat me." After all the jewelry and incense and festivals, the real charge is forgetting. Moses had warned Israel that prosperity would tempt them to forget the Lord who gave it ([Deuteronomy 8:11–14](https://www.churchofjesuschrist.org/study/scriptures/ot/deut/8?lang=eng&id=p11-p14#p11)).

**Question:** What helps you remember the Lord in seasons of plenty, before losses force you to remember?

### Hosea 2:14–15 — "I Will Allure Her ... and Speak Comfortably unto Her"

"Therefore, behold, I will allure her, and bring her into the wilderness, and speak comfortably unto her." This "therefore" is the turning point of the chapter. Verses 6 and 9 also began with "therefore," followed by judgment. Now the same word introduces courtship. The Lord will draw Israel back, not drag her.

The wilderness is where Israel first came to know the Lord after the Exodus. Jeremiah remembers that time as a honeymoon: "I remember thee, the kindness of thy youth, the love of thine espousals, when thou wentest after me in the wilderness" ([Jeremiah 2:2](https://www.churchofjesuschrist.org/study/scriptures/ot/jer/2?lang=eng&id=p2#p2)). The same comforting words open Isaiah's message of return from exile: "Speak ye comfortably to Jerusalem" ([Isaiah 40:2](https://www.churchofjesuschrist.org/study/scriptures/ot/isa/40?lang=eng&id=p2#p2)).

Verse 15 promises, "I will give her her vineyards from thence, and the valley of Achor for a door of hope: and she shall sing there, as in the days of her youth." The footnote explains Achor as "Trouble" and points to Joshua 7. The valley of Achor was where Achan was stoned after he took forbidden plunder at Jericho, the first sin after Israel entered the promised land ([Joshua 7:24–26](https://www.churchofjesuschrist.org/study/scriptures/ot/josh/7?lang=eng&id=p24-p26#p24)). A place remembered for trouble becomes "a door of hope." Isaiah later promised the same valley would become "a place for the herds to lie down in" ([Isaiah 65:10](https://www.churchofjesuschrist.org/study/scriptures/ot/isa/65?lang=eng&id=p10#p10)).

*Christ connection (devotional reading):* Through the Atonement of Jesus Christ, the places where we have sinned or suffered most can become, not places we avoid, but doors of hope.

**Question:** Is there a "valley of Achor," a place of past trouble, in your life that the Lord might turn into a door of hope?

### Hosea 2:16–17 — "Ishi," Not "Baali"

"Thou shalt call me Ishi; and shalt call me no more Baali." The footnotes explain Ishi as "My husband" and Baali as "My master." *Scripture Helps* explains that both words could mean "husband," but "Baali" echoes the name of the false god Baal. Calling the Lord "Ishi" shows that Israel has rejected false gods and has a renewed relationship with the Lord.

Verse 17 adds, "For I will take away the names of Baalim out of her mouth, and they shall no more be remembered by their name." The cure for idolatry is not only to stop serving false gods but to stop speaking of them, to have them crowded out of the heart and the vocabulary.

The shift from "master" to "husband" also says something about the relationship God wants. He seeks a covenant of love and trust rather than mere ownership and obedience. Compare Jesus's words to his disciples: "Henceforth I call you not servants ... but I have called you friends" ([John 15:15](https://www.churchofjesuschrist.org/study/scriptures/nt/john/15?lang=eng&id=p15#p15)).

**Question:** How do you speak of God when you pray? Do your words reflect a relationship of trust and love, or something more distant?

### Hosea 2:18 — A Covenant with the Beasts and an End to War

"In that day will I make a covenant for them with the beasts of the field, and with the fowls of heaven, and with the creeping things of the ground." In verse 12 wild beasts ate Israel's vineyards. Now creation itself is at peace with God's people. "And I will break the bow and the sword and the battle out of the earth, and will make them to lie down safely." In chapter 1 the Lord broke "the bow of Israel" as judgment (1:5); here he breaks every bow so that no one needs one.

The promise looks forward to a renewed world. Ezekiel spoke of "a covenant of peace" in which God's people "shall dwell safely in the wilderness, and sleep in the woods" ([Ezekiel 34:25](https://www.churchofjesuschrist.org/study/scriptures/ot/ezek/34?lang=eng&id=p25#p25)), and Isaiah saw the wolf dwelling with the lamb ([Isaiah 11:6](https://www.churchofjesuschrist.org/study/scriptures/ot/isa/11?lang=eng&id=p6#p6)). The Church's footnotes connect this verse with the Topical Guide entries on the Millennium and peace.

**Question:** The covenant here brings peace to the whole of creation. How can your covenant relationship with God make you a peacemaker where you live?

### Hosea 2:19–20 — "I Will Betroth Thee unto Me for Ever"

"And I will betroth thee unto me for ever; yea, I will betroth thee unto me in righteousness, and in judgment, and in lovingkindness, and in mercies. I will even betroth thee unto me in faithfulness: and thou shalt know the Lord." The word "betroth" appears three times. *Scripture Helps* explains that betrothal in ancient Israel was legally binding, the last step before the full marriage. Here the Lord brings his own qualities to the covenant: righteousness, justice, *hesed* ("lovingkindness"), compassion ("mercies"), and faithfulness.

It is striking that the Lord does not say "*you* will be faithful." He says he will betroth Israel "in faithfulness," his own. The relationship rests on his character. The result is "thou shalt know the Lord." In Hosea, to "know" the Lord is more than having information about him. It is the intimate, loyal knowledge of a covenant relationship. Jesus said, "this is life eternal, that they might know thee the only true God, and Jesus Christ, whom thou hast sent" ([John 17:3](https://www.churchofjesuschrist.org/study/scriptures/nt/john/17?lang=eng&id=p3#p3)).

*Christ connection (New Testament reading):* The New Testament calls Jesus the Bridegroom ([Matthew 9:15](https://www.churchofjesuschrist.org/study/scriptures/nt/matt/9?lang=eng&id=p15#p15)) and foresees "the marriage of the Lamb" with his prepared people ([Revelation 19:7](https://www.churchofjesuschrist.org/study/scriptures/nt/rev/19?lang=eng&id=p7#p7)). *Scripture Helps* cites President Russell M. Nelson's teaching about *hesed*: because of this covenant love, God will keep loving and working with those who covenant with him, forgive them when they repent, and help them return when they stray.

*Restoration reading:* Latter-day Saints make covenants at baptism and in the temple. The official lesson opens with a picture of a newly married couple outside a temple and suggests reading Hosea 2:19–20 with children to compare covenants with marriage ([official lesson](https://www.churchofjesuschrist.org/study/manual/come-follow-me-for-home-and-church-old-testament-2026/47?lang=eng)).

**Question:** Which of the five words in verses 19–20 (righteousness, judgment, lovingkindness, mercies, faithfulness) do you most need to trust in the Lord right now? Why?

### Hosea 2:21–23 — "Thou Art My People"

"I will hear, saith the Lord, I will hear the heavens, and they shall hear the earth; and the earth shall hear the corn, and the wine, and the oil; and they shall hear Jezreel." The picture is a chain of answers. Jezreel (God's people) calls for food; grain, wine, and oil call to the earth; the earth calls to the heavens for rain; the heavens call to the Lord; and the Lord answers. Every gift Israel had credited to Baal now flows clearly from the Lord.

Verse 23 completes the reversal of chapter 1: "I will sow her unto me in the earth; and I will have mercy upon her that had not obtained mercy; and I will say to them which were not my people, Thou art my people; and they shall say, Thou art my God." The footnotes note that "her" stands for the Hebrew *lo-ruhamah* and "them" for *lo-ammi*. Jezreel ("God sows") is now sown for life. Lo-ruhamah receives mercy. Lo-ammi is called "my people." And for the first time in the book, the people answer: "Thou art my God."

*Christ connection (New Testament reading):* Paul quoted verse 23 alongside 1:10 to describe God's mercy reaching Gentiles: "I will call them my people, which were not my people; and her beloved, which was not beloved" ([Romans 9:25](https://www.churchofjesuschrist.org/study/scriptures/nt/rom/9?lang=eng&id=p25#p25)). Peter echoed it: "which had not obtained mercy, but now have obtained mercy" ([1 Peter 2:10](https://www.churchofjesuschrist.org/study/scriptures/nt/1-pet/2?lang=eng&id=p10#p10)). John Hilton III's slides lay out all three name reversals side by side and conclude with a short line worth remembering: "We don't achieve our identity by what we do. We receive our identity by what Christ did" ([Hilton, Class 46 slides](https://www.dropbox.com/scl/fi/cbft6sna5osmeec29m0w2/46-Hosea-Joel.pdf?rlkey=7tgk1qjl09srdxa8r6ell7nbg&st=22efq01e&dl=1)).

**Question:** Hosea 2:23 ends with God saying "Thou art my people" and the people answering "Thou art my God." When have you felt yourself give that answer?

## Hosea 3: Love Her Again

Chapter 3 is short and told in Hosea's own voice ("the Lord unto me"). It may describe Hosea taking back Gomer after she left him, or another stage of the same symbolic drama. The point is that love that has been betrayed seeks reconciliation, and that Israel will one day return.

### Hosea 3:1 — "Go Yet, Love a Woman"

"Then said the Lord unto me, Go yet, love a woman beloved of her friend, yet an adulteress, according to the love of the Lord toward the children of Israel, who look to other gods, and love flagons of wine." "Go yet" means "go again." *Scripture Helps* reads this as Hosea's effort to reconcile with Gomer after she had left him. The verse does not name the woman, and some readers think it is a different woman, but the story makes the best sense as a return to Gomer.

The key phrase is "according to the love of the Lord toward the children of Israel." Hosea's love is to imitate God's. The word "love" appears four times in one verse. Israel loved other gods and "flagons of wine." The footnote explains the Hebrew as "raisin-cakes (used in fertility rites)." The Lord loved Israel anyway.

*Christ connection (devotional reading):* Paul wrote, "God commendeth his love toward us, in that, while we were yet sinners, Christ died for us" ([Romans 5:8](https://www.churchofjesuschrist.org/study/scriptures/nt/rom/5?lang=eng&id=p8#p8)). The Lord's love does not wait for us to become lovable.

**Question:** The Lord loved Israel while it was still looking to other gods. How does knowing that change the way you approach him when you have strayed?

### Hosea 3:2–3 — Bought Back

"So I bought her to me for fifteen pieces of silver, and for an homer of barley, and an half homer of barley." The text does not explain why a price was needed. She may have been in debt or in someone else's service. The payment, partly in silver and partly in barley, may suggest that Hosea was not a wealthy man and gathered what he had.

Verse 3 sets terms: "Thou shalt abide for me many days; thou shalt not play the harlot, and thou shalt not be for another man: so will I also be for thee." There is a period of waiting and faithfulness, and Hosea binds himself too: "so will I also be for thee." Restoration of the relationship takes time, and it is mutual.

*Christ connection (New Testament reading):* John Hilton III's slides set this verse beside Paul's words, "ye are bought with a price: therefore glorify God in your body, and in your spirit, which are God's" ([1 Corinthians 6:20](https://www.churchofjesuschrist.org/study/scriptures/nt/1-cor/6?lang=eng&id=p20#p20)). Hosea paid to redeem his wife; Christ paid an infinitely greater price to redeem his people.

**Question:** Hosea's redemption of his wife came with a period of waiting ("many days"). Why might real reconciliation with God or with others take time even after forgiveness is offered?

### Hosea 3:4–5 — "Afterward Shall the Children of Israel Return"

"For the children of Israel shall abide many days without a king, and without a prince, and without a sacrifice, and without an image, and without an ephod, and without teraphim." Israel will lose both its legitimate institutions (king, prince, sacrifice, ephod) and its illegitimate ones (image, teraphim, a kind of household idol). Exile would strip away everything Israel had leaned on, good and bad, just as Gomer had to "abide ... many days."

Verse 5 is the promise: "Afterward shall the children of Israel return, and seek the Lord their God, and David their king; and shall fear the Lord and his goodness in the latter days." David had been dead for more than two centuries. "David their king" points to a future king from David's line. Jeremiah and Ezekiel use the same language: "David their king, whom I will raise up unto them" ([Jeremiah 30:9](https://www.churchofjesuschrist.org/study/scriptures/ot/jer/30?lang=eng&id=p9#p9)); "David my servant shall be king over them; and they all shall have one shepherd" ([Ezekiel 37:24](https://www.churchofjesuschrist.org/study/scriptures/ot/ezek/37?lang=eng&id=p24#p24)).

*Christ connection (messianic reading):* Christians, including Latter-day Saints, understand the future David as Jesus Christ, the Son of David, who will reign over gathered Israel. The chapter heading in the Church's edition summarizes, "Israel will seek the Lord, return to the Lord, and receive of His goodness in the latter days."

**Question:** Verse 5 says Israel will "fear the Lord and his goodness." What does it mean to stand in awe of God's goodness, not only of his power?

## Hosea 4: No Knowledge of God in the Land

With chapter 4 the book shifts from Hosea's family to direct prophetic speech. The rest of the book is a series of messages, often loosely connected, that alternate between accusation and appeal. Chapter 4 is framed as a lawsuit. The Lord brings charges against the people and especially against the priests, whose failure to teach left the nation without knowledge of God.

### Hosea 4:1–3 — The Lord's Controversy

"Hear the word of the Lord, ye children of Israel: for the Lord hath a controversy with the inhabitants of the land." A "controversy" is a legal case. The charge is an absence: "there is no truth, nor mercy, nor knowledge of God in the land." *Scripture Helps* notes that "mercy" here is *hesed*, covenant loyalty. Truth, loyal love, and knowledge of God are the three things the covenant was meant to produce, and all three were missing.

Verse 2 lists what filled the vacuum: "swearing, and lying, and killing, and stealing, and committing adultery." The list reads like a summary of the Ten Commandments broken one after another. The footnotes explain "they break out" as "break all bounds" and give "bloodshed leads to bloodshed" for "blood toucheth blood." Violence was spreading from one act to the next.

Verse 3 shows the result reaching the whole creation: "Therefore shall the land mourn, and every one that dwelleth therein shall languish, with the beasts of the field, and with the fowls of heaven; yea, the fishes of the sea also." Human sin harms the land and its creatures. Some readers notice that beasts, birds, and fish are named in the reverse of their order of creation in Genesis 1, as if creation were coming undone. In Enoch's vision the earth itself mourns over the wickedness of its people: "Wo, wo is me, the mother of men; I am pained" ([Moses 7:48](https://www.churchofjesuschrist.org/study/scriptures/pgp/moses/7?lang=eng&id=p48#p48)).

**Question:** Hosea says that a lack of knowledge of God shows up in how people treat each other. How does knowing God change the way you treat others?

### Hosea 4:4–6 — "My People Are Destroyed for Lack of Knowledge"

"Yet let no man strive, nor reprove another: for thy people are as they that strive with the priest." Verse 4 is difficult and has been read in different ways. One reading is that the Lord forbids the people to blame one another, because the real fault lies higher, with the priests. Verse 5 then warns both priest and prophet that they will "fall," and adds "I will destroy thy mother," probably meaning the nation as a whole.

Verse 6 is one of the best-known lines in Hosea: "My people are destroyed for lack of knowledge: because thou hast rejected knowledge, I will also reject thee, that thou shalt be no priest to me: seeing thou hast forgotten the law of thy God, I will also forget thy children." The priests' job was to teach the law. They had not merely failed to learn it; they had "rejected" and "forgotten" it. The punishment matches the sin: those who reject knowledge are rejected, those who forget God's law find their children forgotten.

The knowledge Hosea means is not only facts. It is the knowledge that comes from a living covenant relationship (see the note on [Hosea 2:20](https://www.churchofjesuschrist.org/study/scriptures/ot/hosea/2?lang=eng&id=p20#p20)). The Restoration adds that "it is impossible for a man to be saved in ignorance" ([Doctrine and Covenants 131:6](https://www.churchofjesuschrist.org/study/scriptures/dc-testament/dc/131?lang=eng&id=p6#p6)).

**Question:** Verse 6 holds leaders responsible for what they teach. What responsibility do parents, teachers, and leaders in your circle have for helping others know God?

### Hosea 4:7–11 — "Like People, Like Priest"

"As they were increased, so they sinned against me: therefore will I change their glory into shame." The more priests there were, the more sin there was. Verse 8 adds, "They eat up the sin of my people, and they set their heart on their iniquity." Priests received a share of the sin offerings ([Leviticus 6:25–26](https://www.churchofjesuschrist.org/study/scriptures/ot/lev/6?lang=eng&id=p25-p26#p25)), so more sin meant more food for them. Instead of calling the people to repent, they profited from their sins. The priests in Eli's day had abused the offerings in a similar way ([1 Samuel 2:17](https://www.churchofjesuschrist.org/study/scriptures/ot/1-sam/2?lang=eng&id=p17#p17)).

"And there shall be, like people, like priest" (verse 9). Leaders and people would share the same judgment. Verse 10 describes futility: "they shall eat, and not have enough: they shall commit whoredom, and shall not increase." The fertility rites of Baal worship promised plenty and children. They delivered neither. Verse 11 is a short proverb: "Whoredom and wine and new wine take away the heart." The word "heart" here means understanding. Indulgence dulls the ability to discern.

**Question:** "Like people, like priest." In what ways do leaders and the people they serve shape each other, for good or ill?

### Hosea 4:12–14 — Counsel from a Stick

"My people ask counsel at their stocks, and their staff declareth unto them." A "stock" is a piece of wood. The people were seeking guidance from wooden idols or from divining rods. The footnote points to the Topical Guide on superstitions and to [Isaiah 44:20](https://www.churchofjesuschrist.org/study/scriptures/ot/isa/44?lang=eng&id=p20#p20). The cause: "the spirit of whoredoms hath caused them to err, and they have gone a whoring from under their God."

Verse 13 describes the worship at the hilltop shrines, "under oaks and poplars and elms, because the shadow thereof is good." These shady groves were places of fertility rites. The consequence touched families: "therefore your daughters shall commit whoredom, and your spouses shall commit adultery."

Verse 14 contains a surprise: "I will not punish your daughters when they commit whoredom, nor your spouses when they commit adultery: for themselves are separated with whores, and they sacrifice with harlots." The Lord refuses to apply a double standard. The men of Israel, who joined in the shrine practices themselves, could not condemn the women of their families while excusing themselves. The heaviest blame falls on the men who led the way. "Therefore the people that doth not understand shall fall."

This verse is a useful corrective to reading Hosea's marriage imagery as though it blamed women in particular. Hosea's sharpest words land on priests, kings, and the men of the nation.

**Question:** Verse 14 refuses to let anyone condemn in others what they excuse in themselves. Where might you be holding others to a standard you excuse yourself from?

### Hosea 4:15–19 — "Ephraim Is Joined to Idols"

"Though thou, Israel, play the harlot, yet let not Judah offend; and come not ye unto Gilgal, neither go ye up to Beth-aven." Hosea warns Judah not to follow Israel's example. Gilgal and Bethel were northern sanctuaries with sacred histories; Amos warned against them too ([Amos 4:4](https://www.churchofjesuschrist.org/study/scriptures/ot/amos/4?lang=eng&id=p4#p4); [Amos 5:5](https://www.churchofjesuschrist.org/study/scriptures/ot/amos/5?lang=eng&id=p5#p5)). "Beth-aven" is probably Hosea's name for Bethel. Bethel means "house of God" ([Genesis 28:17–19](https://www.churchofjesuschrist.org/study/scriptures/ot/gen/28?lang=eng&id=p17-p19#p17)), and many readers understand Beth-aven as a mocking twist on it, "house of wickedness" or "house of nothing," because one of Jeroboam's golden calves stood there ([1 Kings 12:29](https://www.churchofjesuschrist.org/study/scriptures/ot/1-kgs/12?lang=eng&id=p29#p29)). Even the oath "The Lord liveth" had become empty at such places.

Verse 16 gives two pictures: "Israel slideth back as a backsliding heifer" (a stubborn young cow that will not be led), "now the Lord will feed them as a lamb in a large place." The second line may be bitter irony: a lamb alone in a wide open space is not cared for but exposed.

Verse 17 is one of the saddest lines in the book: "Ephraim is joined to idols: let him alone." The Lord's patience is not endless indifference. There comes a time when he allows people to have what they insist on choosing. The footnote to verse 18 gives "deeply love dishonor" for "with shame do love." Verse 19 ends with "The wind hath bound her up in her wings, and they shall be ashamed because of their sacrifices." The wind that would sweep Israel away was approaching.

**Question:** "Let him alone" is a frightening phrase. What does it teach about the role of agency in our relationship with God?

## Hosea 5: The Lord Withdraws

Chapter 5 continues the indictment of priests and rulers. It warns that both Israel and Judah will fall, mocks Israel's trust in Assyria, and ends with the Lord withdrawing until his people seek him.

### Hosea 5:1–4 — A Snare and a Net

"Hear ye this, O priests; and hearken, ye house of Israel; and give ye ear, O house of the king." Three groups are summoned: priests, people, and royal house. "Judgment is toward you, because ye have been a snare on Mizpah, and a net spread upon Tabor." Mizpah and Tabor were high places, possibly sites of shrines. Those who should have guided the people had trapped them instead.

Verse 2 is obscure but seems to say that the rebels had gone deep into slaughter, though the Lord had rebuked them all. Verse 3 is direct: "I know Ephraim, and Israel is not hid from me." Nothing is concealed from God.

Verse 4 diagnoses the condition: "They will not frame their doings to turn unto their God: for the spirit of whoredoms is in the midst of them, and they have not known the Lord." The footnote paraphrases, "Their behavior does not permit them to return to their God." Sin had become a habit that blocked the way back. Repentance can become harder the longer we delay it ([Alma 34:33–34](https://www.churchofjesuschrist.org/study/scriptures/bofm/alma/34?lang=eng&id=p33-p34#p33)).

**Question:** How can patterns of behavior make it harder to "turn unto ... God"? What habits help keep the way open?

### Hosea 5:5–7 — Seeking but Not Finding

"The pride of Israel doth testify to his face." Israel's arrogance was its own witness against it. "Therefore shall Israel and Ephraim fall in their iniquity; Judah also shall fall with them."

Verse 6 is sobering: "They shall go with their flocks and with their herds to seek the Lord; but they shall not find him; he hath withdrawn himself from them." The people would come with animals for sacrifice, but the Lord would not be found. Sacrifice without a changed heart could not reach him. Hosea will say this more directly in 6:6.

Verse 7: "They have dealt treacherously against the Lord: for they have begotten strange children." "Strange children" may mean children raised outside the covenant, or children born of the shrine practices. The footnote explains the end of the verse, "now shall a month devour them with their portions," as "Within a month they and their property shall be destroyed." Judgment would come quickly.

**Question:** Why might someone go through the motions of seeking the Lord without truly finding him? What makes the difference?

### Hosea 5:8–12 — The Alarm in Benjamin

"Blow ye the cornet in Gibeah, and the trumpet in Ramah: cry aloud at Beth-aven, after thee, O Benjamin." The footnote explains the "cornet" as a *shofar*, a ram's horn used to sound alarms. Gibeah, Ramah, and Bethel lay along the border between Israel and Judah in the territory of Benjamin. The alarm warns of an army moving through. Many scholars connect these verses with the wars of Hosea's time, when Israel and Judah fought each other, though the text does not name a specific battle.

"Ephraim shall be desolate in the day of rebuke" (verse 9). But Judah is not innocent: "The princes of Judah were like them that remove the bound" (verse 10). The footnote explains "bound" as a "boundary marker" and points to [Deuteronomy 19:14](https://www.churchofjesuschrist.org/study/scriptures/ot/deut/19?lang=eng&id=p14#p14), which forbade moving a neighbor's landmark. Judah's leaders were grabbing land, so "I will pour out my wrath upon them like water."

Verse 11: Ephraim "willingly walked after the commandment." The footnote offers "after filth" as an alternative reading, which fits the context better: Israel chose to follow worthless things. Verse 12 offers two humble images of judgment: "Therefore will I be unto Ephraim as a moth, and to the house of Judah as rottenness." Before the lion of verse 14, God works like a moth in cloth or rot in wood, slowly and almost unseen. Some decline happens gradually before anyone notices.

**Question:** The Lord compares his judgment to a moth and to rot. Why might gradual spiritual decline be more dangerous than a sudden crisis?

### Hosea 5:13–15 — "Till They Acknowledge Their Offence"

"When Ephraim saw his sickness, and Judah saw his wound, then went Ephraim to the Assyrian, and sent to king Jareb: yet could he not heal you, nor cure you of your wound." Israel did see that it was sick. It sought the wrong doctor. The northern kings paid Assyria for protection ([2 Kings 15:19–20](https://www.churchofjesuschrist.org/study/scriptures/ot/2-kgs/15?lang=eng&id=p19-p20#p19)). No Assyrian king named Jareb is known from other sources, and many translators take the word as a title rather than a name. Either way, the point is clear: Assyria could not heal Israel.

Verse 14 turns from moth to lion: "For I will be unto Ephraim as a lion, and as a young lion to the house of Judah: I, even I, will tear and go away." The God Israel had ignored was more dangerous than the empire it feared. The image of God as a lion that tears is unsettling. Hosea uses it to show that Israel's real problem was not Assyria but its broken relationship with the Lord. The lion image will return with a very different meaning in 11:10.

Verse 15: "I will go and return to my place, till they acknowledge their offence, and seek my face: in their affliction they will seek me early." The Lord withdraws not to abandon but to wait. He is waiting for confession ("acknowledge their offence") and for a sincere search ("seek my face"). "Early" may mean earnestly or diligently. Compare "those that seek me early shall find me" ([Proverbs 8:17](https://www.churchofjesuschrist.org/study/scriptures/ot/prov/8?lang=eng&id=p17#p17)).

**Question:** Why might the Lord sometimes wait for us to acknowledge our need before he heals us?

## Hosea 6: Mercy and Not Sacrifice

Chapter 6 contains two of the most quoted passages in Hosea: the call "let us return unto the Lord" with its promise of being raised "in the third day," and the Lord's declaration, "I desired mercy, and not sacrifice." It also contains a sharp warning that shallow repentance does not last.

### Hosea 6:1–3 — "Come, and Let Us Return unto the Lord"

"Come, and let us return unto the Lord: for he hath torn, and he will heal us; he hath smitten, and he will bind us up." These verses answer 5:15. The Lord said he would wait until his people sought him, and here they speak. The healing God and the tearing lion are the same God. The one who wounded in judgment is the one who can heal.

"After two days will he revive us: in the third day he will raise us up, and we shall live in his sight" (verse 2). The footnotes give "restore us to life; i.e., resurrect us" for "revive us" and "presence" for "sight." In its first setting, "two days ... third day" probably meant "very soon": Israel's restoration would come quickly. But the footnote's language opens a further reading.

Verse 3 continues: "Then shall we know, if we follow on to know the Lord: his going forth is prepared as the morning; and he shall come unto us as the rain, as the latter and former rain unto the earth." The footnote explains the rains as "spring rain ... winter rain." The Lord's coming is as dependable as dawn and as life-giving as the seasonal rains.

*Christ connection (resurrection reading):* Paul taught that Christ "rose again the third day according to the scriptures" ([1 Corinthians 15:3–4](https://www.churchofjesuschrist.org/study/scriptures/nt/1-cor/15?lang=eng&id=p3-p4#p3)), and the risen Lord told his disciples, "Thus it is written ... to rise from the dead the third day" ([Luke 24:46](https://www.churchofjesuschrist.org/study/scriptures/nt/luke/24?lang=eng&id=p46#p46)). Hosea 6:2 is one Old Testament passage Christians have connected with that teaching. Breitenstein's commentary sees in "the third day he will raise us up" a prophecy of Christ's death and resurrection ([*Old Testament Minute: Hosea*](https://scripturecentral.org/archive/books/book/old-testament-minute-hosea)). The verse speaks of "us," not of a single person. A Christian reading sees Israel's hope fulfilled in the One whose resurrection on the third day makes possible our own.

**Question:** These verses are spoken by the people, but the next verses suggest their repentance was shallow. How can you tell the difference between real repentance and a quick return that does not last?

### Hosea 6:4–5 — Goodness Like a Morning Cloud

"O Ephraim, what shall I do unto thee? O Judah, what shall I do unto thee?" The Lord's question sounds like a parent at a loss. "For your goodness is as a morning cloud, and as the early dew it goeth away." The word translated "goodness" is *hesed* again, the covenant loyalty God wanted. Israel's loyalty evaporated by midmorning, like mist over the hills.

The official lesson pairs this verse with images of lasting righteousness: "then had thy peace been as a river, and thy righteousness as the waves of the sea" ([Isaiah 48:18](https://www.churchofjesuschrist.org/study/scriptures/ot/isa/48?lang=eng&id=p18#p18)), and Lehi's wish that his sons would be like a river "continually running" and a valley "firm and steadfast, and immovable" ([1 Nephi 2:9–10](https://www.churchofjesuschrist.org/study/scriptures/bofm/1-ne/2?lang=eng&id=p9-p10#p9)) ([official lesson](https://www.churchofjesuschrist.org/study/manual/come-follow-me-for-home-and-church-old-testament-2026/47?lang=eng)).

Verse 5: "Therefore have I hewed them by the prophets; I have slain them by the words of my mouth: and thy judgments are as the light that goeth forth." The prophets' words cut like tools on stone. The word of God both wounds and reveals, like light breaking in the morning.

**Question:** What practices help turn short-lived spiritual feelings, "morning cloud" goodness, into lasting faithfulness?

### Hosea 6:6 — "For I Desired Mercy, and Not Sacrifice"

"For I desired mercy, and not sacrifice; and the knowledge of God more than burnt offerings." The footnote explains "mercy" as "charity, or lovingkindness" and points to Matthew 12:7. *Scripture Helps* explains that the Lord was not rejecting the law of sacrifice, which he had given. He valued heartfelt devotion and compassion more. Israel kept up its sacrifices while it "transgressed the covenant" (verse 7). The sacrifices had become a substitute for faithfulness instead of an expression of it. Samuel had taught that "to obey is better than sacrifice" ([1 Samuel 15:22](https://www.churchofjesuschrist.org/study/scriptures/ot/1-sam/15?lang=eng&id=p22#p22)), and Micah summed up what the Lord requires: "to do justly, and to love mercy, and to walk humbly with thy God" ([Micah 6:8](https://www.churchofjesuschrist.org/study/scriptures/ot/micah/6?lang=eng&id=p8#p8)).

*Scripture Helps* adds a Book of Mormon connection: the purpose of the law of sacrifice was to point to "that great and last sacrifice" of the Son of God, which brings "the bowels of mercy" ([Alma 34:14–15](https://www.churchofjesuschrist.org/study/scriptures/bofm/alma/34?lang=eng&id=p14-p15#p14)). Israel performed the sacrifices without seeing their purpose. The law was meant to turn hearts to the Redeemer and lead to repentance.

*Christ connection (New Testament reading):* Jesus quoted this verse twice. When Pharisees objected that he ate with publicans and sinners, he said, "go ye and learn what that meaneth, I will have mercy, and not sacrifice: for I am not come to call the righteous, but sinners to repentance" ([Matthew 9:13](https://www.churchofjesuschrist.org/study/scriptures/nt/matt/9?lang=eng&id=p13#p13); see [Matthew 9:10–12](https://www.churchofjesuschrist.org/study/scriptures/nt/matt/9?lang=eng&id=p10-p12#p10)). When they condemned his hungry disciples for plucking grain on the Sabbath, he said, "if ye had known what this meaneth, I will have mercy, and not sacrifice, ye would not have condemned the guiltless" ([Matthew 12:7](https://www.churchofjesuschrist.org/study/scriptures/nt/matt/12?lang=eng&id=p7#p7); see [Matthew 12:3–8](https://www.churchofjesuschrist.org/study/scriptures/nt/matt/12?lang=eng&id=p3-p8#p3)). Both times he used Hosea against people who used religious rules to shut others out. John Hilton III's slides pair these passages with Jesus's warning to those who tithe mint and anise but omit "the weightier matters of the law, judgment, mercy, and faith" ([Matthew 23:23](https://www.churchofjesuschrist.org/study/scriptures/nt/matt/23?lang=eng&id=p23#p23)), and ask a pointed question: where might we choose "sacrifice" over "mercy" today ([Hilton, Class 46 slides](https://www.dropbox.com/scl/fi/cbft6sna5osmeec29m0w2/46-Hosea-Joel.pdf?rlkey=7tgk1qjl09srdxa8r6ell7nbg&st=22efq01e&dl=1))?

*Restoration reading:* When the resurrected Savior ended the law of sacrifice among the Nephites, he did not end sacrifice. He asked instead for "a broken heart and a contrite spirit" ([3 Nephi 9:19–20](https://www.churchofjesuschrist.org/study/scriptures/bofm/3-ne/9?lang=eng&id=p19-p20#p19)).

**Question:** Where might you be tempted to choose "sacrifice," visible religious performance, over "mercy," loyal love for God and others? What would it look like to choose mercy this week?

### Hosea 6:7–11 — Treachery on the Road

"But they like men have transgressed the covenant: there have they dealt treacherously against me." The footnote gives "Adam" as an alternative for "men." Read that way, Israel broke its covenant as Adam broke his in the garden. Either way, the charge is covenant betrayal, the opposite of *hesed*.

Verses 8–9 name places: "Gilead is a city of them that work iniquity, and is polluted with blood." Gilead lay east of the Jordan. "As troops of robbers wait for a man, so the company of priests murder in the way by consent." The footnote explains "in the way" as "on the road to Shechem." Shechem was a city of refuge ([Joshua 20:7](https://www.churchofjesuschrist.org/study/scriptures/ot/josh/20?lang=eng&id=p7#p7)), a place where those fleeing for safety should have found protection. Priests, of all people, were preying on travelers on the road to a place of safety.

Verse 10: "I have seen an horrible thing in the house of Israel." Verse 11 turns to Judah: "Also, O Judah, he hath set an harvest for thee, when I returned the captivity of my people." "Harvest" can mean judgment, the reaping of what has been sown; it can also point to the restoration that follows. The line looks ahead to a day when the Lord brings his people back.

**Question:** Verse 9 describes religious leaders preying on the vulnerable. What responsibility do people with spiritual authority have to protect those who come to them for safety?

## Between the Chapters: Hosea 7–9

This week's reading skips Hosea 7–9. Those chapters continue the same themes: Israel's leaders are like an overheated oven ([Hosea 7:4–7](https://www.churchofjesuschrist.org/study/scriptures/ot/hosea/7?lang=eng&id=p4-p7#p4)); Ephraim is "a cake not turned," half-baked ([7:8](https://www.churchofjesuschrist.org/study/scriptures/ot/hosea/7?lang=eng&id=p8#p8)), and "a silly dove" flying between Egypt and Assyria ([7:11](https://www.churchofjesuschrist.org/study/scriptures/ot/hosea/7?lang=eng&id=p11#p11)); and "they have sown the wind, and they shall reap the whirlwind" ([8:7](https://www.churchofjesuschrist.org/study/scriptures/ot/hosea/8?lang=eng&id=p7#p7)). Chapter 9 warns that Israel will "eat unclean things in Assyria" ([9:3](https://www.churchofjesuschrist.org/study/scriptures/ot/hosea/9?lang=eng&id=p3#p3)). Reading these chapters quickly will help the reading pick up smoothly in chapter 10.

## Hosea 10: Break Up Your Fallow Ground

Chapter 10 uses farming language throughout. Israel is an empty vine, its judgment sprouts like weeds, and it is a heifer who prefers easy work. At the center stands the invitation of verse 12: "Sow to yourselves in righteousness ... for it is time to seek the Lord." The chapter also describes the coming fall of Samaria and its king.

### Hosea 10:1–2 — An Empty Vine and a Divided Heart

"Israel is an empty vine, he bringeth forth fruit unto himself." Israel was a vine planted by the Lord (compare [Isaiah 5:1–7](https://www.churchofjesuschrist.org/study/scriptures/ot/isa/5?lang=eng&id=p1-p7#p1)), and the footnote points to the Topical Guide on the vineyard of the Lord. But its fruit was "unto himself." The more prosperous it grew, the more altars it built: "according to the multitude of his fruit he hath increased the altars; according to the goodness of his land they have made goodly images." Prosperity was being spent on idolatry. (Breitenstein's commentary describes Israel as an "empty vine" punished for divided loyalty.)

Verse 2 names the root: "Their heart is divided." The footnote gives "guilty" for "faulty." A divided heart cannot stay loyal. "He shall break down their altars, he shall spoil their images."

*Christ connection (New Testament reading):* Jesus said, "I am the vine, ye are the branches: He that abideth in me ... bringeth forth much fruit: for without me ye can do nothing" ([John 15:5](https://www.churchofjesuschrist.org/study/scriptures/nt/john/15?lang=eng&id=p5#p5)). Israel's fruit was "unto himself." Fruit that lasts comes from abiding in Christ.

**Question:** How can increased blessings lead to a divided heart? What helps you keep your heart whole as your circumstances improve?

### Hosea 10:3–4 — "We Have No King"

"For now they shall say, We have no king, because we feared not the Lord; what then should a king do to us?" Israel would lose its monarchy, and the people would admit that a king could not have saved them anyway. Their real problem was that they "feared not the Lord."

Verse 4: "They have spoken words, swearing falsely in making a covenant: thus judgment springeth up as hemlock in the furrows of the field." Their covenants, with God, with each other, and with foreign powers, were empty words. Justice itself had become poisonous, like a weed spreading through plowed rows. Amos makes a similar charge: "ye have turned judgment into gall" ([Amos 6:12](https://www.churchofjesuschrist.org/study/scriptures/ot/amos/6?lang=eng&id=p12#p12)).

**Question:** What happens in a community when people's words and promises can no longer be trusted?

### Hosea 10:5–8 — The Calf Carried Away

"The inhabitants of Samaria shall fear because of the calves of Beth-aven." The golden calf at Bethel ("Beth-aven") was the pride of the northern kingdom. Now the people and its priests will mourn "for the glory thereof, because it is departed from it." The idol could not protect itself. "It shall be also carried unto Assyria for a present to king Jareb" (verse 6). The god Israel worshipped would become tribute for the empire.

Verse 7: "As for Samaria, her king is cut off as the foam upon the water." A king who seemed powerful is as insubstantial as foam. Verse 8: "The high places also of Aven, the sin of Israel, shall be destroyed: the thorn and the thistle shall come up on their altars." Weeds will grow on the abandoned shrines. Then the terrified people "shall say to the mountains, Cover us; and to the hills, Fall on us."

*Christ connection (New Testament reading):* On the way to the cross, Jesus told the women who mourned for him that days of judgment were coming when people would "say to the mountains, Fall on us; and to the hills, Cover us" ([Luke 23:30](https://www.churchofjesuschrist.org/study/scriptures/nt/luke/23?lang=eng&id=p30#p30)). John's Revelation uses the same words for those who hide "from the wrath of the Lamb" ([Revelation 6:16](https://www.churchofjesuschrist.org/study/scriptures/nt/rev/6?lang=eng&id=p16#p16)). Alma described the wicked wishing that the rocks and mountains would fall on them to hide them from God's presence ([Alma 12:14](https://www.churchofjesuschrist.org/study/scriptures/bofm/alma/12?lang=eng&id=p14#p14)).

**Question:** Israel's idol could not save itself. What things in modern life promise security but could not survive a real crisis?

### Hosea 10:9–11 — From the Days of Gibeah

"O Israel, thou hast sinned from the days of Gibeah." Gibeah was the scene of one of the darkest stories in the Bible, the abuse and murder of a Levite's concubine and the civil war that followed ([Judges 19:22](https://www.churchofjesuschrist.org/study/scriptures/ot/judg/19?lang=eng&id=p22#p22); [Judges 20:13](https://www.churchofjesuschrist.org/study/scriptures/ot/judg/20?lang=eng&id=p13#p13)). The book of Judges summarizes that era: "every man did that which was right in his own eyes" ([Judges 21:25](https://www.churchofjesuschrist.org/study/scriptures/ot/judg/21?lang=eng&id=p25#p25)). Hosea says Israel's sin is as old as that atrocity. "There they stood": they have persisted in it ever since.

Verse 10: "It is in my desire that I should chastise them; and the people shall be gathered against them, when they shall bind themselves in their two furrows." The meaning of "two furrows" is uncertain. It may refer to Israel's double sin or to being yoked for punishment.

Verse 11 returns to farming: "Ephraim is as an heifer that is taught, and loveth to tread out the corn." Threshing was easy work for a young cow, and under the law she could eat while she worked ([Deuteronomy 25:4](https://www.churchofjesuschrist.org/study/scriptures/ot/deut/25?lang=eng&id=p4#p4)). Ephraim preferred the easy, rewarding labor. "But I passed over upon her fair neck": now a yoke would be placed on her neck for harder work. "Judah shall plow, and Jacob shall break his clods." All of Israel would face hard service.

**Question:** Ephraim preferred easy work. When has the Lord moved you from comfortable service to harder work that helped you grow?

### Hosea 10:12 — "It Is Time to Seek the Lord"

"Sow to yourselves in righteousness, reap in mercy; break up your fallow ground: for it is time to seek the Lord, till he come and rain righteousness upon you." This verse is the heart of the chapter. After all the images of weeds and yokes, the Lord offers a way forward.

"Sow ... in righteousness, reap in mercy." *Hesed* again: those who sow righteousness will reap covenant love. "Break up your fallow ground." Fallow ground is land left unplowed. It has grown hard and weedy. Jeremiah used the same image: "Break up your fallow ground, and sow not among thorns" ([Jeremiah 4:3](https://www.churchofjesuschrist.org/study/scriptures/ot/jer/4?lang=eng&id=p3#p3)). Before seed can grow, hard ground must be broken. "For it is time to seek the Lord." The opportunity is now. "Till he come and rain righteousness upon you." The farmer prepares the soil, but the rain must come from heaven.

The official lesson's ideas for children use this verse to teach that "it is time to seek the Lord," suggesting a clock showing ways to seek him and pretend planting and harvesting ([official lesson](https://www.churchofjesuschrist.org/study/manual/come-follow-me-for-home-and-church-old-testament-2026/47?lang=eng)). Paul taught the same principle: "whatsoever a man soweth, that shall he also reap ... let us not be weary in well doing: for in due season we shall reap, if we faint not" ([Galatians 6:7–9](https://www.churchofjesuschrist.org/study/scriptures/nt/gal/6?lang=eng&id=p7-p9#p7)). The Lord told early Latter-day Saints, "if ye sow good ye shall also reap good for your reward" ([Doctrine and Covenants 6:33](https://www.churchofjesuschrist.org/study/scriptures/dc-testament/dc/6?lang=eng&id=p33#p33)).

**Question:** What "fallow ground" in your life has grown hard from neglect? What would it take to break it up this week?

### Hosea 10:13–15 — Plowing Wickedness

"Ye have plowed wickedness, ye have reaped iniquity; ye have eaten the fruit of lies." Verse 13 is the opposite of verse 12. Israel had sown the wrong seed and reaped the expected harvest. The reason: "because thou didst trust in thy way, in the multitude of thy mighty men." Israel trusted its army. Compare "Some trust in chariots, and some in horses: but we will remember the name of the Lord our God" ([Psalm 20:7](https://www.churchofjesuschrist.org/study/scriptures/ot/ps/20?lang=eng&id=p7#p7)).

Verse 14 describes the war to come: "all thy fortresses shall be spoiled, as Shalman spoiled Beth-arbel in the day of battle: the mother was dashed in pieces upon her children." The identities of Shalman and Beth-arbel are uncertain; Hosea's hearers apparently knew the event. The brutality described was a real practice in ancient warfare. Hosea does not present it as good. He warns that a nation that trusts in violence will suffer violence. The verse is painful to read, and it should be. It shows the human cost of the path Israel had chosen.

Verse 15: "So shall Beth-el do unto you because of your great wickedness: in a morning shall the king of Israel utterly be cut off." Bethel, the "house of God" turned to idol worship, would be the cause of Israel's ruin. The last king of Israel, Hoshea, was imprisoned by Assyria and his kingdom ended ([2 Kings 17:4–6](https://www.churchofjesuschrist.org/study/scriptures/ot/2-kgs/17?lang=eng&id=p4-p6#p4)).

**Question:** Israel trusted "in the multitude of thy mighty men." What do people today trust for security instead of God?

## Hosea 11: How Shall I Give Thee Up?

Chapter 11 is the emotional center of the book. For a moment Hosea drops the marriage image and speaks of God as a parent who taught a child to walk. The chapter moves from memory to grief to an astonishing declaration of mercy: "I will not execute the fierceness of mine anger ... for I am God, and not man."

### Hosea 11:1–2 — "Out of Egypt"

"When Israel was a child, then I loved him, and called my son out of Egypt." The Lord had told Pharaoh, "Israel is my son, even my firstborn" ([Exodus 4:22](https://www.churchofjesuschrist.org/study/scriptures/ot/ex/4?lang=eng&id=p22#p22)). The Exodus was an act of fatherly love. The Bible Dictionary cites this verse as an example of Hosea's central theme, God's love redeeming his people from Egypt.

Verse 2 shows the sad contrast: "As they called them, so they went from them: they sacrificed unto Baalim, and burned incense to graven images." The more the prophets called, the further Israel went.

*Christ connection (New Testament reading):* Matthew records that Joseph took the young Jesus and Mary to Egypt, and that they stayed there until Herod died, "that it might be fulfilled which was spoken of the Lord by the prophet, saying, Out of Egypt have I called my son" ([Matthew 2:15](https://www.churchofjesuschrist.org/study/scriptures/nt/matt/2?lang=eng&id=p15#p15)). The chapter heading in the Church's edition calls Israel's Exodus a similitude of the Savior's coming out of Egypt as a child. In Hosea, the verse looks back to Israel's history. Matthew sees Jesus living Israel's story over again, but faithfully. Where Israel, God's son, turned away, Jesus, God's Son, remained true.

**Question:** Matthew saw Jesus reliving Israel's story faithfully. How does it help you to know that Christ walked the path where others had failed?

### Hosea 11:3–4 — Cords of a Man, Bands of Love

"I taught Ephraim also to go, taking them by their arms; but they knew not that I healed them." The picture is of a parent teaching a toddler to walk, holding the child up by the arms. Israel did not even realize who had been holding it up, or who had healed its falls.

Verse 4: "I drew them with cords of a man, with bands of love: and I was to them as they that take off the yoke on their jaws, and I laid meat unto them." "Cords of a man" are human ties, the kind used to guide a child, not ropes for an animal. God led by love, not force. He was like a farmer who lifts the yoke so an ox can eat, and bends down to feed it. Breitenstein's commentary notes the tenderness of these images and Israel's failure to recognize it.

*Christ connection (devotional reading):* Jeremiah heard the Lord say, "I have loved thee with an everlasting love: therefore with lovingkindness have I drawn thee" ([Jeremiah 31:3](https://www.churchofjesuschrist.org/study/scriptures/ot/jer/31?lang=eng&id=p3#p3)). Jesus said, "I, if I be lifted up from the earth, will draw all men unto me" ([John 12:32](https://www.churchofjesuschrist.org/study/scriptures/nt/john/12?lang=eng&id=p32#p32); compare [3 Nephi 27:14](https://www.churchofjesuschrist.org/study/scriptures/bofm/3-ne/27?lang=eng&id=p14#p14)). The Lord still draws with bands of love.

**Question:** When have you later realized that the Lord was "taking [you] by [your] arms," holding you up and healing you, even though you did not recognize it at the time?

### Hosea 11:5–7 — Bent to Backsliding

"He shall not return into the land of Egypt, but the Assyrian shall be his king, because they refused to return." The word "return" is used twice with two meanings. Israel would not go back to slavery in Egypt, but it would fall under Assyria, because it "refused to return" to the Lord. (Some passages in Hosea do speak of a return to Egypt as a symbol of bondage, such as [Hosea 8:13](https://www.churchofjesuschrist.org/study/scriptures/ot/hosea/8?lang=eng&id=p13#p13); here the meaning is that Assyria, not Egypt, will rule them.)

Verse 6: "the sword shall abide on his cities ... because of their own counsels." Israel's own plans would bring its ruin. Verse 7: "my people are bent to backsliding from me: though they called them to the most High, none at all would exalt him." The footnote points to the Topical Guide on ingratitude. Israel was bent, warped toward turning away.

**Question:** What does it mean to be "bent to backsliding"? How does the Lord help straighten a heart that keeps leaning away?

### Hosea 11:8–9 — "Mine Heart Is Turned Within Me"

"How shall I give thee up, Ephraim? how shall I deliver thee, Israel? how shall I make thee as Admah? how shall I set thee as Zeboim?" Admah and Zeboim were cities destroyed with Sodom and Gomorrah ([Deuteronomy 29:23](https://www.churchofjesuschrist.org/study/scriptures/ot/deut/29?lang=eng&id=p23#p23)). By the logic of verses 5–7, Israel deserved the same fate. But the Lord cannot bring himself to do it. "Mine heart is turned within me, my repentings are kindled together." Breitenstein's commentary puts it simply: God "did not want Israel to suffer a similar fate because He had compassion for His people."

The Joseph Smith Translation adds a phrase to verse 8. The Church's footnote gives it as "toward thee, and my mercies are extended to gather thee." The JST makes explicit what the verse implies: the Lord's turning heart leads to gathering.

Verse 9: "I will not execute the fierceness of mine anger, I will not return to destroy Ephraim: for I am God, and not man; the Holy One in the midst of thee: and I will not enter into the city." The footnote gives "come in anger" for "enter." The reason for mercy is God's nature: "I am God, and not man." A human being, wronged this deeply, might give up. God does not. His holiness is not only his distance from sin but his steady faithfulness in the face of it.

This passage does not mean that Israel escaped all consequences. Hosea knew the northern kingdom would fall. But it shows that judgment is never the last word with God, and that he takes no pleasure in it.

*Christ connection (New Testament reading):* Jesus wept over Jerusalem: "how often would I have gathered thy children together, as a hen doth gather her brood under her wings, and ye would not!" ([Luke 13:34](https://www.churchofjesuschrist.org/study/scriptures/nt/luke/13?lang=eng&id=p34#p34)). The JST's "my mercies are extended to gather thee" and the Savior's lament express the same heart.

**Question:** "I am God, and not man." How does God's mercy differ from human patience? How does knowing that change the way you approach him after you have sinned?

### Hosea 11:10–12 — The Lion Roars, the Children Come Home

"They shall walk after the Lord: he shall roar like a lion: when he shall roar, then the children shall tremble from the west." In 5:14 the lion roared to tear. Now the lion roars to call his children home. They come trembling, but they come. Breitenstein reads the roar as calling the exiles home.

Verse 11: "They shall tremble as a bird out of Egypt, and as a dove out of the land of Assyria: and I will place them in their houses, saith the Lord." Ephraim had been "a silly dove" flitting between Egypt and Assyria (7:11). Now doves return from both places, gathered home.

Verse 12 changes tone abruptly: "Ephraim compasseth me about with lies, and the house of Israel with deceit: but Judah yet ruleth with God, and is faithful with the saints." The footnote gives "walks" for "ruleth." The verse contrasts Ephraim's deceit with Judah's faithfulness, though 12:2 will show that Judah too faces judgment. The Bible Dictionary treats 11:12 through the end of the book as one movement toward "a final redemption."

*Restoration reading:* Latter-day Saints see the gathering of Israel from every direction as a work of the last days. The footnote to [Hosea 1:11](https://www.churchofjesuschrist.org/study/scriptures/ot/hosea/1?lang=eng&id=p11#p11) points to the Topical Guide on that gathering.

**Question:** The same lion that tore in judgment now roars to gather. How can the Lord's call feel frightening and welcoming at the same time?

## Hosea 12: Remember Jacob

Chapter 12 uses the story of Jacob, Israel's ancestor, to teach Israel. Jacob was a grasper and a deceiver, but he wrestled with God, wept, prayed, and was changed. Hosea calls the nation to do the same: "turn thou to thy God: keep mercy and judgment, and wait on thy God continually."

### Hosea 12:1–2 — Feeding on Wind

"Ephraim feedeth on wind, and followeth after the east wind." The east wind was the hot, dry wind off the desert that withered crops. Israel was chasing what could not nourish it and would destroy it. "He daily increaseth lies and desolation; and they do make a covenant with the Assyrians, and oil is carried into Egypt." Israel played both empires against each other. Its last king made a treaty with Assyria and then secretly sought help from Egypt ([2 Kings 17:3–4](https://www.churchofjesuschrist.org/study/scriptures/ot/2-kgs/17?lang=eng&id=p3-p4#p3)). Olive oil was a valuable export, perhaps sent as a gift to win Egyptian support.

Verse 2: "The Lord hath also a controversy with Judah, and will punish Jacob according to his ways; according to his doings will he recompense him." Judah is not exempt. The name "Jacob" then becomes the subject of the next verses.

**Question:** "Ephraim feedeth on wind." What modern pursuits leave people hungry no matter how much of them they consume?

### Hosea 12:3–6 — Jacob Wept and Prevailed

"He took his brother by the heel in the womb, and by his strength he had power with God." Jacob's name was tied to the heel he grabbed at birth ([Genesis 25:26](https://www.churchofjesuschrist.org/study/scriptures/ot/gen/25?lang=eng&id=p26#p26)), and his life began with grasping. Later he wrestled through the night and would not let go until he was blessed ([Genesis 32:26](https://www.churchofjesuschrist.org/study/scriptures/ot/gen/32?lang=eng&id=p26#p26)). The Lord renamed him Israel, "for as a prince hast thou power with God and with men, and hast prevailed" ([Genesis 32:28](https://www.churchofjesuschrist.org/study/scriptures/ot/gen/32?lang=eng&id=p28#p28)).

Verse 4 adds details not in Genesis: "Yea, he had power over the angel, and prevailed: he wept, and made supplication unto him." Hosea emphasizes that Jacob prevailed by weeping and pleading. His strength in the end was surrender. "He found him in Beth-el, and there he spake with us." The footnote gives "him" as an alternative for "us." Bethel was where God met Jacob twice ([Genesis 28:10–19](https://www.churchofjesuschrist.org/study/scriptures/ot/gen/28?lang=eng&id=p10-p19#p10); [Genesis 35:15](https://www.churchofjesuschrist.org/study/scriptures/ot/gen/35?lang=eng&id=p15#p15)). Breitenstein's commentary connects these verses with Jacob's heel-catching and wrestling. The irony is sharp: the place where Jacob met the true God had become Israel's center of calf worship.

Verse 5: "Even the Lord God of hosts; the Lord is his memorial." The God Jacob met is the Lord whose name is his "memorial," the name by which he is remembered ([Exodus 3:15](https://www.churchofjesuschrist.org/study/scriptures/ot/ex/3?lang=eng&id=p15#p15)).

Verse 6 draws the lesson: "Therefore turn thou to thy God: keep mercy and judgment, and wait on thy God continually." Like Jacob, Israel must turn, practice *hesed* ("mercy") and justice ("judgment"), and wait on God. The footnote points to the Topical Guide on conversion.

**Question:** Jacob prevailed by weeping and making supplication. What does his story teach about how real spiritual strength is found?

### Hosea 12:7–9 — The Merchant with False Balances

"He is a merchant, the balances of deceit are in his hand: he loveth to oppress." The scales were rigged. Amos condemned the same practice: "making the ephah small, and the shekel great, and falsifying the balances by deceit" ([Amos 8:5](https://www.churchofjesuschrist.org/study/scriptures/ot/amos/8?lang=eng&id=p5#p5)). "A false balance is abomination to the Lord" ([Proverbs 11:1](https://www.churchofjesuschrist.org/study/scriptures/ot/prov/11?lang=eng&id=p1#p1)).

Verse 8 gives Ephraim's self-justification: "Yet I am become rich, I have found me out substance: in all my labours they shall find none iniquity in me that were sin." Success seemed to prove innocence. Compare the Laodiceans: "thou sayest, I am rich, and increased with goods, and have need of nothing; and knowest not that thou art wretched, and miserable, and poor" ([Revelation 3:17](https://www.churchofjesuschrist.org/study/scriptures/nt/rev/3?lang=eng&id=p17#p17)).

Verse 9 answers: "And I that am the Lord thy God from the land of Egypt will yet make thee to dwell in tabernacles, as in the days of the solemn feast." The footnote gives "tents" for "tabernacles" and points to Leviticus 23. At the Feast of Tabernacles, Israel lived in booths to remember the wilderness ([Leviticus 23:42–43](https://www.churchofjesuschrist.org/study/scriptures/ot/lev/23?lang=eng&id=p42-p43#p42)). The Lord will take Israel back to the tents. That could mean losing its houses, or it could mean a fresh start, back in the wilderness where the relationship began (compare 2:14).

**Question:** Ephraim thought wealth proved it was innocent. Why is prosperity such a poor measure of righteousness?

### Hosea 12:10–11 — Visions and Similitudes

"I have also spoken by the prophets, and I have multiplied visions, and used similitudes, by the ministry of the prophets." The Lord had not been silent. He had spoken clearly and often, in direct words, visions, and comparisons. Hosea's own marriage and children were among those similitudes.

*Restoration reading:* The Book of Mormon and the Pearl of Great Price teach that the Lord's similitudes point to Christ. Nephi wrote that "all things which have been given of God from the beginning of the world, unto man, are the typifying of him" ([2 Nephi 11:4](https://www.churchofjesuschrist.org/study/scriptures/bofm/2-ne/11?lang=eng&id=p4#p4)). The Lord told Adam that "all things have their likeness, and all things are created and made to bear record of me" ([Moses 6:63](https://www.churchofjesuschrist.org/study/scriptures/pgp/moses/6?lang=eng&id=p63#p63)).

Verse 11: "Is there iniquity in Gilead? surely they are vanity: they sacrifice bullocks in Gilgal; yea, their altars are as heaps in the furrows of the fields." The altars of Gilgal will become heaps of stones in the fields. Shrines that were meant to be holy would end as rubble.

**Question:** The Lord says he "used similitudes" to teach. What similitudes, symbols, or comparisons have helped you understand the Savior?

### Hosea 12:12–14 — By a Prophet

"And Jacob fled into the country of Syria, and Israel served for a wife, and for a wife he kept sheep." Jacob fled from Esau to Laban's household in Haran and served seven years twice for Rachel ([Genesis 29:20](https://www.churchofjesuschrist.org/study/scriptures/ot/gen/29?lang=eng&id=p20#p20)). The verse reminds Israel of its humble beginnings: a fugitive shepherd who worked for his wife.

Verse 13: "And by a prophet the Lord brought Israel out of Egypt, and by a prophet was he preserved." The prophet is Moses. The contrast with verse 12 may be intentional: Jacob tended sheep for a wife; the Lord, through a prophet, tended Israel. Israel's existence depended on prophets. Moses foretold that the Lord would raise up another "Prophet ... like unto me" ([Deuteronomy 18:15](https://www.churchofjesuschrist.org/study/scriptures/ot/deut/18?lang=eng&id=p15#p15)), which Latter-day Saints and other Christians understand as a prophecy of Christ ([Acts 3:22–23](https://www.churchofjesuschrist.org/study/scriptures/nt/acts/3?lang=eng&id=p22-p23#p22); [3 Nephi 20:23](https://www.churchofjesuschrist.org/study/scriptures/bofm/3-ne/20?lang=eng&id=p23#p23)). Now Israel was rejecting the prophets sent to preserve it.

Verse 14: "Ephraim provoked him to anger most bitterly: therefore shall he leave his blood upon him, and his reproach shall his Lord return unto him." The footnote explains "blood" as "guilt." Israel would bear the consequences of its own choices.

**Question:** "By a prophet was he preserved." How have prophets helped preserve you and your family?

## Hosea 13: No Saviour beside Me

Chapter 13 is the last and fiercest judgment speech in Hosea, but it also contains two of the book's clearest testimonies of the Redeemer: "there is no saviour beside me" and "I will redeem them from death."

### Hosea 13:1–3 — Kissing Calves

"When Ephraim spake trembling, he exalted himself in Israel; but when he offended in Baal, he died." Ephraim was once a respected tribe whose word made others tremble. Its turn to Baal brought spiritual death, as at Baal-peor in the wilderness ([Numbers 25:3](https://www.churchofjesuschrist.org/study/scriptures/ot/num/25?lang=eng&id=p3#p3)).

Verse 2: "And now they sin more and more, and have made them molten images of their silver ... all of it the work of the craftsmen: they say of them, Let the men that sacrifice kiss the calves." Kissing an idol was an act of worship. Elijah had been told of seven thousand in Israel whose mouths had not kissed Baal ([1 Kings 19:18](https://www.churchofjesuschrist.org/study/scriptures/ot/1-kgs/19?lang=eng&id=p18#p18)). The absurdity is plain: human beings kissing calves made by human hands.

Verse 3 describes how short-lived the idolaters will be: "as the morning cloud, and as the early dew that passeth away, as the chaff that is driven with the whirlwind out of the floor, and as the smoke out of the chimney." The first two images repeat 6:4. Compare also "the ungodly ... are like the chaff which the wind driveth away" ([Psalm 1:4](https://www.churchofjesuschrist.org/study/scriptures/ot/ps/1?lang=eng&id=p4#p4)). In the Book of Mormon, King Limhi quoted the Lord's warning that if his people "sow filthiness they shall reap the chaff thereof in the whirlwind" ([Mosiah 7:30](https://www.churchofjesuschrist.org/study/scriptures/bofm/mosiah/7?lang=eng&id=p30#p30)). Thompson's *Interpreter* article notes that critics point to this resemblance with Hosea 13:3 and argues that it does not provide an adequate foundation for concluding that the brass plates contained Hosea (Thompson, *Interpreter* 45: 106 n. 63).

**Question:** Hosea pictures people kissing calves they made themselves. What are some ways people today bow to things of their own making?

### Hosea 13:4–6 — "There Is No Saviour beside Me"

"Yet I am the Lord thy God from the land of Egypt, and thou shalt know no god but me: for there is no saviour beside me." This verse echoes the first of the Ten Commandments ([Exodus 20:2–3](https://www.churchofjesuschrist.org/study/scriptures/ot/ex/20?lang=eng&id=p2-p3#p2)). Israel's identity began when God saved it from Egypt. No other god had done that, and none could. Isaiah says the same: "I, even I, am the Lord; and beside me there is no saviour" ([Isaiah 43:11](https://www.churchofjesuschrist.org/study/scriptures/ot/isa/43?lang=eng&id=p11#p11)). Breitenstein's commentary highlights this verse.

Verse 5: "I did know thee in the wilderness, in the land of great drought." God knew Israel when it had nothing. Verse 6 traces the familiar pattern: "According to their pasture, so were they filled; they were filled, and their heart was exalted; therefore have they forgotten me." Full, then proud, then forgetful. Moses warned of exactly this ([Deuteronomy 8:11–14](https://www.churchofjesuschrist.org/study/scriptures/ot/deut/8?lang=eng&id=p11-p14#p11); compare [Deuteronomy 32:15](https://www.churchofjesuschrist.org/study/scriptures/ot/deut/32?lang=eng&id=p15#p15)).

*Christ connection (Restoration reading):* The Church's footnote to "saviour" points to the Topical Guide entries "Jesus Christ, Jehovah" and "Jesus Christ, Redeemer." Latter-day Saints understand the Lord (Jehovah) of the Old Testament to be the premortal Jesus Christ. The God who saved Israel from Egypt is the Savior. Peter declared that "there is none other name under heaven given among men, whereby we must be saved" ([Acts 4:12](https://www.churchofjesuschrist.org/study/scriptures/nt/acts/4?lang=eng&id=p12#p12)), and King Benjamin taught that salvation comes "only in and through the name of Christ, the Lord Omnipotent" ([Mosiah 3:17](https://www.churchofjesuschrist.org/study/scriptures/bofm/mosiah/3?lang=eng&id=p17#p17)).

**Question:** "There is no saviour beside me." What other "saviors" do people look to today? Why can none of them do what Christ does?

### Hosea 13:7–11 — Lion, Leopard, and Bear

"Therefore I will be unto them as a lion: as a leopard by the way will I observe them: I will meet them as a bear that is bereaved of her whelps, and will rend the caul of their heart." The footnote explains "caul" as "chamber." Hosea piles up images of wild animals to show how terrifying judgment would be. The God who fed Israel in the wilderness now meets it as a predator. A bear robbed of her cubs was proverbially dangerous ([2 Samuel 17:8](https://www.churchofjesuschrist.org/study/scriptures/ot/2-sam/17?lang=eng&id=p8#p8)). Perhaps the image also implies grief: God is like a mother who has lost her children.

Verse 9 is the key: "O Israel, thou hast destroyed thyself; but in me is thine help." Israel's destruction was self-inflicted. Its help was only in the Lord. Verses 10–11 return to the theme of kings: "I will be thy king: where is any other that may save thee in all thy cities? ... I gave thee a king in mine anger, and took him away in my wrath." When Israel first demanded a king, the Lord told Samuel, "they have not rejected thee, but they have rejected me" ([1 Samuel 8:7](https://www.churchofjesuschrist.org/study/scriptures/ot/1-sam/8?lang=eng&id=p7#p7)). Israel's kings could not save it.

**Question:** "Thou hast destroyed thyself; but in me is thine help." How does this verse hold together personal responsibility and divine rescue?

### Hosea 13:12–14 — "O Death, I Will Be Thy Plagues"

"The iniquity of Ephraim is bound up; his sin is hid." Ephraim's sin is wrapped and stored like a document kept for evidence. Verse 13 uses an image of childbirth: "The sorrows of a travailing woman shall come upon him: he is an unwise son; for he should not stay long in the place of the breaking forth of children." Israel is pictured as a child who will not come out at the moment of birth. Its crisis could be a new beginning, but it refuses to move.

Then comes verse 14: "I will ransom them from the power of the grave; I will redeem them from death: O death, I will be thy plagues; O grave, I will be thy destruction: repentance shall be hid from mine eyes." The footnote gives "compassion" for "repentance."

This verse can be read two ways. In its immediate setting, some modern translations read the first lines as questions: will I ransom them from death? The answer would then be no, and the verse would call death and the grave to come with their plagues, with "compassion ... hid from mine eyes." Read that way, it continues the judgment. The King James Version, followed by the Church's chapter heading, reads the lines as promises: "He ransoms from the grave and redeems from death." Both readings take death seriously. The question is who has the last word.

*Christ connection (New Testament and Book of Mormon reading):* Paul quoted this verse in his great chapter on the Resurrection: "O death, where is thy sting? O grave, where is thy victory? ... But thanks be to God, which giveth us the victory through our Lord Jesus Christ" ([1 Corinthians 15:55–57](https://www.churchofjesuschrist.org/study/scriptures/nt/1-cor/15?lang=eng&id=p55-p57#p55)). Abinadi used the same language: "the grave hath no victory, and the sting of death is swallowed up in Christ" ([Mosiah 16:8](https://www.churchofjesuschrist.org/study/scriptures/bofm/mosiah/16?lang=eng&id=p8#p8)). Breitenstein's commentary connects 13:14 with 1 Corinthians 15:55, and John Hilton III's slides present it as "a Hosea verse Paul quoted." Whatever the verse meant in Hosea's courtroom, Christ's Resurrection settled who wins. The Church's footnotes link it to the Topical Guide entries on the Resurrection and on Jesus Christ's death and resurrection.

**Question:** Hosea's chapter of judgment contains a promise of victory over death. How does the Resurrection of Jesus Christ change the way you face loss?

### Hosea 13:15–16 — The East Wind and the Fall of Samaria

"Though he be fruitful among his brethren, an east wind shall come, the wind of the Lord shall come up from the wilderness, and his spring shall become dry, and his fountain shall be dried up." The name Ephraim was associated with fruitfulness ([Genesis 41:52](https://www.churchofjesuschrist.org/study/scriptures/ot/gen/41?lang=eng&id=p52#p52)). The scorching east wind, here identified with Assyria's invasion, would dry up his springs. "He shall spoil the treasure of all pleasant vessels."

Verse 16 is one of the most painful verses in the prophets: "Samaria shall become desolate; for she hath rebelled against her God: they shall fall by the sword: their infants shall be dashed in pieces, and their women with child shall be ripped up." These were real atrocities of ancient warfare. Second Kings records that an Israelite king, Menahem, ripped up the pregnant women of a town that resisted him ([2 Kings 15:16](https://www.churchofjesuschrist.org/study/scriptures/ot/2-kgs/15?lang=eng&id=p16#p16)). Israel would suffer what it had inflicted.

How should a reader take such a verse? First, it is a warning of what war would bring, spoken before it happened, by a prophet who had begged his people to turn back. Second, it describes the horror without celebrating it. Third, it is not the end of the book. Immediately after it comes chapter 14: "O Israel, return unto the Lord thy God." It is right to grieve while reading verse 16. The prophet's purpose was to move his hearers to turn before it came to pass.

**Question:** How can reading about the suffering of war deepen your compassion for people who suffer violence today?

## Hosea 14: I Will Love Them Freely

The book ends with an invitation, a prayer the Lord provides for his people, and his answer: "I will heal their backsliding, I will love them freely." The last chapter gives this week's lesson its title.

### Hosea 14:1–3 — "Take with You Words"

"O Israel, return unto the Lord thy God; for thou hast fallen by thine iniquity." After all the warnings, the final word is "return." Verse 2: "Take with you words, and turn to the Lord: say unto him, Take away all iniquity, and receive us graciously: so will we render the calves of our lips." Israel had offered calves on its altars. Now it is to offer words: confession and praise. Breitenstein's commentary notes that repentance is offered "instead of sacrificial calves." The phrase "calves of our lips" is echoed in Hebrews: "let us offer the sacrifice of praise to God continually, that is, the fruit of our lips" ([Hebrews 13:15](https://www.churchofjesuschrist.org/study/scriptures/nt/heb/13?lang=eng&id=p15#p15)).

The Lord does more than invite. He writes the prayer. Israel does not have to find the words on its own.

Verse 3 contains three renunciations and a confession of faith: "Asshur shall not save us; we will not ride upon horses: neither will we say any more to the work of our hands, Ye are our gods: for in thee the fatherless findeth mercy." The footnote explains Asshur as "Assyria." Israel gives up foreign alliances, military power, and idols, the three false saviors of the book. It confesses instead that in God "the fatherless findeth mercy." People who have no other protector find compassion in him.

**Question:** The Lord gave Israel the words to pray. If you were to "take with you words" to the Lord today, what would you say?

### Hosea 14:4–7 — "I Will Be as the Dew unto Israel"

"I will heal their backsliding, I will love them freely: for mine anger is turned away from him." This is the Lord's answer to the prayer of verses 2–3. "Backsliding" is turning away, the habit Israel was "bent" toward (11:7). The Lord treats it as a sickness he will heal, not only a crime he will punish. "I will love them freely": generously, without price, not because Israel earned it. The Bible Dictionary cites 14:4 as an example that Israel's restoration is "due to His love." John wrote, "We love him, because he first loved us" ([1 John 4:19](https://www.churchofjesuschrist.org/study/scriptures/nt/1-jn/4?lang=eng&id=p19#p19)).

Verse 5: "I will be as the dew unto Israel." Israel's goodness had been like the dew that vanishes (6:4; 13:3). Now the Lord himself will be the dew, the quiet, daily moisture that sustains life in a dry land. "He shall grow as the lily, and cast forth his roots as Lebanon." Lilies are beautiful but fragile; the cedars of Lebanon are deep-rooted and strong. Israel will have both beauty and stability.

Verses 6–7 continue the images: branches spreading, beauty "as the olive tree," fragrance "as Lebanon," shade under which others "shall return; they shall revive as the corn, and grow as the vine." A restored Israel becomes a blessing to others, a tree others can rest under.

*Christ connection (devotional reading):* The official lesson invites readers to notice the plant images in verses 5–8 and what they teach about the blessings of repentance, and suggests singing "Come unto Jesus" ([official lesson](https://www.churchofjesuschrist.org/study/manual/come-follow-me-for-home-and-church-old-testament-2026/47?lang=eng)). Through Christ, repentance is not only an escape from punishment but a return to life and growth.

**Question:** "I will love them freely." Is there any part of you that still feels you must earn God's love? How does this verse speak to that feeling?

### Hosea 14:8 — "From Me Is Thy Fruit Found"

"Ephraim shall say, What have I to do any more with idols? I have heard him, and observed him: I am like a green fir tree. From me is thy fruit found." The verse is a dialogue. Ephraim renounces idols. The Lord answers that he has heard and watches over Ephraim. Then the Lord describes himself as an evergreen tree, "a green fir tree," always alive, never withering. Last comes the decisive line: "From me is thy fruit found."

It is a striking image. Israel had looked for fertility from Baal's sacred trees under which it worshipped (4:13). Now the Lord says that every good thing it ever produced came from him. This is the answer to 2:8, "she did not know that I gave her corn, and wine, and oil."

*Christ connection (New Testament reading):* "As the branch cannot bear fruit of itself, except it abide in the vine; no more can ye, except ye abide in me" ([John 15:4](https://www.churchofjesuschrist.org/study/scriptures/nt/john/15?lang=eng&id=p4#p4)).

**Question:** "From me is thy fruit found." What good things in your life do you most need to remember came from the Lord?

### Hosea 14:9 — "The Ways of the Lord Are Right"

"Who is wise, and he shall understand these things? prudent, and he shall know them? for the ways of the Lord are right, and the just shall walk in them: but the transgressors shall fall therein." The book closes with a word to readers in every generation, in the style of the wisdom literature. The same road, "the ways of the Lord," leads to two outcomes. The just walk in it; transgressors stumble on it. The difference is not the road but the walker.

The verse invites the reader to reflect on the whole book. Hosea is not easy to understand. Its images are startling, its moods shift quickly, and some of its verses are painful. The closing verse says that wisdom is needed to understand it and that the Lord's ways are right even when they are hard to follow.

**Question:** Having read Hosea, what do you understand now about "the ways of the Lord" that you did not understand before?

## Joel 1: What the Locusts Left

Joel's book opens with a national catastrophe. Waves of locusts and a severe drought have stripped the land. Joel calls every group in Judah to mourn, from drunkards to farmers to priests, and summons the nation to fast and cry to the Lord. Then he names what the disaster points to: "the day of the Lord is at hand."

### Joel 1:1–3 — Tell Your Children

"The word of the Lord that came to Joel the son of Pethuel." That is all we know about Joel personally. The book names no king and gives no date (see the introduction).

"Hear this, ye old men, and give ear, all ye inhabitants of the land. Hath this been in your days, or even in the days of your fathers?" The footnote gives "elders" for "old men." The disaster was without precedent even in the memory of the oldest people. Verse 3 says, "Tell ye your children of it, and let your children tell their children, and their children another generation." Four generations are named. The locust plague was to be remembered as Israel remembered the Exodus ([Exodus 10:2](https://www.churchofjesuschrist.org/study/scriptures/ot/ex/10?lang=eng&id=p2#p2)), as a lesson passed down.

Kent P. Jackson, as cited by Scripture Central, has argued that Joel deliberately omits references to his own time so that his message speaks to the latter days ([Scripture Central, "Why Did Moroni Quote from the Book of Joel?"](https://scripturecentral.org/knowhy/why-did-moroni-quote-from-the-book-of-joel)). Whether or not one reads every verse that way, the lack of a date does let the book speak to any generation that faces disaster.

**Question:** What hard experiences in your family's history are worth telling to your children and grandchildren because of what God taught through them?

### Joel 1:4–7 — Four Waves of Locusts

"That which the palmerworm hath left hath the locust eaten; and that which the locust hath left hath the cankerworm eaten; and that which the cankerworm hath left hath the caterpiller eaten." The four names may refer to different kinds of locusts or to stages in the insect's growth. The Church's footnote explains, "The invading or conquering armies are compared to four varieties (or stages of growth) of locusts." Each wave consumed what the previous wave left. Nothing remained.

Locust plagues were one of the curses the law of Moses warned of for covenant breaking ([Deuteronomy 28:38](https://www.churchofjesuschrist.org/study/scriptures/ot/deut/28?lang=eng&id=p38#p38)), and Solomon prayed at the temple's dedication that the Lord would hear Israel if "locust" or "caterpiller" came ([1 Kings 8:37–38](https://www.churchofjesuschrist.org/study/scriptures/ot/1-kgs/8?lang=eng&id=p37-p38#p37)). The plague of locusts in Egypt was so severe that "there remained not any green thing" ([Exodus 10:15](https://www.churchofjesuschrist.org/study/scriptures/ot/ex/10?lang=eng&id=p15#p15)).

Verse 5 calls the drunkards to wake and weep, because "the new wine ... is cut off from your mouth." Even those who cared only for pleasure would feel the loss. Verse 6 describes the locusts as "a nation ... strong, and without number, whose teeth are the teeth of a lion." Verse 7: "He hath laid my vine waste, and barked my fig tree: he hath made it clean bare ... the branches thereof are made white." Locusts strip even the bark from trees. The vine and fig tree, symbols of peace and prosperity ([Micah 4:4](https://www.churchofjesuschrist.org/study/scriptures/ot/micah/4?lang=eng&id=p4#p4)), are left white and bare.

Notice the word "my": "my land," "my vine," "my fig tree." The land belonged to the Lord.

**Question:** Joel describes disaster coming in waves, each taking what the last one left. How can faith help when troubles seem to pile one on top of another?

### Joel 1:8–12 — The Land Mourns

"Lament like a virgin girded with sackcloth for the husband of her youth." The image is of a young woman whose bridegroom has died before the marriage could begin. The grief is sharp because so much hope was lost.

Verse 9 gives the most serious loss: "The meat offering and the drink offering is cut off from the house of the Lord; the priests, the Lord's ministers, mourn." ("Meat" in the KJV means food in general; the "meat offering" was an offering of flour and oil, as [Leviticus 2:1](https://www.churchofjesuschrist.org/study/scriptures/ot/lev/2?lang=eng&id=p1#p1) describes.) Without grain and wine, the daily offerings at the temple could not continue. The disaster threatened Judah's worship itself.

Verses 10–12 describe the devastation in agricultural terms: "The field is wasted, the land mourneth; for the corn is wasted: the new wine is dried up, the oil languisheth." The footnote gives "grain" for "corn." Grain, wine, and oil were the three staples Hosea had said Israel credited to Baal ([Hosea 2:8](https://www.churchofjesuschrist.org/study/scriptures/ot/hosea/2?lang=eng&id=p8#p8)). Now they are gone. Farmers and vinedressers are told to "howl"; "the vine is dried up, and the fig tree languisheth; the pomegranate tree, the palm tree also, and the apple tree." The last line of verse 12 sums it up: "joy is withered away from the sons of men." Joy withers with the crops.

**Question:** Joel says joy withered along with the harvest. What helps you keep joy when outward blessings are taken away?

### Joel 1:13–14 — Sanctify a Fast, Call a Solemn Assembly

"Gird yourselves, and lament, ye priests: howl, ye ministers of the altar: come, lie all night in sackcloth, ye ministers of my God." Joel calls the priests to lead the people in repentance. Sackcloth was rough cloth worn in mourning.

Verse 14 gives the instructions: "Sanctify ye a fast, call a solemn assembly, gather the elders and all the inhabitants of the land into the house of the Lord your God, and cry unto the Lord." The footnotes point to the Topical Guide entries on fasting and solemn assemblies. A solemn assembly was a sacred gathering for worship and renewal. Scripture records a similar gathering in King Jehoshaphat's day, when the people of Judah fasted and gathered at the temple "with their little ones, their wives, and their children" ([2 Chronicles 20:3–4](https://www.churchofjesuschrist.org/study/scriptures/ot/2-chr/20?lang=eng&id=p3-p4#p3), [13](https://www.churchofjesuschrist.org/study/scriptures/ot/2-chr/20?lang=eng&id=p13#p13)).

*Restoration reading:* The Lord commanded the early Saints to "call a solemn assembly" ([Doctrine and Covenants 88:70](https://www.churchofjesuschrist.org/study/scriptures/dc-testament/dc/88?lang=eng&id=p70#p70)). Latter-day Saints still hold solemn assemblies, for example when they sustain a new President of the Church. *Scripture Helps* summarizes Joel's counsel as gathering to the house of the Lord.

**Question:** Joel's answer to disaster was to fast, gather, and pray together at the house of the Lord. How does gathering with others to worship strengthen you in hard times?

### Joel 1:15–20 — "Alas for the Day!"

"Alas for the day! for the day of the Lord is at hand, and as a destruction from the Almighty shall it come." This is the first mention of "the day of the Lord" in Joel. The footnote points to the Topical Guide entry "Day of the Lord." *Scripture Helps* explains it as the time when the Lord brings judgment to the wicked and deliverance to the righteous. Joel sees the locusts as a warning of that day.

Verses 16–18 return to the scene: "Is not the meat cut off before our eyes, yea, joy and gladness from the house of our God? The seed is rotten under their clods, the garners are laid desolate, the barns are broken down." The footnotes give "food" for "meat" and "storehouses" for "garners." The future harvest is lost too: seed planted in dry ground has rotted. "How do the beasts groan! the herds of cattle are perplexed, because they have no pasture."

Verses 19–20 turn the lament into prayer: "O Lord, to thee will I cry: for the fire hath devoured the pastures of the wilderness ... The beasts of the field cry also unto thee: for the rivers of waters are dried up." Drought had brought fire. Joel prays, and he hears the animals crying to God too. In Joel's world, all creation looks to the Lord. Jeremiah described a similar drought in Judah, when the nobles' servants "came to the pits, and found no water" ([Jeremiah 14:3](https://www.churchofjesuschrist.org/study/scriptures/ot/jer/14?lang=eng&id=p3#p3)).

John Hilton III's slides walk through this chapter's images one after another, the four locusts, the withered vines, the groaning cattle, the dried rivers, to show how complete the disaster was before Joel calls the people to return ([Hilton, Class 46 slides](https://www.dropbox.com/scl/fi/cbft6sna5osmeec29m0w2/46-Hosea-Joel.pdf?rlkey=7tgk1qjl09srdxa8r6ell7nbg&st=22efq01e&dl=1)).

**Question:** Joel's lament becomes a prayer: "O Lord, to thee will I cry." How can sorrow become a way of turning toward God rather than away from him?

## Joel 2: Rend Your Heart, and the Spirit Poured Out

Chapter 2 is the center of Joel's book. It begins with an alarm and a terrifying army, turns to the Lord's call to "rend your heart, and not your garments," and then promises restoration: rain, full harvests, the years the locusts ate, and the Spirit poured out "upon all flesh." It ends with the verses Peter quoted at Pentecost and Moroni quoted to Joseph Smith.

### Joel 2:1–2 — Blow the Trumpet in Zion

"Blow ye the trumpet in Zion, and sound an alarm in my holy mountain: let all the inhabitants of the land tremble: for the day of the Lord cometh, for it is nigh at hand." The footnote explains "trumpet" as "*shofar*, or ram's horn." The shofar was blown to warn of attack. "My holy mountain" is Zion, the temple mount in Jerusalem.

Verse 2: "A day of darkness and of gloominess, a day of clouds and of thick darkness, as the morning spread upon the mountains: a great people and a strong; there hath not been ever the like." A swarm of locusts can darken the sky. But "a great people and a strong" points beyond insects to an army. The disaster of chapter 1 becomes a picture of the coming day of the Lord.

**Question:** Why does the Lord send warnings before judgment? What "trumpets" has he sounded in your life?

### Joel 2:3–11 — The Lord's Army

These verses describe an unstoppable army. "A fire devoureth before them; and behind them a flame burneth: the land is as the garden of Eden before them, and behind them a desolate wilderness" (verse 3). The army reverses creation, turning Eden into wilderness.

The description fits both locusts and soldiers. "The appearance of them is as the appearance of horses; and as horsemen, so shall they run" (verse 4). They sound like chariots and fire (verse 5). "Before their face the people shall be much pained: all faces shall gather blackness" (verse 6); the footnote explains "blackness" as a Hebrew idiom meaning "gloom." "They shall run like mighty men; they shall climb the wall like men of war ... they shall not break their ranks" (verse 7). "They shall enter in at the windows like a thief" (verse 9). Locusts get into everything, even houses. The cosmos shakes: "the sun and the moon shall be dark, and the stars shall withdraw their shining" (verse 10).

Verse 11 reveals whose army it is: "And the Lord shall utter his voice before his army: for his camp is very great: for he is strong that executeth his word: for the day of the Lord is great and very terrible; and who can abide it?" The invaders are the Lord's instrument. *Scripture Helps* highlights the question at the end of the verse, "who can abide it?" Malachi asks the same question about the day of the Lord's coming ([Malachi 3:2](https://www.churchofjesuschrist.org/study/scriptures/ot/mal/3?lang=eng&id=p2#p2)).

John's Revelation borrows Joel's imagery: locusts "like unto horses prepared unto battle" ([Revelation 9:7](https://www.churchofjesuschrist.org/study/scriptures/nt/rev/9?lang=eng&id=p7#p7)). The Bible Dictionary notes that there are "traces of Joel in Revelation." The Church's chapter heading for Joel 2 reads these verses as prophecy of "war and desolation" that "will precede the Second Coming."

**Question:** Joel asks, "who can abide it?" What does it mean to you to be able to "abide" the day of the Lord?

### Joel 2:12–14 — "Rend Your Heart, and Not Your Garments"

"Therefore also now, saith the Lord, turn ye even to me with all your heart, and with fasting, and with weeping, and with mourning." After the overwhelming army comes "therefore also now." Even now, at this late moment, the Lord invites return. John Hilton III's slides emphasize the Hebrew word for "return" in this verse, *shuv*, and present Joel's whole book as a call to turn back to God.

Verse 13: "And rend your heart, and not your garments, and turn unto the Lord your God: for he is gracious and merciful, slow to anger, and of great kindness, and repenteth him of the evil." *Scripture Helps* explains that tearing one's clothes was a traditional outward sign of mourning. Rending the heart means repenting with a broken heart and contrite spirit. The official lesson asks how rending the heart differs from rending garments ([official lesson](https://www.churchofjesuschrist.org/study/manual/come-follow-me-for-home-and-church-old-testament-2026/47?lang=eng)). Torn clothes can be mended or replaced. A torn heart is a real change.

The description of God in verse 13 quotes the Lord's own self-revelation to Moses: "merciful and gracious, longsuffering, and abundant in goodness and truth" ([Exodus 34:6](https://www.churchofjesuschrist.org/study/scriptures/ot/ex/34?lang=eng&id=p6#p6)). Jonah quoted it too, unhappily, when God spared Nineveh ([Jonah 4:2](https://www.churchofjesuschrist.org/study/scriptures/ot/jonah/4?lang=eng&id=p2#p2)). "Great kindness" is *hesed* again.

The Joseph Smith Translation, given in the Church's footnotes, adds "and repent" to verse 13 and changes its last phrase to "he will turn away the evil from you." It makes clear that the people must repent and that God removes the evil from them. The JST also begins verse 14 with "Therefore repent, and who knoweth but he will return and leave a blessing behind him."

Verse 14 holds out hope: "Who knoweth if he will return and repent, and leave a blessing behind him; even a meat offering and a drink offering unto the Lord your God?" The blessing Joel hopes for is grain and wine to offer at the temple again. The first thing restored would be the ability to worship.

*Restoration reading:* The resurrected Savior asked the Nephites to offer "a broken heart and a contrite spirit" instead of animal sacrifices ([3 Nephi 9:20](https://www.churchofjesuschrist.org/study/scriptures/bofm/3-ne/9?lang=eng&id=p20#p20)). The Psalmist said, "a broken and a contrite heart, O God, thou wilt not despise" ([Psalm 51:17](https://www.churchofjesuschrist.org/study/scriptures/ot/ps/51?lang=eng&id=p17#p17)). Both describe a rent heart.

**Question:** What is the difference between showing sorrow and actually changing? How do you know when your heart, and not only your garments, has been rent?

### Joel 2:15–17 — Gather the People

"Blow the trumpet in Zion, sanctify a fast, call a solemn assembly." The trumpet that sounded alarm in verse 1 now calls the people to worship. Verse 16 says who should come: "Gather the people, sanctify the congregation, assemble the elders, gather the children, and those that suck the breasts: let the bridegroom go forth of his chamber, and the bride out of her closet." The footnote gives "wedding canopy" for "closet." No one is exempt: elders, children, nursing babies, even a couple on their wedding day.

Verse 17 gives the priests their prayer: "Let the priests, the ministers of the Lord, weep between the porch and the altar, and let them say, Spare thy people, O Lord, and give not thine heritage to reproach, that the heathen should rule over them: wherefore should they say among the people, Where is their God?" The priests stood between the temple porch and the altar, the place of intercession. Their prayer appeals to God's reputation. Moses had prayed in a similar way after the golden calf: "Wherefore should the Egyptians speak, and say, For mischief did he bring them out?" ([Exodus 32:12](https://www.churchofjesuschrist.org/study/scriptures/ot/ex/32?lang=eng&id=p12#p12); compare [Psalm 79:10](https://www.churchofjesuschrist.org/study/scriptures/ot/ps/79?lang=eng&id=p10#p10)).

**Question:** Joel calls everyone, from the oldest to the youngest, to gather. What does it teach that repentance and worship are meant to be shared by the whole community?

### Joel 2:18–20 — The Lord Answers

"Then will the Lord be jealous for his land, and pity his people." The footnotes give "zealous" for "jealous" and "have compassion on" for "pity." This verse is the turning point of Joel. Everything before it is warning and lament; everything after it is promise. The Lord's zeal is the passion of covenant love that refuses to let his people go (compare [Zechariah 8:2](https://www.churchofjesuschrist.org/study/scriptures/ot/zech/8?lang=eng&id=p2#p2)).

Verse 19: "Yea, the Lord will answer and say unto his people, Behold, I will send you corn, and wine, and oil, and ye shall be satisfied therewith: and I will no more make you a reproach among the heathen." The three staples that the locusts took will return.

Verse 20: "But I will remove far off from you the northern army, and will drive him into a land barren and desolate, with his face toward the east sea, and his hinder part toward the utmost sea, and his stink shall come up." "Northern army" may point beyond locusts to human invaders: Assyria and Babylon both invaded Israel from the north. The army will be driven into the desert and the seas (the Dead Sea to the east and the Mediterranean to the west), and its dead will rot. Ezekiel also prophesies of a great army from "the north parts" in the last days ([Ezekiel 38:15](https://www.churchofjesuschrist.org/study/scriptures/ot/ezek/38?lang=eng&id=p15#p15)).

**Question:** The Lord's answer came after the people gathered, fasted, and prayed. How have you seen the Lord answer after you sought him with others?

### Joel 2:21–27 — "I Will Restore to You the Years"

Three groups are told not to fear. "Fear not, O land; be glad and rejoice: for the Lord will do great things" (verse 21). The locust army had "done great things" in verse 20; now the Lord does greater. "Be not afraid, ye beasts of the field: for the pastures of the wilderness do spring" (verse 22). The animals that cried in 1:20 are answered. "Be glad then, ye children of Zion, and rejoice in the Lord your God: for he hath given you the former rain moderately, and he will cause to come down for you the rain, the former rain, and the latter rain in the first month" (verse 23). The autumn and spring rains will both come (compare [Deuteronomy 11:14](https://www.churchofjesuschrist.org/study/scriptures/ot/deut/11?lang=eng&id=p14#p14); [James 5:7](https://www.churchofjesuschrist.org/study/scriptures/nt/james/5?lang=eng&id=p7#p7)).

"And the floors shall be full of wheat, and the fats shall overflow with wine and oil" (verse 24). The footnote gives "vats" for "fats." The threshing floors and presses that were empty in chapter 1 will overflow.

Verse 25 is one of the most comforting promises in the Old Testament: "And I will restore to you the years that the locust hath eaten, the cankerworm, and the caterpiller, and the palmerworm, my great army which I sent among you." The Lord does not only stop the loss. He restores what was lost. He even calls the locusts "my great army which I sent," taking responsibility for the discipline and for the restoration.

Verses 26–27: "And ye shall eat in plenty, and be satisfied, and praise the name of the Lord your God, that hath dealt wondrously with you: and my people shall never be ashamed. And ye shall know that I am in the midst of Israel, and that I am the Lord your God, and none else: and my people shall never be ashamed." The phrase "my people shall never be ashamed" is repeated. The greatest restoration is not the harvest but the Lord's presence: "I am in the midst of Israel." The footnote points to the Topical Guide on the presence of God.

*Christ connection (devotional reading):* Through the Atonement of Jesus Christ, years lost to sin, grief, illness, or other people's choices are not beyond the Lord's power to redeem. Joel's promise does not mean that every loss is undone in this life. It does mean that no loss is outside the reach of the Lord, who promises his people they "shall never be ashamed."

**Question:** What "years that the locust hath eaten" would you like the Lord to restore? What would it mean to trust him with them?

### Joel 2:28–29 — "I Will Pour Out My Spirit upon All Flesh"

"And it shall come to pass afterward, that I will pour out my spirit upon all flesh; and your sons and your daughters shall prophesy, your old men shall dream dreams, your young men shall see visions: And also upon the servants and upon the handmaids in those days will I pour out my spirit."

"Afterward" means after the restoration of the land. The blessings of rain and harvest lead to an even greater gift: the Lord's own Spirit, "poured out" like rain. The promise crosses every line that divided ancient society: sons and daughters (gender), old men and young men (age), servants and handmaids (social class). Moses had once wished "that all the Lord's people were prophets, and that the Lord would put his spirit upon them!" ([Numbers 11:29](https://www.churchofjesuschrist.org/study/scriptures/ot/num/11?lang=eng&id=p29#p29)). Joel prophesied that the wish would come true. The footnotes connect these verses with the Topical Guide entries on the gifts of the Holy Ghost, dreams, visions, dispensations, and the Restoration of the gospel.

*Christ connection (New Testament reading):* On the day of Pentecost the Holy Ghost came upon the disciples "as of a rushing mighty wind," with "cloven tongues like as of fire" ([Acts 2:1–4](https://www.churchofjesuschrist.org/study/scriptures/nt/acts/2?lang=eng&id=p1-p4#p1)). When onlookers thought they were drunk, Peter said, "this is that which was spoken by the prophet Joel" ([Acts 2:16](https://www.churchofjesuschrist.org/study/scriptures/nt/acts/2?lang=eng&id=p16#p16)) and quoted Joel 2:28–32, beginning "in the last days" ([Acts 2:17–21](https://www.churchofjesuschrist.org/study/scriptures/nt/acts/2?lang=eng&id=p17-p21#p17)). He then testified that the risen Jesus, "having received of the Father the promise of the Holy Ghost," had poured out what they saw and heard ([Acts 2:32–33](https://www.churchofjesuschrist.org/study/scriptures/nt/acts/2?lang=eng&id=p32-p33#p32)). The Spirit poured out is a gift of the risen Christ.

*Restoration reading:* On the night of September 21, 1823, the angel Moroni quoted this passage to Joseph Smith: "He also quoted the second chapter of Joel, from the twenty-eighth verse to the last. He also said that this was not yet fulfilled, but was soon to be" ([Joseph Smith—History 1:41](https://www.churchofjesuschrist.org/study/scriptures/pgp/js-h/1?lang=eng&id=p41#p41)). *Scripture Helps* explains that Joel's prophecy has multiple fulfillments, at Pentecost and in the latter days, and quotes President Gordon B. Hinckley: "The vision of Joel has been fulfilled." Scripture Central's KnoWhy connects the prophecy with the outpouring of the Spirit in Kirtland in the 1830s and with the restoration of priesthood keys, and cites Kent P. Jackson's view that many of the gifts Joel describes "are indeed had among the faithful today" ([Scripture Central, KnoWhy #651](https://scripturecentral.org/knowhy/why-did-moroni-quote-from-the-book-of-joel)). At the Kirtland Temple dedication Joseph Smith prayed that the Lord's blessing would be fulfilled "as upon those on the day of Pentecost" ([Doctrine and Covenants 109:36–37](https://www.churchofjesuschrist.org/study/scriptures/dc-testament/dc/109?lang=eng&id=p36-p37#p36)).

The official lesson quotes President Russell M. Nelson: "In coming days, it will not be possible to survive spiritually without the guiding, directing, comforting, and constant influence of the Holy Ghost." It also quotes Elder David A. Bednar: "We seem to believe that the Holy Ghost is dramatic and big and sudden, when it's still and small and incremental over time" ([official lesson](https://www.churchofjesuschrist.org/study/manual/come-follow-me-for-home-and-church-old-testament-2026/47?lang=eng)). The lesson points to scriptural examples of people receiving the Spirit, including King Benjamin's people ([Mosiah 4:2–3](https://www.churchofjesuschrist.org/study/scriptures/bofm/mosiah/4?lang=eng&id=p2-p3#p2)) and the Lamanites in Helaman's day ([Helaman 6:36](https://www.churchofjesuschrist.org/study/scriptures/bofm/hel/6?lang=eng&id=p36#p36)). The children's section suggests reading verses 28–29 with children's own names in place of "your sons" and "your daughters."

**Question:** Joel's promise includes "your sons and your daughters," "old men," "young men," "servants," and "handmaids." Where do you fit in that list? How has the Spirit been poured out on you?

### Joel 2:30–32 — "Whosoever Shall Call on the Name of the Lord"

"And I will shew wonders in the heavens and in the earth, blood, and fire, and pillars of smoke. The sun shall be turned into darkness, and the moon into blood, before the great and the terrible day of the Lord come." These signs return to the darkness of 2:10. They precede the Lord's coming. Jesus spoke of the sun darkened and the moon not giving her light before his Second Coming ([Matthew 24:29](https://www.churchofjesuschrist.org/study/scriptures/nt/matt/24?lang=eng&id=p29#p29)), and latter-day revelation repeats Joel's words: "they shall behold blood, and fire, and vapors of smoke. And before the day of the Lord shall come, the sun shall be darkened, and the moon be turned into blood" ([Doctrine and Covenants 45:41–42](https://www.churchofjesuschrist.org/study/scriptures/dc-testament/dc/45?lang=eng&id=p41-p42#p41); compare [Doctrine and Covenants 29:14](https://www.churchofjesuschrist.org/study/scriptures/dc-testament/dc/29?lang=eng&id=p14#p14)). Scripture Central's KnoWhy notes that these last verses Moroni quoted deal with signs preceding the Second Coming.

Verse 32 is the promise in the middle of the signs: "And it shall come to pass, that whosoever shall call on the name of the Lord shall be delivered: for in mount Zion and in Jerusalem shall be deliverance, as the Lord hath said, and in the remnant whom the Lord shall call." The footnote notes that "the Lord" here is "Jehovah." The verse has two callings: the people call on the Lord, and the Lord calls the remnant. Obadiah echoes it: "upon mount Zion shall be deliverance" ([Obadiah 1:17](https://www.churchofjesuschrist.org/study/scriptures/ot/obad/1?lang=eng&id=p17#p17)).

*Christ connection (New Testament reading):* Peter ended his quotation of Joel with "whosoever shall call on the name of the Lord shall be saved" ([Acts 2:21](https://www.churchofjesuschrist.org/study/scriptures/nt/acts/2?lang=eng&id=p21#p21)), and then told the people whose name that was: "Repent, and be baptized every one of you in the name of Jesus Christ" ([Acts 2:38](https://www.churchofjesuschrist.org/study/scriptures/nt/acts/2?lang=eng&id=p38#p38)). Paul quoted the same verse: "For whosoever shall call upon the name of the Lord shall be saved" ([Romans 10:13](https://www.churchofjesuschrist.org/study/scriptures/nt/rom/10?lang=eng&id=p13#p13)). The "Lord" of Joel 2:32, Jehovah, is identified in the New Testament with Jesus Christ.

John Hilton III's slides follow Peter's sermon to the hearers' response: "Men and brethren, what shall we do?" ([Acts 2:37](https://www.churchofjesuschrist.org/study/scriptures/nt/acts/2?lang=eng&id=p37#p37)). They connect it with the same question in other scriptures: the crowds who asked John the Baptist ([Luke 3:10](https://www.churchofjesuschrist.org/study/scriptures/nt/luke/3?lang=eng&id=p10#p10)), King Lamoni's father ([Alma 22:15](https://www.churchofjesuschrist.org/study/scriptures/bofm/alma/22?lang=eng&id=p15#p15)), and the humble Zoramites ([Alma 32:9](https://www.churchofjesuschrist.org/study/scriptures/bofm/alma/32?lang=eng&id=p9#p9)). Joel's promise invites each reader to ask that question too.

**Question:** When you hear the promise that "whosoever shall call on the name of the Lord shall be delivered," what does calling on his name look like in your daily life?

## Joel 3: The Valley of Decision

Chapter 3 widens the view from Judah to all nations. The Lord will gather the nations for judgment in "the valley of Jehoshaphat," hold them accountable for how they treated his people, and then dwell in Zion. The chapter ends with the mountains dripping new wine and a fountain flowing from the house of the Lord.

### Joel 3:1–3 — Judgment in the Valley of Jehoshaphat

"For, behold, in those days, and in that time, when I shall bring again the captivity of Judah and Jerusalem." The footnote explains "bring again the captivity" as "cause the return." The gathering of God's people and the judgment of the nations happen together.

Verse 2: "I will also gather all nations, and will bring them down into the valley of Jehoshaphat, and will plead with them there for my people and for my heritage Israel, whom they have scattered among the nations, and parted my land." Jehoshaphat was also the name of a king of Judah, and the name is usually explained as "the Lord judges," which matches what happens there: "there will I sit to judge" (verse 12). No valley by that name is mentioned elsewhere in the Old Testament, and its location is uncertain. It may be symbolic, a place named for what God will do there. The Lord "pleads," or brings a lawsuit, against the nations on behalf of his people.

Verse 3 lists the crimes: "And they have cast lots for my people; and have given a boy for an harlot, and sold a girl for wine, that they might drink." The nations had treated captive children as cheap goods, trading a boy for a prostitute's fee and a girl for a drink. The Lord sees these children, and he takes their mistreatment as an offense against himself. This verse is a reminder that God is the defender of exploited children.

**Question:** The Lord takes up the cause of children who were sold. How does this verse shape the way you think about vulnerable people in your own community and in the world?

### Joel 3:4–8 — Tyre, Zidon, and Philistia

"Yea, and what have ye to do with me, O Tyre, and Zidon, and all the coasts of Palestine? will ye render me a recompence?" Tyre and Zidon were Phoenician trading cities on the coast north of Israel. "Palestine" here means Philistia, the land of the Philistines on the southern coast. All three were Judah's neighbors. Verse 5 charges them with plunder: "ye have taken my silver and my gold, and have carried into your temples my goodly pleasant things."

Verse 6 charges them with slave trading: "The children also of Judah and the children of Jerusalem have ye sold unto the Grecians, that ye might remove them far from their border." Amos condemned Gaza and Tyre for selling whole populations of captives ([Amos 1:6](https://www.churchofjesuschrist.org/study/scriptures/ot/amos/1?lang=eng&id=p6#p6), [9](https://www.churchofjesuschrist.org/study/scriptures/ot/amos/1?lang=eng&id=p9#p9)). Ezekiel describes Tyre's merchants trading "the persons of men" ([Ezekiel 27:13](https://www.churchofjesuschrist.org/study/scriptures/ot/ezek/27?lang=eng&id=p13#p13)).

Verses 7–8 announce the reversal: "Behold, I will raise them out of the place whither ye have sold them, and will return your recompence upon your own head: And I will sell your sons and your daughters into the hand of the children of Judah, and they shall sell them to the Sabeans, to a people far off." The Sabeans were a distant trading people, probably in southern Arabia. The punishment matches the crime. This is the ancient principle of measure for measure: those who sold others will be sold. It describes judgment in the terms of Joel's world and announces that injustice will not go unanswered. It is not a commandment to enslave anyone, and the gospel of Jesus Christ calls his followers to break cycles of retaliation ([Matthew 5:38–44](https://www.churchofjesuschrist.org/study/scriptures/nt/matt/5?lang=eng&id=p38-p44#p38)). "For the Lord hath spoken it."

**Question:** Joel promises that God will "return your recompence upon your own head." How does trusting God to judge free you from the need to take revenge yourself?

### Joel 3:9–13 — Beat Your Plowshares into Swords

"Proclaim ye this among the Gentiles; Prepare war, wake up the mighty men, let all the men of war draw near." The Lord now summons the nations to battle, with heavy irony. Verse 10: "Beat your plowshares into swords, and your pruninghooks into spears: let the weak say, I am strong." This is the reverse of the famous promise in Isaiah and Micah that the nations "shall beat their swords into plowshares, and their spears into pruninghooks" ([Isaiah 2:4](https://www.churchofjesuschrist.org/study/scriptures/ot/isa/2?lang=eng&id=p4#p4); [Micah 4:3](https://www.churchofjesuschrist.org/study/scriptures/ot/micah/4?lang=eng&id=p3#p3)). Before the peace of the Lord's reign, the nations will make their last war. Even farm tools will be turned into weapons, and the weak will pretend to be strong.

Verse 11: "Assemble yourselves, and come, all ye heathen ... thither cause thy mighty ones to come down, O Lord." Joel interrupts with a prayer: may the Lord's own mighty ones (his heavenly armies) come down. Verse 12: "Let the heathen be wakened, and come up to the valley of Jehoshaphat: for there will I sit to judge all the heathen round about." The Lord sits as judge. The footnote points to the Topical Guide entry "Jesus Christ, Judge."

Verse 13 shifts to the images of harvest: "Put ye in the sickle, for the harvest is ripe: come, get you down; for the press is full, the fats overflow; for their wickedness is great." The footnote gives "vats" for "fats." In 2:24 the vats overflowed with blessing; here they overflow with wickedness ripe for judgment. John's Revelation uses the same pictures: "Thrust in thy sickle, and reap: for the time is come ... for the harvest of the earth is ripe" ([Revelation 14:15](https://www.churchofjesuschrist.org/study/scriptures/nt/rev/14?lang=eng&id=p15#p15); see [Revelation 14:19–20](https://www.churchofjesuschrist.org/study/scriptures/nt/rev/14?lang=eng&id=p19-p20#p19)).

*Restoration reading:* Latter-day revelation uses the harvest image in a hopeful way for missionary work: "the field is white already to harvest; and lo, he that thrusteth in his sickle with his might ... bringeth salvation to his soul" ([Doctrine and Covenants 4:4](https://www.churchofjesuschrist.org/study/scriptures/dc-testament/dc/4?lang=eng&id=p4#p4)). The same image describes judgment for the wicked and gathering for the faithful.

**Question:** The harvest image can mean judgment or gathering. What determines which harvest a person is part of?

### Joel 3:14–17 — "Multitudes in the Valley of Decision"

"Multitudes, multitudes in the valley of decision: for the day of the Lord is near in the valley of decision." The repetition creates a picture of vast crowds. The "decision" in Joel's setting is the Lord's verdict, his judgment on the nations. But the phrase has also become a call to personal choice. John Hilton III's class takes its title, "Your Day of Decision," from this verse and asks whether, on our day of decision, we will turn toward God or away from him ([Hilton, Class 46](https://johnhiltoniii.com/findingchrist/class-46-your-day-of-decision-hosea-1-6-10-14/)). Both meanings are useful: the Lord will decide, and each person decides now how to meet him.

Verse 15: "The sun and the moon shall be darkened, and the stars shall withdraw their shining." The signs of 2:10 and 2:31 return.

Verse 16: "The Lord also shall roar out of Zion, and utter his voice from Jerusalem; and the heavens and the earth shall shake: but the Lord will be the hope of his people, and the strength of the children of Israel." Amos opens his book with nearly the same words ([Amos 1:2](https://www.churchofjesuschrist.org/study/scriptures/ot/amos/1?lang=eng&id=p2#p2)). Hosea's lion roared to gather his children (Hosea 11:10); Joel's roars from Zion. The same voice that shakes heaven and earth is "the hope of his people." The footnotes connect this verse with the Topical Guide entries on the Second Coming and on hope. Latter-day revelation uses the same language: the Lord "shall utter his voice out of Zion, and he shall speak from Jerusalem" ([Doctrine and Covenants 133:21](https://www.churchofjesuschrist.org/study/scriptures/dc-testament/dc/133?lang=eng&id=p21#p21)).

Verse 17: "So shall ye know that I am the Lord your God dwelling in Zion, my holy mountain: then shall Jerusalem be holy, and there shall no strangers pass through her any more." "Strangers" here means invaders who would trample the holy city (compare [Isaiah 52:1](https://www.churchofjesuschrist.org/study/scriptures/ot/isa/52?lang=eng&id=p1#p1)). The footnotes point to the Topical Guide entries on the Millennium and on Jerusalem. The goal of the whole book is that God's people "know" him, the same goal Hosea named ([Hosea 2:20](https://www.churchofjesuschrist.org/study/scriptures/ot/hosea/2?lang=eng&id=p20#p20)).

**Question:** "The Lord will be the hope of his people." In a time of shaking, what does it mean to you that the Lord is your hope?

### Joel 3:18–21 — A Fountain from the House of the Lord

"And it shall come to pass in that day, that the mountains shall drop down new wine, and the hills shall flow with milk, and all the rivers of Judah shall flow with waters, and a fountain shall come forth of the house of the Lord, and shall water the valley of Shittim." The book that began with dried rivers and withered vines ends with the opposite. Amos used similar language for the abundance of the last days ([Amos 9:13](https://www.churchofjesuschrist.org/study/scriptures/ot/amos/9?lang=eng&id=p13#p13)). A land "flowing with milk" recalls the promised land itself.

The fountain from the temple appears in other prophets. Ezekiel saw water flowing from under the threshold of the temple and turning into a river that brought life wherever it went ([Ezekiel 47:1–9](https://www.churchofjesuschrist.org/study/scriptures/ot/ezek/47?lang=eng&id=p1-p9#p1)); Zechariah saw "living waters" going out from Jerusalem ([Zechariah 14:8](https://www.churchofjesuschrist.org/study/scriptures/ot/zech/14?lang=eng&id=p8#p8)); John saw "a pure river of water of life ... proceeding out of the throne of God and of the Lamb" ([Revelation 22:1](https://www.churchofjesuschrist.org/study/scriptures/nt/rev/22?lang=eng&id=p1#p1)). Shittim was where Israel camped before crossing the Jordan, and where it fell into the worship of Baal of Peor ([Numbers 25:1–3](https://www.churchofjesuschrist.org/study/scriptures/ot/num/25?lang=eng&id=p1-p3#p1)). If Joel means that place, the image is fitting: the Lord's water will heal even the valley remembered for Israel's apostasy. The exact location of Joel's "valley of Shittim" is uncertain.

Verse 19: "Egypt shall be a desolation, and Edom shall be a desolate wilderness, for the violence against the children of Judah, because they have shed innocent blood in their land." The reason for judgment is violence and innocent blood. The Lord defends the innocent.

Verses 20–21: "But Judah shall dwell for ever, and Jerusalem from generation to generation. For I will cleanse their blood that I have not cleansed: for the Lord dwelleth in Zion." Verse 21 is difficult. It may mean that the Lord will avenge the innocent blood that has not yet been avenged, or that he will cleanse his people from guilt not yet cleansed. Either way, the book's last words are its foundation: "the Lord dwelleth in Zion."

*Christ connection (devotional reading):* Jesus offered "living water," "a well of water springing up into everlasting life" ([John 4:14](https://www.churchofjesuschrist.org/study/scriptures/nt/john/4?lang=eng&id=p14#p14)). The fountain from the house of the Lord is a picture of what he offers through his temple covenants and his Spirit: life flowing out into dry places.

*Restoration reading:* Latter-day Saints look for a latter-day Zion and a renewed Jerusalem, where the Lord will dwell with his people ([Ether 13:3](https://www.churchofjesuschrist.org/study/scriptures/bofm/ether/13?lang=eng&id=p3#p3); [3 Nephi 20:22](https://www.churchofjesuschrist.org/study/scriptures/bofm/3-ne/20?lang=eng&id=p22#p22); [Doctrine and Covenants 133:12–13](https://www.churchofjesuschrist.org/study/scriptures/dc-testament/dc/133?lang=eng&id=p12-p13#p12)).

**Question:** Joel ends with water flowing from the house of the Lord into a dry valley. Where in your life do you need that water to flow?

## Weekly Study and Discussion Plan

**Monday, November 16 — Hosea 1–3: A marriage as a message.** Read the introduction's notes on the marriage imagery first. Make a chart of the three children's names in chapter 1 and their reversals in 2:1 and 2:22–23. Read 2:14–23 slowly and mark every promise beginning with "I will." Ponder: What does it mean that the Lord says, "I will betroth thee unto me for ever"?

**Tuesday, November 17 — Hosea 4–6: Mercy and not sacrifice.** Underline "knowledge" in 4:1, 4:6, and 6:6. Read 6:6 beside [Matthew 9:10–13](https://www.churchofjesuschrist.org/study/scriptures/nt/matt/9?lang=eng&id=p10-p13#p10) and [Matthew 12:1–8](https://www.churchofjesuschrist.org/study/scriptures/nt/matt/12?lang=eng&id=p1-p8#p1). Ask: Where am I offering "sacrifice" when the Lord wants "mercy"? Choose one act of mercy for this week.

**Wednesday, November 18 — Hosea 10–11: It is time to seek the Lord.** Memorize 10:12. Read 11:1–4 and 11:8–9 aloud. Then read [Luke 15:11–24](https://www.churchofjesuschrist.org/study/scriptures/nt/luke/15?lang=eng&id=p11-p24#p11). Ponder: How does "How shall I give thee up?" describe God's feelings for you?

**Thursday, November 19 — Hosea 12–14: Return and be healed.** Read Jacob's story in [Genesis 32:24–30](https://www.churchofjesuschrist.org/study/scriptures/ot/gen/32?lang=eng&id=p24-p30#p24) with Hosea 12:3–6. Read 13:14 with [1 Corinthians 15:54–57](https://www.churchofjesuschrist.org/study/scriptures/nt/1-cor/15?lang=eng&id=p54-p57#p54). Then pray using the words of Hosea 14:2–3 in your own language. Finish with 14:4: "I will love them freely."

**Friday, November 20 — Joel 1: What the locusts left.** List every loss in chapter 1. Then read 1:14 and 1:19. Consider joining or planning a fast this coming fast Sunday with a specific purpose. Ponder: How can sorrow become a prayer?

**Saturday, November 21 — Joel 2: Rend your heart; the Spirit poured out.** Read 2:12–13 and talk about the difference between rending hearts and garments. Read 2:25 and write down "years" you hope the Lord will restore. Read 2:28–32 beside [Acts 2:1–21](https://www.churchofjesuschrist.org/study/scriptures/nt/acts/2?lang=eng&id=p1-p21#p1) and [Joseph Smith—History 1:41](https://www.churchofjesuschrist.org/study/scriptures/pgp/js-h/1?lang=eng&id=p41#p41). Ask: Where have I seen Joel's prophecy fulfilled?

**Sunday, November 22 — Joel 3: The valley of decision.** Read 3:14–18. Talk about two kinds of decision: the Lord's judgment and our own choices. Read [Ezekiel 47:1–12](https://www.churchofjesuschrist.org/study/scriptures/ot/ezek/47?lang=eng&id=p1-p12#p1) beside 3:18. Ponder: What decision will you make this week to turn toward the Lord?

**For a 45-minute class or family discussion:**

1. *Opening (5 minutes):* Read Hosea 14:4 and Joel 2:13. Ask: What do these verses teach about who God is?
2. *Marriage and covenant (10 minutes):* Read Hosea 2:14–20. Discuss how covenants with God are like a marriage. Gently acknowledge that some of the chapter's imagery is hard, and emphasize that it describes national judgment, not a pattern for treating anyone.
3. *Mercy, not sacrifice (8 minutes):* Read Hosea 6:4–6 and Matthew 9:10–13. Discuss what outward observance without inward devotion looks like today.
4. *How shall I give thee up? (7 minutes):* Read Hosea 11:1–4, 8–9. Invite people to notice the parent images and to share, if they wish, what these verses teach about God's love.
5. *Rend your heart (7 minutes):* Read Joel 2:12–14 and 2:25. Talk about real repentance and the Lord's power to restore.
6. *The Spirit poured out (8 minutes):* Read Joel 2:28–29, Acts 2:16–17, and Joseph Smith—History 1:41. Close by testifying that the Lord pours out his Spirit on all who seek him, and that whoever calls on the name of the Lord Jesus Christ shall be delivered.

**With children:** Show a picture of a bride and groom or a temple, and explain that when we make covenants with Heavenly Father and Jesus Christ, it is a promise of love, like a marriage (Hosea 2:19–20). Give each child a cup and pour water into it slowly as you read Joel 2:28–29, replacing "your sons" and "your daughters" with the children's names, to show how the Holy Ghost can be "poured out" on them. Plant a seed in a cup and read Hosea 10:12: "it is time to seek the Lord." Talk about small ways to seek him each day, and check on the seed together through the week. End by reading Hosea 13:4, "there is no saviour beside me," and testifying of Jesus Christ as the Savior.
