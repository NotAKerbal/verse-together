# I Will Turn Their Mourning into Joy

**Come, Follow Me study week:** October 26–November 1, 2026
**Readings:** Jeremiah 31–33; 36–39; Lamentations 1; 3

This is a personal Latter-day Saint study guide. It is not an official publication of The Church of Jesus Christ of Latter-day Saints. It works through every verse in this week's reading: 40 verses of Jeremiah 31, 44 of Jeremiah 32, 26 of Jeremiah 33, 32 of Jeremiah 36, 21 of Jeremiah 37, 28 of Jeremiah 38, 18 of Jeremiah 39, 22 of Lamentations 1, and 66 of Lamentations 3.

Bible quotations come from the King James Version. Most of the discussion is my own close reading of those chapters. Where a modern resource has shaped a point, the guide names it briefly in the text, and all of these resources are listed at the end.

The guide also tries to keep four kinds of statements separate:

- **Ancient context:** what the text likely meant in Jeremiah's world.
- **Official Church teaching:** what Church publications teach.
- **Scholars' proposals:** individual interpretations, which remain open to discussion.
- **Application:** devotional uses for our own lives.

These chapters hold some of the most hopeful promises in the Old Testament. They also hold some of its darkest scenes. The week's title comes from [Jeremiah 31:13](https://www.churchofjesuschrist.org/study/scriptures/ot/jer/31?lang=eng&id=p13#p13). In these chapters, joy does not erase mourning. The promise is that the Lord will turn mourning into joy, and that turning happens with grief still in view.

## Historical and Literary Context

**A book arranged by collections.** Jeremiah is not a diary told in order. It gathers oracles, poems, prayers, and stories into collections, so the reader moves back and forth in time.

- **Chapters 30–31** are poetry of restoration and are often called a book of consolation.
- **Chapters 32–33** take place during Babylon's siege of Jerusalem. Chapter 32 is dated to the tenth year of King Zedekiah. Chapter 33 continues while Jeremiah is still confined and gives no new date.
- **Chapter 36** jumps back to the fourth and fifth years of King Jehoiakim, about 605/604 BC.
- **Chapters 37–39** return to the final siege and the fall of the city.

**Approximate dates.** Dates for this period are approximate, and scholars often differ by a year.

- **Jeremiah's call** came in Josiah's thirteenth year, about 627/626 BC ([Jeremiah 1:2](https://www.churchofjesuschrist.org/study/scriptures/ot/jer/1?lang=eng&id=p2#p2)).
- **His ministry** continued past Jerusalem's fall and ended in Egypt ([Jeremiah 43–44](https://www.churchofjesuschrist.org/study/scriptures/ot/jer/43?lang=eng)). The Bible does not record when or how he died.
- **Josiah** died about 609 BC.
- **Jehoahaz** ruled briefly after him.
- **Jehoiakim** reigned about 609–598.
- **Jehoiachin** (also called Coniah or Jeconiah) reigned about three months before being taken to Babylon in 597.
- **Zedekiah** reigned from 597 until Jerusalem fell, about 587/586.

**The final siege.** Babylon's siege began in Zedekiah's ninth year, in the tenth month ([Jeremiah 39:1](https://www.churchofjesuschrist.org/study/scriptures/ot/jer/39?lang=eng&id=p1#p1)).

- **A brief reprieve.** An Egyptian army caused a short break in the siege ([Jeremiah 37:5](https://www.churchofjesuschrist.org/study/scriptures/ot/jer/37?lang=eng&id=p5#p5)).
- **Jeremiah 32–33.** Chapter 32 is dated to Zedekiah's tenth year, around 588/587. Chapter 33 follows while Jeremiah is still shut up in the court of the prison ([Jeremiah 33:1](https://www.churchofjesuschrist.org/study/scriptures/ot/jer/33?lang=eng&id=p1#p1)) and supplies no new date.
- **The breach.** The wall was broken in Zedekiah's eleventh year, fourth month.
- **The temple burned** a month later, in the fifth month ([Jeremiah 52:12–13](https://www.churchofjesuschrist.org/study/scriptures/ot/jer/52?lang=eng&id=p12-p13#p12)). These are two separate events and should not be merged.

The Book of Mormon begins in this same world: Lehi preached "in the commencement of the first year of the reign of Zedekiah" ([1 Nephi 1:4](https://www.churchofjesuschrist.org/study/scriptures/bofm/1-ne/1?lang=eng&id=p4#p4)).

**Lamentations.** Lamentations is five poems of grief written after Jerusalem fell.

- **Authorship.** The book itself names no author. Jewish and Christian tradition credit Jeremiah, and the Church's [Guide to the Scriptures](https://www.churchofjesuschrist.org/study/scriptures/gs/lamentations-book-of?lang=eng) presents it as his work, written after the fall around 586 BC. Because the text is anonymous, this guide treats Jeremiah's authorship as traditional and respected but not certain.
- **Acrostics.** Chapter 1 has 22 verses, each beginning with the next letter of the Hebrew alphabet.
- **Chapter 3** expands the pattern to 66 verses, with three verses in a row for each letter.

The alphabet form is itself meaningful. Grief is given order, from first letter to last, without pretending the grief is small.

**Reading judgment and lament responsibly.** Jeremiah and Lamentations interpret the fall of Jerusalem as a national covenant crisis.

- **Not a diagnosis.** These texts are not a tool for deciding why a particular person today is suffering. Jesus refused that kind of reasoning ([John 9:2–3](https://www.churchofjesuschrist.org/study/scriptures/nt/john/9?lang=eng&id=p2-p3#p2); [Luke 13:1–5](https://www.churchofjesuschrist.org/study/scriptures/nt/luke/13?lang=eng&id=p1-p5#p1)).
- **Not a verdict on Jewish people.** Lamentations confesses sin in Zion's own voice. That confession never gives outsiders permission to call Jewish suffering, ancient or modern, deserved.
- **The promises still stand.** The covenant promises to Israel and Judah here are real and lasting. The Book of Mormon rebukes Gentiles who forget the Jews, whom the Lord calls "mine ancient covenant people" ([2 Nephi 29:4–5](https://www.churchofjesuschrist.org/study/scriptures/bofm/2-ne/29?lang=eng&id=p4-p5#p4)).
- **Christ connections are labeled.** Christian readings, and Latter-day Saint readings in particular, find Christ in this material. This guide marks when a connection is explicit in later scripture, when it is official interpretation, and when it is typology or devotional application.

## Significant Insights

**1. The prophet who uprooted was also called to plant.** Jeremiah's call had six verbs: "to root out, and to pull down, and to destroy, and to throw down, to build, and to plant" ([Jeremiah 1:10](https://www.churchofjesuschrist.org/study/scriptures/ot/jer/1?lang=eng&id=p10#p10)). The [official 2026 lesson](https://www.churchofjesuschrist.org/study/manual/come-follow-me-for-home-and-church-old-testament-2026/44?lang=eng) uses that verse to ask how a life left in ruins by sin or hardship can be rebuilt.

Jeremiah 31:28 answers with the same vocabulary. God, who watched over Judah to pluck up and break down, will watch over them "to build, and to plant." Jeremiah 32 then acts it out: a real field is bought in a land about to be lost.

**2. Hope can be signed, sealed, witnessed, and buried in a jar.** Jeremiah 32 does not show hope as a mood. It shows a legal act: silver weighed, a deed signed and sealed, witnesses gathered, and copies stored for a long future. Faith in God's promises can take concrete, careful, public form even when the outward evidence points the other way.

**3. Covenant loyalty runs through the week.** Avram R. Shannon's short [Scripture Central entry on *ḥesed*](https://scripturecentral.org/archive/books/book-chapter/hesed) explains that this Hebrew word is hard to translate. At its core it means covenant loyalty, the faithful conduct proper to a covenant relationship. The KJV often renders it "mercy" or "lovingkindness" because that is how the Lord treats His covenant people. Shannon also notes that the Lord wants the same loyalty from His people, not just general niceness.

His list of related passages includes four verses in this week's reading:

- [Jeremiah 31:3](https://www.churchofjesuschrist.org/study/scriptures/ot/jer/31?lang=eng&id=p3#p3)
- [Jeremiah 32:18](https://www.churchofjesuschrist.org/study/scriptures/ot/jer/32?lang=eng&id=p18#p18)
- [Jeremiah 33:11](https://www.churchofjesuschrist.org/study/scriptures/ot/jer/33?lang=eng&id=p11#p11)
- [Lamentations 3:22](https://www.churchofjesuschrist.org/study/scriptures/ot/lam/3?lang=eng&id=p22#p22)

Read side by side, those verses show God's steady covenant love running underneath both judgment and restoration.

**4. God's word outlasts the fire.** John Hilton III's [class slides](https://johnhiltoniii.com/findingchrist/class-43-when-gods-word-is-hard-to-accept-jeremiah-31-33-36-38-lamentations-1-3/) organize this material around a question: what do people do when God's word is hard to accept? His slides answer partly through Jeremiah 36. A king cuts and burns the scroll, and the Lord simply has it written again, with more added.

Jehoiakim rejects the word with a knife. Zedekiah keeps asking for the word but will not obey it. Both are warnings, and both are mirrors.

**5. Courage often comes from the margins.** The man who saves Jeremiah from the cistern is not a priest or a prince. He is Ebed-melech, an Ethiopian court official whose name means "servant of the king." He uses the access he has, says plainly that something is wrong, and handles the rescue with practical gentleness. The Lord later tells him that He knows he was afraid ([Jeremiah 39:17](https://www.churchofjesuschrist.org/study/scriptures/ot/jer/39?lang=eng&id=p17#p17)).

**6. Lament is faithful speech.** [Scripture Helps](https://www.churchofjesuschrist.org/study/manual/scripture-helps-old-testament/42-jeremiah-31-33-36-38-lamentations-1-3?lang=eng) points out that Lamentations, unlike many Old Testament books, records no response from the Lord. It holds the people's grief and longing as they are.

That is a gift. Scripture gives sufferers words for what they feel and does not force a quick resolution. Even the famous hope of Lamentations 3:21–24 rises from inside remembered pain, and the chapter ends with a cry for justice rather than calm.

**7. The promises to Israel and Judah endure.** Jeremiah 31:35–37 and 33:19–26 tie Israel's continued existence to sunrise, moonlight, and the tides. Christians, including Latter-day Saints, read the new covenant through Jesus Christ. Reading it well means rejoicing in that fulfillment without claiming that God has cast off the people He named in these very verses.

## Jeremiah 31: Mourning Turned to Joy for All Israel

Jeremiah 31 continues the poetry of chapter 30. Its hope reaches both kingdoms.

- **The northern kingdom** appears as Ephraim, Samaria, Jacob, and Rachel's children. It had been scattered by Assyria more than a century earlier.
- **The southern kingdom** appears as Judah, Zion, and Jerusalem. It was facing exile to Babylon.

The Lord speaks most of the chapter, but many voices are quoted along the way: watchmen, weeping Rachel, repentant Ephraim, people blessing Judah, and the prophet waking from sleep. Watch for each voice as you read.

### Jeremiah 31:1–3 — Everlasting Love for All the Families of Israel

"At the same time" points back to [Jeremiah 30:24](https://www.churchofjesuschrist.org/study/scriptures/ot/jer/30?lang=eng&id=p24#p24), "in the latter days." The opening promise covers "all the families of Israel," so no tribe is written off.

Verse 2 remembers the Exodus. A people who survived the sword "found grace in the wilderness," when God went "to cause him to rest." Exile is pictured as a second wilderness, and God goes ahead to give rest, as He promised Moses: "My presence shall go with thee, and I will give thee rest" ([Exodus 33:14](https://www.churchofjesuschrist.org/study/scriptures/ot/ex/33?lang=eng&id=p14#p14)).

Verse 3 changes voice. Israel (or the prophet speaking for Israel) says, "The LORD hath appeared of old unto me," and then quotes God: "I have loved thee with an everlasting love: therefore with lovingkindness have I drawn thee." "Lovingkindness" is *ḥesed*, covenant loyalty. God's love does not just wait; it draws, like Hosea's "cords of a man, with bands of love" ([Hosea 11:4](https://www.churchofjesuschrist.org/study/scriptures/ot/hosea/11?lang=eng&id=p4#p4)).

As devotional application, Christians may think of Jesus promising to "draw all men unto me" ([John 12:32](https://www.churchofjesuschrist.org/study/scriptures/nt/john/12?lang=eng&id=p32#p32)).

*Discuss:* How is being "drawn" by love different from being pushed by fear? When have you felt drawn back to God?

### Jeremiah 31:4–6 — Rebuilt, Replanted, and Going Up to Zion

The Lord promises to rebuild the "virgin of Israel," a personification of the people as a young woman with a future. She will again take up tabrets (hand drums) and dance, as Miriam did after the sea crossing ([Exodus 15:20](https://www.churchofjesuschrist.org/study/scriptures/ot/ex/15?lang=eng&id=p20#p20)).

Verse 5 sets the scene on "the mountains of Samaria," the heart of the former northern kingdom. There, people who plant vines will "eat them as common things." Ancient context: the law kept the fruit of new trees off-limits for several years before ordinary use ([Leviticus 19:23–25](https://www.churchofjesuschrist.org/study/scriptures/ot/lev/19?lang=eng&id=p23-p25#p23)). The promise may mean the planters will live long enough and peacefully enough to enjoy their harvest.

Verse 6 is remarkable. Watchmen on Mount Ephraim usually shouted warnings. Now they call: "Arise ye, and let us go up to Zion unto the LORD our God." The north had long worshipped apart from Jerusalem ([1 Kings 12:26–29](https://www.churchofjesuschrist.org/study/scriptures/ot/1-kgs/12?lang=eng&id=p26-p29#p26)). The poem pictures a family reunion in worship, without erasing the history of division.

*Discuss:* What old divisions in families or congregations could become shared paths "up to Zion"?

### Jeremiah 31:7–9 — A Great Company, Including the Blind and the Lame

The Lord commands celebration "for Jacob" among the leading nations. The song's words are a prayer: "O LORD, save thy people, the remnant of Israel." Joy still includes asking. The cry "save" belongs to the same language as "Save now, I beseech thee, O LORD" ([Psalm 118:25](https://www.churchofjesuschrist.org/study/scriptures/ot/ps/118?lang=eng&id=p25#p25)), which the crowds echoed with "Hosanna" as Jesus entered Jerusalem ([Matthew 21:9](https://www.churchofjesuschrist.org/study/scriptures/nt/matt/21?lang=eng&id=p9#p9)).

Verse 8 names who comes home. Along with exiles from "the north country" and "the coasts of the earth" come "the blind and the lame, the woman with child and her that travaileth with child together." The gathering moves at the pace of its most vulnerable members, a God who "shall gently lead those that are with young" ([Isaiah 40:11](https://www.churchofjesuschrist.org/study/scriptures/ot/isa/40?lang=eng&id=p11#p11)).

Verse 9 says they come "with weeping" and supplication, but on a level road by rivers where they "shall not stumble." God says, "I am a father to Israel, and Ephraim is my firstborn." This recalls Jacob's crossed hands blessing Ephraim ([Genesis 48:14–20](https://www.churchofjesuschrist.org/study/scriptures/ot/gen/48?lang=eng&id=p14-p20#p14)) and Israel as God's firstborn ([Exodus 4:22](https://www.churchofjesuschrist.org/study/scriptures/ot/ex/4?lang=eng&id=p22#p22)).

*Discuss:* Jesus told servants to "bring in hither the poor, and the maimed, and the halt, and the blind" ([Luke 14:21](https://www.churchofjesuschrist.org/study/scriptures/nt/luke/14?lang=eng&id=p21#p21)). Who might be left behind if our gatherings move only at the speed of the strong?

### Jeremiah 31:10–11 — The Scatterer Becomes the Shepherd

The audience widens to "ye nations" and "the isles afar off." They are to announce: "He that scattered Israel will gather him, and keep him, as a shepherd doth his flock." The same God who allowed the scattering takes responsibility for the gathering, and gathering includes keeping.

Verse 11 uses two rescue words: the Lord "hath redeemed Jacob, and ransomed him." The second describes a near relative stepping in to rescue family. Its noun form returns in Jeremiah 32:7 as "the right of redemption," a thread worth following into next chapter.

The enemy is "stronger than he"; Israel could not free itself. Latter-day Saints connect this with the Book of Mormon promise that scattered Israel would be "gathered together again" ([1 Nephi 10:14](https://www.churchofjesuschrist.org/study/scriptures/bofm/1-ne/10?lang=eng&id=p14#p14)). As typology, Christians hear the Good Shepherd ([John 10:11](https://www.churchofjesuschrist.org/study/scriptures/nt/john/10?lang=eng&id=p11#p11)).

*Discuss:* What does it mean to you that God both gathers and keeps?

### Jeremiah 31:12–14 — Harvest, Dance, and the Turning of Mourning

The returnees "sing in the height of Zion" and "flow together," like a river running uphill, toward the Lord's goodness (compare [Isaiah 2:2](https://www.churchofjesuschrist.org/study/scriptures/ot/isa/2?lang=eng&id=p2#p2)). That goodness is practical: wheat, wine, oil, lambs, and calves, the basics of life in the land. "Their soul shall be as a watered garden" (compare [Isaiah 58:11](https://www.churchofjesuschrist.org/study/scriptures/ot/isa/58?lang=eng&id=p11#p11)).

Verse 13 gives the week's title. The young woman dances, "young men and old together," and the Lord promises, "I will turn their mourning into joy, and will comfort them, and make them rejoice from their sorrow." Notice the three verbs: turn, comfort, make rejoice. Notice also the word *from*. Joy comes out of sorrow; it does not pretend the sorrow never happened. Verse 14 fills even the priests "with fatness," because offerings are abundant again.

As a later echo, Jesus told grieving disciples, "your sorrow shall be turned into joy" ([John 16:20](https://www.churchofjesuschrist.org/study/scriptures/nt/john/16?lang=eng&id=p20#p20)).

*Discuss:* Why might God promise to *turn* mourning rather than simply replace it?

### Jeremiah 31:15 — Rachel Weeping in Ramah

The Lord reports a sound: "A voice was heard in Ramah, lamentation, and bitter weeping; Rahel weeping for her children refused to be comforted for her children, because they were not."

Rachel was Jacob's wife and the mother of Joseph and Benjamin. Scripture Helps notes that she stands here for the northern tribes descended from Joseph, and that Ramah may have been near her tomb. Two traditions place her burial, one on the way to Bethlehem ([Genesis 35:19](https://www.churchofjesuschrist.org/study/scriptures/ot/gen/35?lang=eng&id=p19#p19)) and one near Benjamin's border ([1 Samuel 10:2](https://www.churchofjesuschrist.org/study/scriptures/ot/1-sam/10?lang=eng&id=p2#p2)).

Rachel died giving birth, naming her son "Benoni," son of my sorrow ([Genesis 35:18](https://www.churchofjesuschrist.org/study/scriptures/ot/gen/35?lang=eng&id=p18#p18)). Ramah later became the place where Babylon gathered captives; Jeremiah himself was found there in chains ([Jeremiah 40:1](https://www.churchofjesuschrist.org/study/scriptures/ot/jer/40?lang=eng&id=p1#p1)).

Matthew explicitly applies this verse to Herod's massacre of Bethlehem's children ([Matthew 2:17–18](https://www.churchofjesuschrist.org/study/scriptures/nt/matt/2?lang=eng&id=p17-p18#p17)). He uses Rachel's cry as a pattern of grief for lost children that repeats in Israel's history. In Jeremiah, the verse is not a stand-alone lament; the promise of return follows immediately.

The text does not scold Rachel's refusal to be comforted. Her grief is treated as real. Disciples are invited to "mourn with those that mourn" ([Mosiah 18:9](https://www.churchofjesuschrist.org/study/scriptures/bofm/mosiah/18?lang=eng&id=p9#p9)), not to hurry them along.

*Discuss:* Why does scripture preserve a mother's refusal to be comforted?

### Jeremiah 31:16–17 — Hope in Thine End

God answers Rachel: "Refrain thy voice from weeping, and thine eyes from tears: for thy work shall be rewarded." Her "work" may include her mothering, her labor in birth, and even her grief. None of it is wasted.

The original promise is specific: "they shall come again from the land of the enemy." Verse 17 adds, "there is hope in thine end," meaning in her future, "that thy children shall come again to their own border." The context is the return of exiles to the land.

As application, Latter-day Saints who have lost children often find comfort here, connected to the restoration and resurrection hope of Christ ([Alma 40:23](https://www.churchofjesuschrist.org/study/scriptures/bofm/alma/40?lang=eng&id=p23#p23)). That hope is real, but this verse should not be turned into a timetable or a promise that every painful loss reverses soon. It promises that God hears the weeping and that the end of the story belongs to Him.

*Discuss:* How can we offer this promise to grieving people without rushing or minimizing their grief?

### Jeremiah 31:18–19 — Ephraim Hears Himself Repent

The Lord says, "I have surely heard Ephraim bemoaning himself," and quotes Ephraim's prayer. Ephraim compares himself to "a bullock unaccustomed to the yoke," a young animal fighting its training. Then comes the heart of the prayer: "turn thou me, and I shall be turned; for thou art the LORD my God." Lamentations ends with nearly the same plea ([Lamentations 5:21](https://www.churchofjesuschrist.org/study/scriptures/ot/lam/5?lang=eng&id=p21#p21)).

Verse 19 describes what happens next. After he was turned, he repented. After he was instructed, he "smote upon my thigh," a gesture of grief and shock (compare [Ezekiel 21:12](https://www.churchofjesuschrist.org/study/scriptures/ot/ezek/21?lang=eng&id=p12#p12)). He felt ashamed and confounded, carrying "the reproach of my youth." The order matters: God's turning comes first, and repentance follows. Grace starts the process, and honest sorrow belongs inside it.

Alma likewise describes turning his mind toward Christ in his darkest moment ([Alma 36:17–20](https://www.churchofjesuschrist.org/study/scriptures/bofm/alma/36?lang=eng&id=p17-p20#p17)).

*Discuss:* How is the shame Ephraim describes different from the kind of shame that keeps people away from God?

### Jeremiah 31:20 — My Dear Son

God answers with questions: "Is Ephraim my dear son? is he a pleasant child?" Then He confesses, "since I spake against him, I do earnestly remember him still." Even God's words of judgment did not end His remembering. "My bowels are troubled for him" uses the ancient idiom of the inner organs as the seat of deep feeling. The verse closes, "I will surely have mercy upon him," with a word for mercy that carries tender, parent-like compassion.

Hosea gives God's similar anguish: "mine heart is turned within me" ([Hosea 11:8](https://www.churchofjesuschrist.org/study/scriptures/ot/hosea/11?lang=eng&id=p8#p8)). As application, Jesus' parable of the father who "ran, and fell on his neck" ([Luke 15:20](https://www.churchofjesuschrist.org/study/scriptures/nt/luke/15?lang=eng&id=p20#p20)) shows the same yearning.

*Discuss:* What changes when we picture God not only judging our failures but being troubled with love over them?

### Jeremiah 31:21–22 — Waymarks Home and a New Thing

The Lord commands Israel to "set thee up waymarks, make thee high heaps," like piles of stones marking a road. "Set thine heart toward the highway, even the way which thou wentest." The road into exile becomes the road home. "Turn again, O virgin of Israel, turn again to these thy cities."

Verse 22 asks, "How long wilt thou go about, O thou backsliding daughter?" picturing someone wandering and hesitating. Then it gives a reason to come back: "the LORD hath created a new thing in the earth, A woman shall compass a man."

This line is one of the hardest in Jeremiah. Interpreters have proposed several meanings:

- A reversal of roles, where Israel (pictured as a woman) seeks out or embraces her divine husband.
- A proverb for something unheard of.
- A messianic reading, proposed by some later Christian interpreters.

The context does not settle the question, and confident claims are best avoided. What is clear is that the "new thing" is God's work, and it is meant to end Israel's wandering.

*Discuss:* What "waymarks" — memories, people, covenants — help you find the road back?

### Jeremiah 31:23–26 — Judah's Blessing and a Sweet Sleep

The focus moves to Judah. When the Lord brings back the captives, people will again greet the land with a blessing: "The LORD bless thee, O habitation of justice, and mountain of holiness." Jerusalem's identity is restored as a place of justice and holiness.

Verse 24 shows farmers and shepherds living together, town life and pasture life side by side. Verse 25 gives the reason: "For I have satiated the weary soul, and I have replenished every sorrowful soul." As application, compare the Savior's invitation to the weary: "I will give you rest" ([Matthew 11:28](https://www.churchofjesuschrist.org/study/scriptures/nt/matt/11?lang=eng&id=p28#p28)).

Verse 26 surprises: "Upon this I awaked, and beheld; and my sleep was sweet unto me." Many readers take this as the prophet waking from a revelatory dream; others suggest a different speaker or a quoted saying. The debate is real. Either way, the line stands out in a book where the prophet more often cries, "My bowels, my bowels!" ([Jeremiah 4:19](https://www.churchofjesuschrist.org/study/scriptures/ot/jer/4?lang=eng&id=p19#p19)).

*Discuss:* Why might a vision of restoration bring rest to someone who carried so much grief?

### Jeremiah 31:27–28 — Watching to Build and to Plant

"I will sow the house of Israel and the house of Judah with the seed of man, and with the seed of beast." Empty land will fill again with people and animals. Both houses are named.

Verse 28 then gives one of the most important structural echoes in the book. God has "watched over them, to pluck up, and to break down, and to throw down, and to destroy, and to afflict." Now He will "watch over them, to build, and to plant." These are the verbs of Jeremiah's call ([Jeremiah 1:10](https://www.churchofjesuschrist.org/study/scriptures/ot/jer/1?lang=eng&id=p10#p10)). The verb "watch" is also the Hebrew word behind the KJV's "I will hasten my word to perform it" in the almond-tree vision ([Jeremiah 1:11–12](https://www.churchofjesuschrist.org/study/scriptures/ot/jer/1?lang=eng&id=p11-p12#p11)).

The same divine attention that once followed through on warnings will now follow through on restoration.

*Discuss:* Is there something in your life that God has helped tear down so He could plant something better?

### Jeremiah 31:29–30 — No More Sour Grapes

A popular saying blamed the exile generation's troubles on their ancestors: "The fathers have eaten a sour grape, and the children's teeth are set on edge." Judah had heard that Manasseh's sins sealed its fate ([2 Kings 24:3–4](https://www.churchofjesuschrist.org/study/scriptures/ot/2-kgs/24?lang=eng&id=p3-p4#p3)), which could breed both fatalism and resentment.

The Lord says the saying will end: "every one shall die for his own iniquity." Ezekiel addresses the same proverb at length ([Ezekiel 18:2–20](https://www.churchofjesuschrist.org/study/scriptures/ot/ezek/18?lang=eng&id=p2-p20#p2)), building on [Deuteronomy 24:16](https://www.churchofjesuschrist.org/study/scriptures/ot/deut/24?lang=eng&id=p16#p16). Latter-day Saints affirm that people "will be punished for their own sins, and not for Adam's transgression" ([Articles of Faith 1:2](https://www.churchofjesuschrist.org/study/scriptures/pgp/a-of-f/1?lang=eng&id=p2#p2)).

This does not deny that families pass down consequences, wounds, and patterns; Jeremiah 32:18 still speaks that way. The point is that consequences are not the same as guilt. A child may inherit pain but is not condemned for a parent's choices.

*Discuss:* How can this distinction help people living with the effects of family dysfunction or trauma?

### Jeremiah 31:31–32 — A New Covenant with Israel and Judah

"Behold, the days come, saith the LORD, that I will make a new covenant with the house of Israel, and with the house of Judah." Both houses are named, and the original covenant partners are the people of Israel and Judah.

The new covenant differs from the one made "with their fathers in the day that I took them by the hand to bring them out of the land of Egypt." That is a tender picture of the Exodus: God leading a child by the hand. The problem was not God's faithfulness: "which my covenant they brake, although I was an husband unto them." The marriage metaphor shows that covenant is a relationship, not just a contract.

Christians hear Jesus at the Last Supper: "This cup is the new testament in my blood" ([Luke 22:20](https://www.churchofjesuschrist.org/study/scriptures/nt/luke/22?lang=eng&id=p20#p20)); "testament" there translates the Greek word for covenant. Jewish readers have their own long tradition of reading this promise as covenant renewal for Israel. Both deserve respect.

*Discuss:* What does it mean that the new covenant grows out of God's faithfulness in a broken covenant rather than His abandonment of it?

### Jeremiah 31:33–34 — The Law Written on the Heart

"I will put my law in their inward parts, and write it in their hearts." At Sinai, the law was written on stone ([Exodus 31:18](https://www.churchofjesuschrist.org/study/scriptures/ot/ex/31?lang=eng&id=p18#p18)). Earlier, Jeremiah said Judah's sin was "graven upon the table of their heart" with "a pen of iron" ([Jeremiah 17:1](https://www.churchofjesuschrist.org/study/scriptures/ot/jer/17?lang=eng&id=p1#p1)). The new covenant writes God's law where sin had been engraved.

The covenant formula follows: "will be their God, and they shall be my people." Verse 34 promises that people will no longer need to urge each other, "Know the LORD," because "they shall all know me, from the least of them unto the greatest." The basis is forgiveness: "for I will forgive their iniquity, and I will remember their sin no more." Knowing God grows out of being forgiven by Him.

Readings of the new covenant:

- **The official lesson** stresses that this covenant asks for more than outward devotion.
- **Scripture Helps** explains "new" as the fulness of the gospel revealed anew in the last days.
- **The Guide to the Scriptures** ([New and Everlasting Covenant](https://www.churchofjesuschrist.org/study/scriptures/gs/new-and-everlasting-covenant?lang=eng)) calls it the fulness of the gospel ([D&C 66:2](https://www.churchofjesuschrist.org/study/scriptures/dc-testament/dc/66?lang=eng&id=p2#p2)). It is new each time it is restored after apostasy and everlasting because it is God's covenant across dispensations. It includes priesthood ordinances such as baptism and temple marriage.
- **Book of Mormon covenant.** Baptism itself is a covenant to serve God and keep His commandments ([Mosiah 18:10, 13](https://www.churchofjesuschrist.org/study/scriptures/bofm/mosiah/18?lang=eng&id=p10#p10)).
- **Hebrews.** The New Testament quotes this passage ([Hebrews 8:6–13](https://www.churchofjesuschrist.org/study/scriptures/nt/heb/8?lang=eng&id=p6-p13#p6); [10:16–17](https://www.churchofjesuschrist.org/study/scriptures/nt/heb/10?lang=eng&id=p16-p17#p16)).
- **Paul** describes a letter written "not in tables of stone, but in fleshy tables of the heart" ([2 Corinthians 3:3](https://www.churchofjesuschrist.org/study/scriptures/nt/2-cor/3?lang=eng&id=p3#p3)).

Hebrews calls the first covenant "old," but that is an argument about how God's covenant works in Christ. It is not permission for contempt toward Jewish people. Paul insists God has not cast away His people ([Romans 11:1–2](https://www.churchofjesuschrist.org/study/scriptures/nt/rom/11?lang=eng&id=p1-p2#p1)), "for the gifts and calling of God are without repentance" ([Romans 11:29](https://www.churchofjesuschrist.org/study/scriptures/nt/rom/11?lang=eng&id=p29#p29)).

*Discuss:* What is one commandment you once obeyed mostly from outside pressure that has started to feel written on your heart? What helped that change?

### Jeremiah 31:35–37 — As Sure as Sun, Moon, and Sea

The Lord introduces Himself as Creator: the One "which giveth the sun for a light by day, and the ordinances of the moon and of the stars for a light by night, which divideth the sea when the waves thereof roar." The word "ordinances" here means the fixed decrees of nature.

Verse 36 builds an argument from them: if those decrees ever fail, "then the seed of Israel also shall cease from being a nation before me for ever." Verse 37 adds an impossible condition. Only if the heavens can be measured and the earth's foundations explored to the bottom will God "cast off all the seed of Israel for all that they have done."

Notice "for all that they have done." God is not ignoring Israel's sins. He is declaring that even those sins will not end His commitment.

The plain referent here is the seed of Israel. Christians may rejoice that the covenant blesses others through Christ, but should not read Israel out of a promise made explicitly to Israel.

*Discuss:* How can the regularity of sunrise and tide strengthen your trust in God's covenant faithfulness?

### Jeremiah 31:38–40 — A City Holy from Corner to Corner

The chapter ends with a surveyor's view of the rebuilt city:

- **From the tower of Hananeel** (compare [Nehemiah 3:1](https://www.churchofjesuschrist.org/study/scriptures/ot/neh/3?lang=eng&id=p1#p1)) to the gate of the corner.
- **A measuring line** goes out over the hill Gareb and around to Goath. Neither location can be identified with confidence today.
- **Then the shocking part.** "The whole valley of the dead bodies, and of the ashes" is likely the Hinnom Valley, once a place of child sacrifice and burial ([Jeremiah 7:31–32](https://www.churchofjesuschrist.org/study/scriptures/ot/jer/7?lang=eng&id=p31-p32#p31)). It, along with the fields to the Kidron and the horse gate on the east, "shall be holy unto the LORD."

Places of death and defilement are included in holiness. The last line returns to Jeremiah's call verbs: "it shall not be plucked up, nor thrown down any more for ever." Compare Zechariah's vision of "HOLINESS UNTO THE LORD" on ordinary horse bells ([Zechariah 14:20](https://www.churchofjesuschrist.org/study/scriptures/ot/zech/14?lang=eng&id=p20#p20)).

This passage should not be turned into a map of modern political boundaries. Its theological point is clear: God's restoration reaches even the most desecrated ground. As devotional application, many Christians see here the reach of Christ's redemption into the places we think are beyond saving.

*Discuss:* What "valley of ashes" in a life, family, or community might God still intend to make holy?

## Jeremiah 32: A Field Bought in a Falling Land

This chapter is a prose story.

- **The narrator** gives the setting (verses 1–5).
- **Jeremiah** tells the land purchase and prays in first person (verses 6–25).
- **The Lord** gives a long answer that confirms both judgment and restoration (verses 26–44).

### Jeremiah 32:1–2 — A Date Stamped in a Siege

The word came "in the tenth year of Zedekiah king of Judah, which was the eighteenth year of Nebuchadrezzar," around 588/587 BC. Judah's history is now dated by Babylon's calendar too, a quiet sign of who holds power. "The king of Babylon's army besieged Jerusalem." Jeremiah was "shut up in the court of the prison, which was in the king of Judah's house." This was a guarded courtyard in the palace complex, more open than a dungeon. Chapter 37 explains how he came to be there.

*Discuss:* What does it suggest that one of the book's most hopeful acts happens while the prophet is confined?

### Jeremiah 32:3–5 — The Charge Zedekiah Repeats

Zedekiah's accusation quotes Jeremiah's message back to him: God will give the city to Babylon, and Zedekiah "shall not escape out of the hand of the Chaldeans." He will "speak with him mouth to mouth, and his eyes shall behold his eyes." He will be led to Babylon "until I visit him," a phrase that can mean either judgment or attention; the text leaves it open. Finally: "though ye fight with the Chaldeans, ye shall not prosper."

The irony is sharp. The king can recite the prophecy almost word for word, yet he locks up the messenger instead of acting on the message. Knowing the words is not the same as hearing them.

*Discuss:* What prophetic counsel can you quote but have not yet acted on?

### Jeremiah 32:6–8 — Hanameel's Request and the Right of Redemption

Jeremiah now speaks in first person. The Lord tells him ahead of time that his cousin Hanameel, "the son of Shallum thine uncle," will come and ask him to buy a field in Anathoth. Anathoth was Jeremiah's hometown in Benjamin, a priestly town ([Jeremiah 1:1](https://www.churchofjesuschrist.org/study/scriptures/ot/jer/1?lang=eng&id=p1#p1)). Its people, including members of his own family, had once plotted against him ([Jeremiah 11:21](https://www.churchofjesuschrist.org/study/scriptures/ot/jer/11?lang=eng&id=p21#p21); [12:6](https://www.churchofjesuschrist.org/study/scriptures/ot/jer/12?lang=eng&id=p6#p6)).

"The right of redemption is thine to buy it." Under the law, when a family member had to sell land, the nearest relative had the right and duty to buy it so it stayed in the family ([Leviticus 25:25](https://www.churchofjesuschrist.org/study/scriptures/ot/lev/25?lang=eng&id=p25#p25); compare [Ruth 4:4–6](https://www.churchofjesuschrist.org/study/scriptures/ot/ruth/4?lang=eng&id=p4-p6#p4)).

Hanameel arrives "according to the word of the LORD." Jeremiah says, "Then I knew that this was the word of the LORD." Revelation was confirmed when the event matched it.

As typology, the near-kin redeemer foreshadows Christ the Redeemer ([Isaiah 59:20](https://www.churchofjesuschrist.org/study/scriptures/ot/isa/59?lang=eng&id=p20#p20)).

*Discuss:* How has the Lord confirmed a quiet prompting through later events in your life?

### Jeremiah 32:9–10 — Seventeen Shekels Weighed in the Balances

Jeremiah "weighed him the money, even seventeen shekels of silver." Silver was weighed rather than counted as coins. The text does not dwell on whether the price was high or low, so we should not speculate.

Then come the legal steps: "I subscribed the evidence" (signed the deed), "and sealed it, and took witnesses, and weighed him the money in the balances." Abraham's first purchase of land in Canaan was also silver weighed before witnesses ([Genesis 23:16](https://www.churchofjesuschrist.org/study/scriptures/ot/gen/23?lang=eng&id=p16#p16)). Here, at the far end of that history, a prophet buys land just as the people are losing it.

The care is the point. This is not a rash gesture; it is a valid, witnessed transaction.

*Discuss:* What is the difference between careful faith and impulsive bravado?

### Jeremiah 32:11–12 — Sealed Copy, Open Copy, Public Witnesses

There were two copies of the deed: "that which was sealed according to the law and custom, and that which was open." Practically, an open copy could be read for reference while a sealed copy protected the original terms from tampering.

Jeremiah gave both to "Baruch the son of Neriah, the son of Maaseiah." This is Baruch's first appearance in the book's order, though chapter 36 shows him working with Jeremiah years earlier. It all happened "in the sight of Hanameel," before the witnesses who signed, "before all the Jews that sat in the court of the prison." The prison courtyard became a public record office. Hope was witnessed by a community.

*Discuss:* Why might it matter that this act of hope was public, not private?

### Jeremiah 32:13–15 — An Earthen Vessel and a Long Future

Jeremiah instructs Baruch with the Lord's words: put the deeds "in an earthen vessel, that they may continue many days." Clay jars protected documents over long periods. Much later, scrolls stored in jars near the Dead Sea survived for many centuries. The instruction expects a long wait; Jeremiah had spoken of seventy years ([Jeremiah 29:10](https://www.churchofjesuschrist.org/study/scriptures/ot/jer/29?lang=eng&id=p10#p10)).

Verse 15 explains the sign: "Houses and fields and vineyards shall be possessed again in this land." This was a commanded prophetic sign, not general advice to invest boldly in failing markets or ignore prudence. It declared God's future.

As application, Paul's "treasure in earthen vessels" ([2 Corinthians 4:7](https://www.churchofjesuschrist.org/study/scriptures/nt/2-cor/4?lang=eng&id=p7#p7)) suggests that fragile containers can carry lasting promises.

*Discuss:* What promises from God are you "storing" for a future you cannot yet see?

### Jeremiah 32:16–17 — Prayer After Obedience: Nothing Too Hard

Only after he obeyed did Jeremiah pray. He begins, "Ah Lord GOD!", the same pained exclamation he used at his call ([Jeremiah 1:6](https://www.churchofjesuschrist.org/study/scriptures/ot/jer/1?lang=eng&id=p6#p6)). He affirms God's creative power: "thou hast made the heaven and the earth by thy great power and stretched out arm, and there is nothing too hard for thee."

This echoes the Lord's question to Abraham about Sarah: "Is any thing too hard for the LORD?" ([Genesis 18:14](https://www.churchofjesuschrist.org/study/scriptures/ot/gen/18?lang=eng&id=p14#p14)). Gabriel said the same to Mary: "with God nothing shall be impossible" ([Luke 1:37](https://www.churchofjesuschrist.org/study/scriptures/nt/luke/1?lang=eng&id=p37#p37)).

Jeremiah's confession of God's power is not the end of his prayer. It sets up his honest question.

*Discuss:* Have you ever obeyed first and then prayed about what you did not understand?

### Jeremiah 32:18–19 — Lovingkindness to Thousands, Justice to Each

Jeremiah draws on the language of Sinai: "Thou shewest lovingkindness unto thousands, and recompensest the iniquity of the fathers into the bosom of their children after them" (compare [Exodus 20:5–6](https://www.churchofjesuschrist.org/study/scriptures/ot/ex/20?lang=eng&id=p5-p6#p5); [34:6–7](https://www.churchofjesuschrist.org/study/scriptures/ot/ex/34?lang=eng&id=p6-p7#p6)). Shannon notes Exodus 20:6 as a key *ḥesed* passage.

Notice the proportion. Lovingkindness reaches "thousands," while consequences reach only some generations.

Verse 19 then insists that God's eyes "are open upon all the ways of the sons of men: to give every one according to his ways." The two ideas are held together:

- Consequences flow through families and societies.
- Each person is judged for their own conduct.

This verse cannot be used to blame children for their parents' sins, especially after Jeremiah 31:29–30.

*Discuss:* How do you hold together "consequences pass down" and "each is accountable for their own ways"?

### Jeremiah 32:20–23 — Remembering Exodus, Confessing Failure

Jeremiah recalls God's "signs and wonders in the land of Egypt, even unto this day." He remembers the Exodus "with a strong hand, and with a stretched out arm, and with great terror" (compare [Deuteronomy 26:8](https://www.churchofjesuschrist.org/study/scriptures/ot/deut/26?lang=eng&id=p8#p8)), and the gift of "a land flowing with milk and honey."

Then comes confession: "they obeyed not thy voice, neither walked in thy law; they have done nothing of all that thou commandedst them to do: therefore thou hast caused all this evil to come upon them." "Nothing of all" is prophetic overstatement for a pattern of covenant failure. Jeremiah prays as a member of his people, not as a bystander pointing fingers.

This is a national confession in a specific covenant crisis, not a formula for explaining any individual's suffering.

*Discuss:* Why would remembering God's past deliverance be both comforting and sobering?

### Jeremiah 32:24–25 — The Mounts at the Wall and the Field in the Hand

"Behold the mounts" are siege ramps being built against the walls. "The city is given into the hand of the Chaldeans," through sword, famine, and pestilence. "What thou hast spoken is come to pass; and, behold, thou seest it."

Then the question: "And thou hast said unto me, O Lord GOD, Buy thee the field for money, and take witnesses; for the city is given into the hand of the Chaldeans." Many translations render that last clause as a contrast: *though* the city is given. Either way, Jeremiah lays two things side by side that don't fit — God's judgment and God's command to buy land — and leaves the tension unresolved.

Faithful prayer can name a contradiction honestly.

*Discuss:* What contradiction would you like to bring honestly before God?

### Jeremiah 32:26–27 — The Question Returned

"Then came the word of the LORD unto Jeremiah, saying, Behold, I am the LORD, the God of all flesh: is there any thing too hard for me?" God takes Jeremiah's own confession from verse 17 and returns it as a question. "God of all flesh" includes Babylon, the exiles, and the generations to come.

God does not scold the question. He answers by restating His power and then showing, at length, what He will do with it.

*Discuss:* How does it feel when God answers a question with a question?

### Jeremiah 32:28–29 — Fire on Rooftop Altars

The answer begins with judgment, confirmed rather than cancelled. God will give the city to the Chaldeans and Nebuchadrezzar, and "he shall take it." The Chaldeans will burn the city "with the houses, upon whose roofs they have offered incense unto Baal, and poured out drink offerings unto other gods." Rooftops were used for household worship ([Jeremiah 19:13](https://www.churchofjesuschrist.org/study/scriptures/ot/jer/19?lang=eng&id=p13#p13); [Zephaniah 1:5](https://www.churchofjesuschrist.org/study/scriptures/ot/zeph/1?lang=eng&id=p5#p5)). The very houses where idolatry happened will burn.

The field purchase does not undo the judgment. Hope lies beyond it, not in place of it.

*Discuss:* Why might God confirm hard consequences before giving promises of restoration?

### Jeremiah 32:30–32 — From Youth to This Day

"The children of Israel and the children of Judah have only done evil before me from their youth." Both kingdoms are included, and "only" is strong prophetic indictment. "The work of their hands" points to idols. The city "hath been to me as a provocation… from the day that they built it."

Verse 32 lists who is responsible: "their kings, their princes, their priests, and their prophets, and the men of Judah, and the inhabitants of Jerusalem." Leaders are named first.

This is a collective covenant indictment. It does not deny that faithful individuals existed; Jeremiah, Baruch, and Ebed-melech are right there in the story.

*Discuss:* Why might leaders carry special responsibility in a community's covenant faithfulness?

### Jeremiah 32:33–35 — Back Not Face; Never in God's Mind

"They have turned unto me the back, and not the face," a vivid image of refusal (compare [Jeremiah 2:27](https://www.churchofjesuschrist.org/study/scriptures/ot/jer/2?lang=eng&id=p27#p27)). Yet God kept teaching, "rising up early and teaching them," an idiom for persistent, earnest effort.

Abominations were placed "in the house, which is called by my name" (compare [2 Kings 21:4–7](https://www.churchofjesuschrist.org/study/scriptures/ot/2-kgs/21?lang=eng&id=p4-p7#p4)). High places of Baal in the valley of the son of Hinnom were used "to cause their sons and their daughters to pass through the fire unto Molech."

Then God says, "which I commanded them not, neither came it into my mind" (also [Jeremiah 7:31](https://www.churchofjesuschrist.org/study/scriptures/ot/jer/7?lang=eng&id=p31#p31); [19:5](https://www.churchofjesuschrist.org/study/scriptures/ot/jer/19?lang=eng&id=p5#p5)). Child sacrifice was never His will; it was utterly foreign to Him. That rejection connects with Rachel weeping for her children (31:15) and the promise of good for children in verse 39.

*Discuss:* What does it teach about God that harm to children never "came… into [His] mind"?

### Jeremiah 32:36–38 — Gathered and Settled in Safety

"And now therefore" turns the passage sharply. The people say the city will fall by sword, famine, and pestilence, and God answers with gathering. "I will gather them out of all countries, whither I have driven them in mine anger, and in my fury, and in great wrath." The anger is not downplayed, but it is not the last word. "I will bring them again unto this place, and I will cause them to dwell safely."

Verse 38 restates the covenant formula from 31:33: "they shall be my people, and I will be their God."

*Discuss:* How does the word "therefore" in verse 36 surprise you?

### Jeremiah 32:39–40 — One Heart, One Way, an Everlasting Covenant

"I will give them one heart, and one way" (compare [Ezekiel 11:19](https://www.churchofjesuschrist.org/study/scriptures/ot/ezek/11?lang=eng&id=p19#p19)), so "that they may fear me for ever, for the good of them, and of their children after them." Here "fear" means reverent covenant loyalty, and it leads to good for children. That reverses the shadow over children in verse 18.

Verse 40 promises "an everlasting covenant," in which God says, "I will not turn away from them, to do them good; but I will put my fear in their hearts, that they shall not depart from me." God commits to both sides: He won't leave, and He will help them not to leave.

The official lesson pairs this verse with 31:31 as it asks about the "new" and "everlasting" covenant. For Latter-day Saints, that phrase is connected with the restored fulness of the gospel and its ordinances.

*Discuss:* How has keeping covenants helped you have "one heart" rather than a divided one?

### Jeremiah 32:41–42 — With My Whole Heart and Soul

"Yea, I will rejoice over them to do them good" (compare [Zephaniah 3:17](https://www.churchofjesuschrist.org/study/scriptures/ot/zeph/3?lang=eng&id=p17#p17); [Deuteronomy 30:9](https://www.churchofjesuschrist.org/study/scriptures/ot/deut/30?lang=eng&id=p9#p9)). "I will plant them in this land assuredly with my whole heart and with my whole soul."

Deuteronomy asks Israel to love God "with all thine heart, and with all thy soul" ([Deuteronomy 6:5](https://www.churchofjesuschrist.org/study/scriptures/ot/deut/6?lang=eng&id=p5#p5)). Here God gives that same wholehearted devotion to restoring His people.

Verse 42 balances the account: "Like as I have brought all this great evil upon this people, so will I bring upon them all the good that I have promised them." This is covenant restoration after exile, not a guarantee of quick prosperity to individuals.

*Discuss:* What changes when you imagine God working for your good "with [His] whole heart"?

### Jeremiah 32:43–44 — Fields Bought Again Across the Land

"Fields shall be bought in this land, whereof ye say, It is desolate without man or beast." God answers the people's despairing words with His own. Jeremiah's single purchase becomes a sign of many: "Men shall buy fields for money, and subscribe evidences, and seal them, and take witnesses."

The regions are named one by one:

- the land of Benjamin
- the places about Jerusalem
- the cities of Judah
- the hill country ("the cities of the mountains")
- the lowlands ("the cities of the valley")
- the Negev ("the cities of the south")

Hope is not vague; it has addresses. "For I will cause their captivity to return."

*Discuss:* What ordinary act of hope could you take this week that points to God's future?

## Jeremiah 33: Healing Promised to a Broken City

The word comes to Jeremiah a second time while he is still confined. The chapter moves from the city's present ruin to healing, cleansing, renewed joy, and covenants as dependable as day and night.

### Jeremiah 33:1–3 — Call unto Me

"The word of the LORD came unto Jeremiah the second time, while he was yet shut up." The prophet is confined, but the word is not.

God names Himself: "the LORD the maker thereof, the LORD that formed it, to establish it." The text does not say what "it" is. It may be the earth or the purpose God is bringing about. "Formed" uses potter's language (compare [Jeremiah 18:1–6](https://www.churchofjesuschrist.org/study/scriptures/ot/jer/18?lang=eng&id=p1-p6#p1)).

Then the invitation: "Call unto me, and I will answer thee, and shew thee great and mighty things, which thou knowest not." In context, the "great and mighty things" are the promised restoration that follows. The verse invites prayer and trust; it is not a license to chase secret or speculative revelation.

Compare [James 1:5](https://www.churchofjesuschrist.org/study/scriptures/nt/james/1?lang=eng&id=p5#p5) and [D&C 88:63](https://www.churchofjesuschrist.org/study/scriptures/dc-testament/dc/88?lang=eng&id=p63#p63).

*Discuss:* When has calling on God shown you something you could not see on your own?

### Jeremiah 33:4–5 — Houses Torn Down for a Losing Fight

The Lord speaks about "the houses of this city, and… the houses of the kings of Judah, which are thrown down by the mounts, and by the sword." In ancient sieges, defenders often tore down buildings to reinforce walls: "the houses have ye broken down to fortify the wall" ([Isaiah 22:10](https://www.churchofjesuschrist.org/study/scriptures/ot/isa/22?lang=eng&id=p10#p10)).

But the defense fails. They fight the Chaldeans only "to fill them with the dead bodies of men, whom I have slain in mine anger." The prophet interprets the disaster theologically, and God has "hid my face from this city." The passage does not soften this.

Readers can take the prophet's covenant interpretation seriously without assuming that every catastrophe in history carries the same meaning.

*Discuss:* How can we read scripture's hard theological claims honestly without applying them carelessly to modern tragedies?

### Jeremiah 33:6–8 — Health, Cure, Cleansing

"Behold" signals the turn. "I will bring it health and cure, and I will cure them." Earlier Jeremiah had cried, "Is there no balm in Gilead?… why then is not the health of the daughter of my people recovered?" ([Jeremiah 8:22](https://www.churchofjesuschrist.org/study/scriptures/ot/jer/8?lang=eng&id=p22#p22)). Here God answers that question. He will "reveal unto them the abundance of peace and truth."

Verse 7 restores "the captivity of Judah and the captivity of Israel," both again, building them "as at the first." Verse 8 promises: "I will cleanse them from all their iniquity… and I will pardon all their iniquities." It names sin three ways: iniquity, sin, and transgression. That is the same trio God promises to forgive in [Exodus 34:7](https://www.churchofjesuschrist.org/study/scriptures/ot/ex/34?lang=eng&id=p7#p7).

As a Christian connection, compare "the blood of Jesus Christ his Son cleanseth us from all sin" ([1 John 1:7](https://www.churchofjesuschrist.org/study/scriptures/nt/1-jn/1?lang=eng&id=p7#p7)).

*Discuss:* Why might healing and cleansing be promised together?

### Jeremiah 33:9 — A Name of Joy Before the Nations

The restored city "shall be to me a name of joy, a praise and an honour before all the nations of the earth." Nations that hear "all the good that I do unto them" will "fear and tremble for all the goodness and for all the prosperity that I procure unto it." They tremble at God's goodness, not at disaster. Jerusalem, once called a byword and curse ([Jeremiah 24:9](https://www.churchofjesuschrist.org/study/scriptures/ot/jer/24?lang=eng&id=p9#p9)), becomes a witness to grace.

As application, compare letting light shine so others glorify God ([Matthew 5:16](https://www.churchofjesuschrist.org/study/scriptures/nt/matt/5?lang=eng&id=p16#p16)).

*Discuss:* What does it look like for God's goodness, not just our good behavior, to be visible in a community?

### Jeremiah 33:10–11 — Voices of Bride and Bridegroom Again

"Again there shall be heard in this place, which ye say shall be desolate." The phrase "without man… without beast" repeats like an echo of empty streets.

Then come the returning sounds: "The voice of joy, and the voice of gladness, the voice of the bridegroom, and the voice of the bride." Jeremiah had warned three times that these exact voices would stop ([Jeremiah 7:34](https://www.churchofjesuschrist.org/study/scriptures/ot/jer/7?lang=eng&id=p34#p34); [16:9](https://www.churchofjesuschrist.org/study/scriptures/ot/jer/16?lang=eng&id=p9#p9); [25:10](https://www.churchofjesuschrist.org/study/scriptures/ot/jer/25?lang=eng&id=p10#p10)). Now they return.

Worship returns too: "Praise the LORD of hosts: for the LORD is good; for his mercy endureth for ever." This is the refrain of [Psalm 136](https://www.churchofjesuschrist.org/study/scriptures/ot/ps/136?lang=eng), and it was sung when the second temple's foundation was laid ([Ezra 3:11](https://www.churchofjesuschrist.org/study/scriptures/ot/ezra/3?lang=eng&id=p11#p11)). The word "mercy" is again *ḥesed*.

People will bring "the sacrifice of praise into the house of the LORD" (compare [Hebrews 13:15](https://www.churchofjesuschrist.org/study/scriptures/nt/heb/13?lang=eng&id=p15#p15)).

*Discuss:* What sounds of joy would mark restoration in your home or community?

### Jeremiah 33:12–13 — Shepherds Counting Their Flocks

The desolate land becomes "an habitation of shepherds causing their flocks to lie down" (compare [Psalm 23:2](https://www.churchofjesuschrist.org/study/scriptures/ot/ps/23?lang=eng&id=p2#p2)). Verse 13 lists the regions:

- the hill country
- the lowlands ("the vale")
- the Negev ("the south")
- Benjamin
- the places around Jerusalem
- the cities of Judah

This list closely matches the regions named in 32:44. Verse 13 then adds a picture of its own: "shall the flocks pass again under the hands of him that telleth them." To "tell" here means to count, so the shepherd counts each animal as it passes under his hand. Ordinary daily care returns.

As application, the counting shepherd brings to mind Jesus, who "calleth his own sheep by name" ([John 10:3](https://www.churchofjesuschrist.org/study/scriptures/nt/john/10?lang=eng&id=p3#p3)) and goes after the one ([Luke 15:4](https://www.churchofjesuschrist.org/study/scriptures/nt/luke/15?lang=eng&id=p4#p4)).

*Discuss:* What does it mean to you to be counted, noticed, and known?

### Jeremiah 33:14–16 — The Branch of Righteousness

"I will perform that good thing which I have promised unto the house of Israel and to the house of Judah." God will "cause the Branch of righteousness to grow up unto David; and he shall execute judgment and righteousness in the land." The image of a shoot from David's family tree appears in [Isaiah 11:1](https://www.churchofjesuschrist.org/study/scriptures/ot/isa/11?lang=eng&id=p1#p1), [Jeremiah 23:5](https://www.churchofjesuschrist.org/study/scriptures/ot/jer/23?lang=eng&id=p5#p5), and [Zechariah 3:8](https://www.churchofjesuschrist.org/study/scriptures/ot/zech/3?lang=eng&id=p8#p8).

Verse 16 has a detail worth noticing. "Judah shall be saved, and Jerusalem shall dwell safely: and this is the name wherewith *she* shall be called, The LORD our righteousness." Here the name belongs to the city. In [Jeremiah 23:6](https://www.churchofjesuschrist.org/study/scriptures/ot/jer/23?lang=eng&id=p6#p6), the same name is given to the king ("his name"). The community takes on the character of its righteous ruler.

There is irony too. King Zedekiah's name means "the LORD is righteousness," yet he failed to live up to it.

**Readings of the Branch:**

- **Official Church interpretation.** The official lesson calls the Branch the promised Messiah, and Scripture Helps identifies Him as Jesus Christ, descendant of David.
- **New Testament.** Paul speaks of Christ "made unto us… righteousness" ([1 Corinthians 1:30](https://www.churchofjesuschrist.org/study/scriptures/nt/1-cor/1?lang=eng&id=p30#p30)).
- **Jewish readers.** These verses have long nourished the hope of a righteous Davidic king.

*Discuss:* How does a community come to "be called" by the righteousness of its Lord?

### Jeremiah 33:17–18 — A Man for the Throne, a Man for the Altar

"David shall never want a man to sit upon the throne of the house of Israel" (compare [2 Samuel 7:16](https://www.churchofjesuschrist.org/study/scriptures/ot/2-sam/7?lang=eng&id=p16#p16)). "Neither shall the priests the Levites want a man before me to offer burnt offerings, and to kindle meat offerings" (grain offerings), "and to do sacrifice continually."

These promises were given while the Davidic throne was about to fall and the temple was about to burn. History raises questions: no Davidic king ruled after Zedekiah, and the temple sacrifices later stopped.

Readers respond in different ways:

- **Jewish tradition** continues to hope for the fulfillment of both promises.
- **Christians** see Jesus as David's heir (["the throne of his father David,"](https://www.churchofjesuschrist.org/study/scriptures/nt/luke/1?lang=eng&id=p32#p32) Luke 1:32). Hebrews explores His priesthood.
- **Latter-day Saints** also read [D&C 13](https://www.churchofjesuschrist.org/study/scriptures/dc-testament/dc/13?lang=eng), which looks ahead to the day "the sons of Levi do offer again an offering unto the Lord in righteousness" (compare [Malachi 3:3](https://www.churchofjesuschrist.org/study/scriptures/ot/mal/3?lang=eng&id=p3#p3)). Latter-day Saints do not practice animal sacrifice, and this guide does not claim that literal temple sacrifices continue in Church practice.

None of these readings should erase the promises God made to David's line and to the Levites.

*Discuss:* How do you hold God's promises when their fulfillment looks different from what you expected?

### Jeremiah 33:19–22 — Covenants of Day and Night

A new word comes: "If ye can break my covenant of the day, and my covenant of the night, and that there should not be day and night in their season." God promised after the flood that "day and night shall not cease" ([Genesis 8:22](https://www.churchofjesuschrist.org/study/scriptures/ot/gen/8?lang=eng&id=p22#p22)). Only then could His covenant with David or with "the Levites the priests, my ministers" be broken.

Verse 22 borrows Abraham's language of countless stars and sand ([Genesis 22:17](https://www.churchofjesuschrist.org/study/scriptures/ot/gen/22?lang=eng&id=p17#p17)) and applies it to David's seed and the Levites.

Jonn Claybaugh's 2022 [Interpreter study aid](https://interpreterfoundation.org/cfm-study-aids-old-testament-2022-lesson-43/) points out how this passage grounds covenant confidence in something anyone can observe: the steady return of day and night. Every sunrise preaches this promise. Lamentations 3:23 will say God's mercies are "new every morning."

*Discuss:* What daily rhythm could remind you of God's covenant faithfulness?

### Jeremiah 33:23–26 — The Two Families Not Cast Off

"Considerest thou not what this people have spoken, saying, The two families which the LORD hath chosen, he hath even cast them off?" The "two families" are likely Israel and Judah; verse 26 may suggest Jacob's line and David's line.

Some people were saying God was finished with them. God calls this contempt: "thus they have despised my people, that they should be no more a nation before them."

His reply returns to creation: if He has not set His "covenant… with day and night" and "the ordinances of heaven and earth," then He would reject "the seed of Jacob, and David." Since He has, He will not. He will still take rulers from David's line "over the seed of Abraham, Isaac, and Jacob: for I will cause their captivity to return, and have mercy on them."

This passage directly answers any claim that God has cast off His people. Paul asks, "Hath God cast away his people? God forbid" ([Romans 11:1](https://www.churchofjesuschrist.org/study/scriptures/nt/rom/11?lang=eng&id=p1#p1)). The Book of Mormon forbids Gentiles to mock or scorn the Jews ([3 Nephi 29:8](https://www.churchofjesuschrist.org/study/scriptures/bofm/3-ne/29?lang=eng&id=p8#p8)).

*Discuss:* How should these verses shape how Latter-day Saints speak about Jewish people today?

## Jeremiah 36: The Scroll and the Fire

Chapter 36 jumps back almost twenty years to Jehoiakim's reign, before the final siege. Placed here, between the promises of chapters 31–33 and the last days of Zedekiah, it shows how one king of Judah handled the written word years before the crisis arrived.

### Jeremiah 36:1–3 — Write It So They Might Return

In "the fourth year of Jehoiakim," about 605/604 BC, the Lord commanded, "Take thee a roll of a book, and write therein all the words that I have spoken unto thee against Israel, and against Judah, and against all the nations." The record would cover everything "from the days of Josiah, even unto this day," more than two decades of ministry ([Jeremiah 25:3](https://www.churchofjesuschrist.org/study/scriptures/ot/jer/25?lang=eng&id=p3#p3)). That same year Babylon defeated Egypt at Carchemish ([Jeremiah 46:2](https://www.churchofjesuschrist.org/study/scriptures/ot/jer/46?lang=eng&id=p2#p2)).

The purpose is hope: "It may be that the house of Judah will hear… that they may return every man from his evil way; that I may forgive their iniquity and their sin." Writing the warnings down is an invitation to repent, not just an archive of condemnation. "It may be" shows God really hoping for repentance.

*Discuss:* How do the scriptures function as an invitation in your life rather than just a record?

### Jeremiah 36:4–7 — Baruch Writes; Jeremiah Is Barred

"Jeremiah called Baruch the son of Neriah: and Baruch wrote from the mouth of Jeremiah all the words of the LORD." Then Jeremiah explains, "I am shut up; I cannot go into the house of the LORD." He likely means he was barred from the temple, not imprisoned, since the officials later tell him to hide (verse 19). His earlier temple sermons had caused serious conflict ([Jeremiah 26:7–11](https://www.churchofjesuschrist.org/study/scriptures/ot/jer/26?lang=eng&id=p7-p11#p7)).

So Baruch must go "upon the fasting day" and read "in the ears of the people," including all Judah coming in from the towns. Again, "it may be they will present their supplication before the LORD."

Baruch lends his voice. Faithful messengers often carry words they did not originate, at real risk to themselves (compare [Jeremiah 45](https://www.churchofjesuschrist.org/study/scriptures/ot/jer/45?lang=eng)).

*Discuss:* Who has faithfully brought God's word to you when you could not hear it any other way?

### Jeremiah 36:8–10 — A Fast, a Chamber, and a Public Reading

"Baruch… did according to all that Jeremiah the prophet commanded him." The occasion comes "in the fifth year of Jehoiakim… in the ninth month," early winter, when "they proclaimed a fast before the LORD to all the people." The text does not say why, but national fasts often marked emergencies.

Baruch reads "in the chamber of Gemariah the son of Shaphan the scribe, in the higher court, at the entry of the new gate." The new gate was where Jeremiah had been tried years before ([Jeremiah 26:10](https://www.churchofjesuschrist.org/study/scriptures/ot/jer/26?lang=eng&id=p10#p10)).

The Shaphan family matters in this story:

- Shaphan read the rediscovered book of the law to King Josiah ([2 Kings 22:8–10](https://www.churchofjesuschrist.org/study/scriptures/ot/2-kgs/22?lang=eng&id=p8-p10#p8)).
- His son Ahikam protected Jeremiah ([Jeremiah 26:24](https://www.churchofjesuschrist.org/study/scriptures/ot/jer/26?lang=eng&id=p24#p24)).
- Now another son's chamber hosts the reading of a prophetic scroll.

*Discuss:* How can families pass down a tradition of receiving God's word?

### Jeremiah 36:11–13 — Michaiah Carries the Words Down

Michaiah, son of Gemariah and grandson of Shaphan, hears "all the words of the LORD" and goes down to the palace scribe's chamber, where the princes are meeting. They include:

- **Elishama** the scribe.
- **Delaiah** son of Shemaiah.
- **Elnathan** son of Achbor, who years earlier had been sent to bring the prophet Urijah back from Egypt to be executed ([Jeremiah 26:22–23](https://www.churchofjesuschrist.org/study/scriptures/ot/jer/26?lang=eng&id=p22-p23#p22)).
- **Gemariah**.
- **Zedekiah** son of Hananiah, not the later king.

Michaiah "declared unto them all the words that he had heard." Three generations of one family pass the message along. Elnathan's presence shows how complicated these officials were; he will later plead for the scroll.

*Discuss:* Why might the narrator name so many officials?

### Jeremiah 36:14–15 — "Sit Down Now, and Read It in Our Ears"

The princes send Jehudi, introduced with an unusually long family line: son of Nethaniah, son of Shelemiah, son of Cushi. He brings Baruch with the scroll. The officials say, "Sit down now, and read it in our ears. So Baruch read it in their ears." The request is respectful; they want to hear the words for themselves, not just a report.

*Discuss:* What helps you "sit down" and really hear scripture rather than skim it?

### Jeremiah 36:16–19 — Fear, Questions, and Protective Counsel

When they heard everything, "they were afraid both one and other," looking at one another in alarm. They said, "We will surely tell the king of all these words." It was their duty.

But first they asked a careful question: "How didst thou write all these words at his mouth?" They wanted to know where the words came from. Baruch answered plainly: "He pronounced all these words unto me with his mouth, and I wrote them with ink in the book."

Then they gave protective advice: "Go, hide thee, thou and Jeremiah; and let no man know where ye be." They knew the king. Urijah had been killed for a similar message ([Jeremiah 26:20–23](https://www.churchofjesuschrist.org/study/scriptures/ot/jer/26?lang=eng&id=p20-p23#p20)).

These officials did their duty and also protected the messengers.

*Discuss:* How can we be faithful to institutional responsibilities while also protecting the vulnerable?

### Jeremiah 36:20–21 — The Scroll Stored, the Words Reported

The officials go to the king but leave the scroll "in the chamber of Elishama the scribe," perhaps to protect it. They tell the king its contents. The king sends Jehudi to bring the scroll, and Jehudi reads it "in the ears of the king, and in the ears of all the princes which stood beside the king."

The audience has grown: the people, then the officials, now the king and his court. Each level has a chance to respond.

*Discuss:* Why might the king insist on hearing the scroll itself rather than a report?

### Jeremiah 36:22–23 — Three or Four Leaves at a Time

"The king sat in the winterhouse in the ninth month: and there was a fire on the hearth burning before him." The scene is a cold month and a warm room. "When Jehudi had read three or four leaves" — sections or columns of the scroll — "he cut it with the penknife," a scribe's knife, "and cast it into the fire… until all the roll was consumed."

The text says "he," most naturally the king, whose hearth this was. Notice how deliberate it is. The king listens section by section and destroys each one in turn, until the whole message is gone.

Compare his father Josiah, who tore his clothes in humility when he heard the book of the law read ([2 Kings 22:11, 19](https://www.churchofjesuschrist.org/study/scriptures/ot/2-kgs/22?lang=eng&id=p11#p11)).

*Discuss:* What are subtle ways we "cut" parts of God's word we don't want to hear?

### Jeremiah 36:24–26 — No Torn Garments; Intercessors and a Hidden Prophet

"Yet they were not afraid, nor rent their garments, neither the king, nor any of his servants." The phrasing points straight back to Josiah's torn clothes. The court reacts the opposite way from the first officials (verse 16).

But not everyone agreed: "Elnathan and Delaiah and Gemariah had made intercession to the king that he would not burn the roll: but he would not hear them." Some officials did speak up.

The king commanded "Jerahmeel the son of Hammelech, and Seraiah the son of Azriel, and Shelemiah the son of Abdeel" to take Baruch and Jeremiah. The KJV gives Jerahmeel's name as "son of Hammelech"; he may have been a royal official or a prince, but the text does not identify him further. "But the LORD hid them."

The officials' advice to hide and the Lord's protection work together. This is protection, not a promise that the faithful are never harmed; Urijah was killed, and Jeremiah would later be beaten.

*Discuss:* When have you spoken up even though it didn't change the outcome? Why does scripture record such intercessions?

### Jeremiah 36:27–29 — Take Thee Again Another Roll

"After that the king had burned the roll," God simply says, "Take thee again another roll, and write in it all the former words." God's word does not depend on any one copy.

Jeremiah is to tell Jehoiakim: "Thou hast burned this roll, saying, Why hast thou written therein, saying, The king of Babylon shall certainly come and destroy this land?" The king's objection was to the content, the certainty of Babylon's coming.

Notice how little ceremony the Lord uses. There is no rebuke for the lost roll and no lament over it; the instruction simply begins, "Take thee again." The king's question, "Why hast thou written therein?", is answered by writing it again. "The word of our God shall stand for ever" ([Isaiah 40:8](https://www.churchofjesuschrist.org/study/scriptures/ot/isa/40?lang=eng&id=p8#p8); [1 Peter 1:25](https://www.churchofjesuschrist.org/study/scriptures/nt/1-pet/1?lang=eng&id=p25#p25)).

*Discuss:* Which teaching have you resisted mainly because it predicted something you did not want?

### Jeremiah 36:30–31 — The Burned Roll's Answer to the King

"He shall have none to sit upon the throne of David." This raises a question: Jehoiakim's son Jehoiachin did reign, but only for three months before being exiled ([2 Kings 24:6–8](https://www.churchofjesuschrist.org/study/scriptures/ot/2-kgs/24?lang=eng&id=p6-p8#p6)). The prophecy likely means no lasting, secure reign, which matches [Jeremiah 22:30](https://www.churchofjesuschrist.org/study/scriptures/ot/jer/22?lang=eng&id=p30#p30).

"His dead body shall be cast out in the day to the heat, and in the night to the frost." There is irony: the king who warmed himself at a winter fire will lie exposed to the frost. Compare [Jeremiah 22:19](https://www.churchofjesuschrist.org/study/scriptures/ot/jer/22?lang=eng&id=p19#p19). Kings does not narrate this, saying only that Jehoiakim "slept with his fathers" (2 Kings 24:6), and readers should acknowledge the gap honestly.

Verse 31 extends judgment to "his seed and his servants" and the people, "but they hearkened not." This is about refusing God's word. It is not a warning against honest questions or respectful disagreement in other settings.

*Discuss:* What is the difference between wrestling with a hard teaching and destroying it?

### Jeremiah 36:32 — Many Like Words Added

"Then took Jeremiah another roll, and gave it to Baruch… who wrote therein… all the words of the book which Jehoiakim… had burned in the fire: and there were added besides unto them many like words." The new scroll did not just copy the old one; it grew. Prophetic books took shape over time.

Notice who does the work. Jeremiah takes the new roll, but Baruch writes it again "from the mouth of Jeremiah," the same scribe who had just been forced into hiding. The king's fire cost the faithful their labor and their safety, and they paid that cost a second time.

*Discuss:* How has God responded to your losses by giving "many like words" rather than simply restoring what was?

## Jeremiah 37: A King Who Asked but Would Not Hear

Back in Zedekiah's reign, an Egyptian army brings a brief false hope. Jeremiah is arrested on a false charge, beaten, and confined. The official lesson invites readers to compare evidence that Zedekiah believed Jeremiah with evidence that he did not.

### Jeremiah 37:1–2 — A King Installed by Babylon; A People Who Would Not Hear

"King Zedekiah the son of Josiah reigned instead of Coniah the son of Jehoiakim." Coniah is Jehoiachin. Nebuchadrezzar made Zedekiah king (compare [2 Kings 24:17](https://www.churchofjesuschrist.org/study/scriptures/ot/2-kgs/24?lang=eng&id=p17#p17)), so his throne depended on Babylon.

Verse 2 gives the verdict in advance: "neither he, nor his servants, nor the people of the land, did hearken unto the words of the LORD." Lehi's call came in the first year of this reign ([1 Nephi 1:4](https://www.churchofjesuschrist.org/study/scriptures/bofm/1-ne/1?lang=eng&id=p4#p4)), and Nephi later says that Jerusalem's people had cast Jeremiah into prison ([1 Nephi 7:14](https://www.churchofjesuschrist.org/study/scriptures/bofm/1-ne/7?lang=eng&id=p14#p14)).

*Discuss:* Why might the narrator give the summary verdict before telling the story?

### Jeremiah 37:3–5 — "Pray Now unto the LORD Our God for Us"

Zedekiah sends Jehucal son of Shelemiah and Zephaniah the priest with a request: "Pray now unto the LORD our God for us." He wants prayer, not counsel; help, not change. Still, he calls the Lord "our God."

Verse 4 notes that Jeremiah "came in and went out among the people," not yet imprisoned. Verse 5 explains the mood: "Pharaoh's army was come forth out of Egypt," and the besieging Chaldeans "departed from Jerusalem." The city must have hoped deliverance had arrived.

*Discuss:* When have you asked God for help while hoping not to be asked to change?

### Jeremiah 37:6–8 — Egypt Will Go Home

The Lord's message to the king: "Pharaoh's army, which is come forth to help you, shall return to Egypt into their own land. And the Chaldeans shall come again, and fight against this city, and take it, and burn it with fire."

The relief is temporary, not deliverance. Jeremiah had already warned that the withdrawing army would return ([Jeremiah 34:21–22](https://www.churchofjesuschrist.org/study/scriptures/ot/jer/34?lang=eng&id=p21-p22#p21)).

*Discuss:* How do we tell a real answer apart from a temporary reprieve?

### Jeremiah 37:9–10 — Deceive Not Yourselves

"Deceive not yourselves, saying, The Chaldeans shall surely depart from us: for they shall not depart." The danger is self-deception.

Verse 10 uses vivid exaggeration: even if Judah defeated the whole Chaldean army and only wounded men remained, "yet should they rise up every man in his tent, and burn this city with fire." This expresses the certainty of this particular oracle. It is not a general rule about warfare or a reason to give up in every struggle.

*Discuss:* Where are we most tempted to tell ourselves comforting stories that God has not confirmed?

### Jeremiah 37:11–14 — Stopped at the Gate of Benjamin

While the Chaldean army was away, "Jeremiah went forth out of Jerusalem to go into the land of Benjamin, to separate himself thence in the midst of the people." The KJV wording is obscure. Many understand it to mean he went to take his share of family property among his people. The exact purpose is uncertain, though it may connect to his family land at Anathoth.

At the gate of Benjamin, Irijah son of Shelemiah, son of Hananiah, a captain of the guard, arrested him: "Thou fallest away to the Chaldeans." The charge was desertion. It was plausible given Jeremiah's message to surrender (38:2), but it was false.

"It is false; I fall not away to the Chaldeans." But Irijah "hearkened not to him" and brought him to the princes.

*Discuss:* How should disciples respond when a believable story about them is false?

### Jeremiah 37:15–16 — Beaten and Confined in Jonathan's House

"The princes were wroth with Jeremiah, and smote him, and put him in prison in the house of Jonathan the scribe: for they had made that the prison." It was an improvised jail in a private house. Verse 16 describes "the dungeon," probably a cistern-like pit, and "the cabins," small cells. Jeremiah "remained there many days."

These officials differ sharply from the more careful ones of chapter 36. Leadership had changed, and so had the climate.

*Discuss:* How do changes in leadership affect how a community treats truth-tellers?

### Jeremiah 37:17–19 — "Is There Any Word from the LORD?" "There Is."

Zedekiah "sent, and took him out" and asked him "secretly in his house," "Is there any word from the LORD?" Jeremiah's answer is short and fearless: "There is: for, said he, thou shalt be delivered into the hand of the king of Babylon." Bruised from prison, he does not soften it.

Then he asks for justice: "What have I offended against thee… that ye have put me in prison?" He presses further: "Where are now your prophets which prophesied unto you, saying, The king of Babylon shall not come against you?" The question answers itself. The king of Babylon has come, the comforting prophets are nowhere in the room, and the one who told the truth has been beaten and jailed for it.

*Discuss:* What does Zedekiah's secret question reveal about what he believed?

### Jeremiah 37:20–21 — An Honest Plea and Daily Bread

Jeremiah pleads respectfully: "Hear now, I pray thee, O my lord the king… that thou cause me not to return to the house of Jonathan the scribe, lest I die there." Asking for safety is not a lack of faith. Jesus withdrew from danger when His time had not come ([Luke 4:29–30](https://www.churchofjesuschrist.org/study/scriptures/nt/luke/4?lang=eng&id=p29-p30#p29)), and Paul appealed to Caesar ([Acts 25:11](https://www.churchofjesuschrist.org/study/scriptures/nt/acts/25?lang=eng&id=p11#p11)).

Zedekiah moves him to "the court of the prison" and orders that he receive "daily a piece of bread out of the bakers' street, until all the bread in the city were spent." It is a small mercy in a starving city. The phrase anticipates the famine (38:9).

As application, compare "Give us this day our daily bread" ([Matthew 6:11](https://www.churchofjesuschrist.org/study/scriptures/nt/matt/6?lang=eng&id=p11#p11)).

*Discuss:* Why might it matter that scripture records a prophet asking for protection?

## Jeremiah 38: The Cistern and the Courtier

Officials try to kill Jeremiah. The king gives way. A foreign court official rescues him. In a final secret meeting, Zedekiah again hears the choice before him and is again held back by fear.

### Jeremiah 38:1–3 — Four Officials Hear the Message

Four officials heard Jeremiah's words "unto all the people":

- **Shephatiah** son of Mattan.
- **Gedaliah** son of Pashur.
- **Jucal** son of Shelemiah, probably the same Jehucal sent in 37:3, though certainty isn't possible.
- **Pashur** son of Malchiah, the same official named in [Jeremiah 21:1](https://www.churchofjesuschrist.org/study/scriptures/ot/jer/21?lang=eng&id=p1#p1). He is a different man from Pashur son of Immer, who put Jeremiah in the stocks ([Jeremiah 20:1–2](https://www.churchofjesuschrist.org/study/scriptures/ot/jer/20?lang=eng&id=p1-p2#p1)).

This Gedaliah is also a different man from Gedaliah son of Ahikam in 39:14.

The message: whoever stays in the city "shall die by the sword, by the famine, and by the pestilence: but he that goeth forth to the Chaldeans shall live; for he shall have his life for a prey." Life is the only spoil they will escape with. "This city shall surely be given."

*Discuss:* Why is it important to keep similar names distinct when reading scripture?

### Jeremiah 38:4–6 — Into the Mire

The princes demand, "let this man be put to death: for thus he weakeneth the hands of the men of war… this man seeketh not the welfare of this people, but the hurt." The irony is that Jeremiah had told exiles to "seek the peace" of their city ([Jeremiah 29:7](https://www.churchofjesuschrist.org/study/scriptures/ot/jer/29?lang=eng&id=p7#p7)). His counsel aimed at saving lives.

Zedekiah gives up: "Behold, he is in your hand: for the king is not he that can do any thing against you." His passivity makes him complicit.

They "cast him into the dungeon of Malchiah the son of Hammelech… and they let down Jeremiah with cords. And in the dungeon there was no water, but mire: so Jeremiah sunk in the mire." This echoes Joseph's pit, where "there was no water in it" ([Genesis 37:24](https://www.churchofjesuschrist.org/study/scriptures/ot/gen/37?lang=eng&id=p24#p24)), and the psalmist's "I sink in deep mire" ([Psalm 69:2](https://www.churchofjesuschrist.org/study/scriptures/ot/ps/69?lang=eng&id=p2#p2)).

*Discuss:* When do leaders "go along" with wrongdoing by claiming they can do nothing?

### Jeremiah 38:7–9 — Ebed-melech Speaks Up

Ebed-melech, whose name means "servant of the king," was "the Ethiopian," a Cushite from the Nile kingdom south of Egypt. He was "one of the eunuchs which was in the king's house." The term can mean a court official generally, and it can also mean a castrated man. Either way, he was a palace insider of foreign origin whose position depended on the king.

He heard what happened while "the king then sitting in the gate of Benjamin," a public place of judgment. He went out and spoke openly: "My lord the king, these men have done evil in all that they have done to Jeremiah the prophet." He named the wrong directly. He also stated the danger: Jeremiah "is like to die for hunger… for there is no more bread in the city."

The official lesson asks what impresses us about Ebed-melech and how we might follow his example. Scripture welcomes eunuchs who hold to God's covenant with "an everlasting name" ([Isaiah 56:3–5](https://www.churchofjesuschrist.org/study/scriptures/ot/isa/56?lang=eng&id=p3-p5#p3)). Another Ethiopian court official later receives the gospel ([Acts 8:27](https://www.churchofjesuschrist.org/study/scriptures/nt/acts/8?lang=eng&id=p27#p27)).

*Discuss:* What access or influence do you have that could protect someone vulnerable?

### Jeremiah 38:10–13 — Thirty Men and Old Rags

The king reverses himself: "Take from hence thirty men with thee, and take up Jeremiah the prophet out of the dungeon, before he die." Some modern translations read "three" here. The KJV, following the received Hebrew text, reads "thirty," and this guide follows the KJV. A party of thirty may have provided protection against interference from the princes.

Ebed-melech went "into the house of the king under the treasury, and took thence old cast clouts and old rotten rags." He lowered them by cords and told Jeremiah, "Put now these… under thine armholes under the cords." This was practical kindness: padding so the ropes would not cut into a weakened man as he was pulled out of the mud. "Jeremiah did so."

They drew him up, "and Jeremiah remained in the court of the prison." The rescue was real but partial. He was saved, not freed.

As application, compare the Samaritan who "bound up his wounds" ([Luke 10:34](https://www.churchofjesuschrist.org/study/scriptures/nt/luke/10?lang=eng&id=p34#p34)).

*Discuss:* What "old rags" of practical care could make someone's rescue gentler?

### Jeremiah 38:14–16 — A Secret Meeting at the Third Entry

Zedekiah brings Jeremiah to "the third entry that is in the house of the LORD" and says, "I will ask thee a thing; hide nothing from me." Jeremiah answers realistically: "If I declare it unto thee, wilt thou not surely put me to death? and if I give thee counsel, wilt thou not hearken unto me?"

The king "sware secretly… As the LORD liveth, that made us this soul, I will not put thee to death, neither will I give thee into the hand of these men that seek thy life." The oath protects the messenger. It does not commit the king to obey the message.

*Discuss:* Why is promising safety to a messenger different from committing to act on the message?

### Jeremiah 38:17–18 — Two Roads Laid Out

The message is direct. "If thou wilt assuredly go forth unto the king of Babylon's princes, then thy soul shall live, and this city shall not be burned with fire; and thou shalt live, and thine house." If not, the city "shall… be given into the hand of the Chaldeans, and they shall burn it with fire, and thou shalt not escape."

Even this late, a merciful option remains open. This was specific counsel in a specific crisis, not a general rule that people should always surrender to oppressors.

*Discuss:* Why might God keep offering a better path even when the outcome seems settled?

### Jeremiah 38:19–20 — Afraid of the Mockers

Zedekiah admits the real obstacle: "I am afraid of the Jews that are fallen to the Chaldeans, lest they deliver me into their hand, and they mock me." He fears humiliation from his own countrymen who had already surrendered. "The fear of man bringeth a snare" ([Proverbs 29:25](https://www.churchofjesuschrist.org/study/scriptures/ot/prov/29?lang=eng&id=p25#p25)).

Jeremiah answers gently: "They shall not deliver thee. Obey, I beseech thee, the voice of the LORD… so it shall be well unto thee, and thy soul shall live." He does not mock the king's fear or call it foolish. He answers it with a promise and a plea, and "I beseech thee" shows a prophet pleading with the very king who had let him be thrown into the mire.

*Discuss:* Whose opinion do you most fear when obeying God? How could you face that fear?

### Jeremiah 38:21–23 — The Women's Song: Feet Sunk in the Mire

If Zedekiah refuses, Jeremiah describes what God has shown him: the women left in the palace will be brought to Babylon's princes. They will sing a taunting lament: "Thy friends have set thee on, and have prevailed against thee: thy feet are sunk in the mire, and they are turned away back."

This echoes 38:6. The king who let the prophet sink into mud will himself be stuck, abandoned by the advisers who pushed him.

Verse 23 adds that his wives and children will be handed over and "thou shalt cause this city to be burned with fire." Responsibility falls on the king.

These women are victims of war's brutality. The text describes an awful consequence of the king's choices. It does not approve of their mistreatment or blame them.

*Discuss:* How does the "mire" image connect Jeremiah's suffering with Zedekiah's choices?

### Jeremiah 38:24–26 — "Let No Man Know of These Words"

Zedekiah's concern is secrecy: "Let no man know of these words, and thou shalt not die." He anticipates that the princes will question Jeremiah. He tells Jeremiah to say, "I presented my supplication before the king, that he would not cause me to return to Jonathan's house, to die there." Jeremiah had in fact made exactly that request (37:20).

The king's priority is protecting himself, even after hearing the word of the Lord.

*Discuss:* What does Zedekiah's concern with secrecy show about where his fear lay?

### Jeremiah 38:27–28 — Silence at the Siege's End

"Then came all the princes unto Jeremiah, and asked him: and he told them according to all these words that the king had commanded. So they left off speaking with him; for the matter was not perceived." Jeremiah shared a true part of the conversation with hostile officials who wanted him dead.

Readers may discuss how this compares with other scriptural cases of protective non-disclosure (compare [Exodus 1:17–19](https://www.churchofjesuschrist.org/study/scriptures/ot/ex/1?lang=eng&id=p17-p19#p17)). It is not a general license for deception.

"So Jeremiah abode in the court of the prison until the day that Jerusalem was taken."

*Discuss:* How do honesty and protecting life interact in this scene?

## Jeremiah 39: The City Falls

The warnings come true. This chapter is short, violent, and precise. It also preserves two small acts of protection, for Jeremiah and for Ebed-melech, and they should be read with neither triumph nor distance.

### Jeremiah 39:1–3 — Siege, Breach, and Babylonian Princes in the Gate

"In the ninth year of Zedekiah… in the tenth month, came Nebuchadrezzar… and they besieged it." "In the eleventh year… in the fourth month, the ninth day of the month, the city was broken up." That is about a year and a half, with an approximate dating. The breach is different from the burning of the temple, which happened in the fifth month ([Jeremiah 52:12–13](https://www.churchofjesuschrist.org/study/scriptures/ot/jer/52?lang=eng&id=p12-p13#p12); [2 Kings 25:8–9](https://www.churchofjesuschrist.org/study/scriptures/ot/2-kgs/25?lang=eng&id=p8-p9#p8)).

"All the princes of the king of Babylon came in, and sat in the middle gate." Some of the listed "names," such as Rab-saris and Rab-mag, are likely titles of high officials. Sitting in the gate meant taking the city's seat of government. It fulfills Jeremiah's call vision: "they shall set every one his throne at the entering of the gates of Jerusalem" ([Jeremiah 1:15](https://www.churchofjesuschrist.org/study/scriptures/ot/jer/1?lang=eng&id=p15#p15)).

*Discuss:* Why might the narrator give such exact dates for the disaster?

### Jeremiah 39:4–5 — Night Flight to the Plains of Jericho

When Zedekiah and his soldiers "saw them," they "fled, and went forth out of the city by night, by the way of the king's garden, by the gate betwixt the two walls," heading toward the Jordan valley. Ezekiel had foreseen a prince slipping out at dusk ([Ezekiel 12:12](https://www.churchofjesuschrist.org/study/scriptures/ot/ezek/12?lang=eng&id=p12#p12)).

The Chaldeans caught him "in the plains of Jericho," near where Israel first entered the land ([Joshua 6](https://www.churchofjesuschrist.org/study/scriptures/ot/josh/6?lang=eng)). They brought him to Nebuchadnezzar "to Riblah in the land of Hamath," in Syria, "where he gave judgment upon him." The two spellings of the Babylonian king's name appear side by side in Jeremiah.

*Discuss:* What can we learn from a flight that runs away from God's counsel rather than toward it?

### Jeremiah 39:6–7 — Eyes That Saw, Then Saw No More

"The king of Babylon slew the sons of Zedekiah in Riblah before his eyes: also the king of Babylon slew all the nobles of Judah. Moreover he put out Zedekiah's eyes, and bound him with chains, to carry him to Babylon."

This is horrific imperial violence. The prophetic writings explain why Judah fell, but they never make Babylon's cruelty good.

Jeremiah had said Zedekiah's eyes would behold the king of Babylon's eyes (32:4; [34:3](https://www.churchofjesuschrist.org/study/scriptures/ot/jer/34?lang=eng&id=p3#p3)). At Riblah that word came true in the most painful way. The last things those eyes saw were the king of Babylon and the deaths of his own sons; then they saw nothing more, and he was bound in chains for the road to Babylon.

**About Mulek.** The Book of Mormon says one son of Zedekiah, Mulek, survived ([Helaman 8:21](https://www.churchofjesuschrist.org/study/scriptures/bofm/hel/8?lang=eng&id=p21#p21); [Mosiah 25:2](https://www.churchofjesuschrist.org/study/scriptures/bofm/mosiah/25?lang=eng&id=p2#p2); [Omni 1:15](https://www.churchofjesuschrist.org/study/scriptures/bofm/omni/1?lang=eng&id=p15#p15)). Jeremiah 39 narrates the sons slain at Riblah and names no survivor. These are separate scriptural witnesses, and this guide keeps the Book of Mormon claim distinct from the Bible's narrative rather than merging the two.

The official lesson invites comparing Zedekiah's end with Lehi's family, who obeyed a warning and left ([1 Nephi 2:1–4](https://www.churchofjesuschrist.org/study/scriptures/bofm/1-ne/2?lang=eng&id=p1-p4#p1)).

*Discuss:* How might Zedekiah's story have ended differently if he had acted on what he heard?

### Jeremiah 39:8–10 — Fire, Broken Walls, Exile, and Vineyards for the Poor

"The Chaldeans burned the king's house, and the houses of the people, with fire, and brake down the walls of Jerusalem." Nebuzar-adan, "the captain of the guard," took the remaining people into exile, including "those that fell away, that fell to him." Even those who surrendered went into exile, but they lived. That is what "life for a prey" meant.

Verse 10 notes that he "left of the poor of the people, which had nothing… and gave them vineyards and fields." Compare [2 Kings 25:12](https://www.churchofjesuschrist.org/study/scriptures/ot/2-kgs/25?lang=eng&id=p12#p12). There is a quiet echo of 32:15's "fields and vineyards," but this is no happy reversal. The poor were left in a ruined land under imperial control.

*Discuss:* How do you respond to the ironies of who keeps the land and who loses it?

### Jeremiah 39:11–14 — Jeremiah Released to Gedaliah

Nebuchadrezzar ordered Nebuzar-adan: "Take him, and look well to him, and do him no harm; but do unto him even as he shall say unto thee." The Babylonians may have known of Jeremiah's counsel to submit.

The officials "took Jeremiah out of the court of the prison, and committed him unto Gedaliah the son of Ahikam the son of Shaphan." The Shaphan family protects him once again (compare 26:24; 36:10–12, 25). This Gedaliah is a different man from the hostile Gedaliah son of Pashur (38:1). "So he dwelt among the people."

[Jeremiah 40:1–6](https://www.churchofjesuschrist.org/study/scriptures/ot/jer/40?lang=eng&id=p1-p6#p1) adds that Jeremiah was found in chains among the captives at Ramah before being freed. The book preserves more than one view of the release.

*Discuss:* What does it mean that faithful people sometimes receive protection from unexpected sources?

### Jeremiah 39:15–18 — A Promise to the Man Who Was Afraid

The narrative steps back to a word given while Jeremiah "was shut up in the court of the prison." "Go and speak to Ebed-melech the Ethiopian." God's words of evil on the city will be fulfilled "before thee." "But I will deliver thee in that day… and thou shalt not be given into the hand of the men of whom thou art afraid."

So Ebed-melech had been afraid. His courage did not mean he felt no fear.

"Thou shalt not fall by the sword, but thy life shall be for a prey unto thee: because thou hast put thy trust in me." This is the same phrase as 38:2, and it is promised to Baruch too ([Jeremiah 45:5](https://www.churchofjesuschrist.org/study/scriptures/ot/jer/45?lang=eng&id=p5#p5)). He gains survival, not riches.

This is a specific promise to one person. It does not mean every rescuer will survive; Urijah did not (26:23).

As application, compare "Inasmuch as ye have done it unto one of the least of these" ([Matthew 25:40](https://www.churchofjesuschrist.org/study/scriptures/nt/matt/25?lang=eng&id=p40#p40)).

*Discuss:* Why might God tell Ebed-melech specifically that He knows the men he fears?

## Lamentations 1: How Doth the City Sit Solitary

This is an alphabet poem in twenty-two verses. Two voices alternate:

- **A narrator** describes Zion in third person (verses 1–11, 17).
- **Zion herself** speaks, briefly in verses 9 and 11, then at length in verses 12–16 and 18–22.

The line "none to comfort" keeps returning (verses 2, 9, 16, 17, 21). Confession of sin, complaint about suffering, and protest against enemy cruelty all stand side by side.

### Lamentations 1:1–2 — The City Sits Solitary

"How doth the city sit solitary, that was full of people!" "How" is the book's Hebrew name, *Eikhah*, a classic opening for a dirge. The city is pictured as a woman who was "great among the nations, and princess among the provinces." Now she has become a "widow" and "tributary," forced into service.

Verse 2: "She weepeth sore in the night, and her tears are on her cheeks: among all her lovers she hath none to comfort her." "Lovers" here means political allies such as Egypt (compare [Jeremiah 2:36](https://www.churchofjesuschrist.org/study/scriptures/ot/jer/2?lang=eng&id=p36#p36)). "All her friends have dealt treacherously with her."

*Discuss:* Why might the poet picture the city as a grieving woman instead of describing ruins?

### Lamentations 1:3–4 — Captivity and Roads That Mourn

"Judah is gone into captivity because of affliction, and because of great servitude: she dwelleth among the heathen, she findeth no rest." This echoes Deuteronomy's warning that among the nations "neither shall the sole of thy foot have rest" ([Deuteronomy 28:65](https://www.churchofjesuschrist.org/study/scriptures/ot/deut/28?lang=eng&id=p65#p65)). Her pursuers caught her "between the straits," in narrow places with no escape.

Verse 4 makes the roads themselves mourn: "The ways of Zion do mourn, because none come to the solemn feasts." The gates stand empty. "Her priests sigh, her virgins are afflicted." Compare the dancing young women promised in Jeremiah 31:4, 13.

*Discuss:* What "empty roads" might mark loss in a family or community?

### Lamentations 1:5–6 — Adversaries on Top, Princes Like Starving Deer

"Her adversaries are the chief," the reverse of Deuteronomy's "he shall be the head, and thou shalt be the tail" ([Deuteronomy 28:44](https://www.churchofjesuschrist.org/study/scriptures/ot/deut/28?lang=eng&id=p44#p44)). "Her enemies prosper; for the LORD hath afflicted her for the multitude of her transgressions." This is the city's covenant confession. "Her children are gone into captivity."

Verse 6: "From the daughter of Zion all her beauty is departed: her princes are become like harts that find no pasture." They are weak deer running from hunters, a picture that recalls Zedekiah's flight in Jeremiah 39.

This national confession is not a tool for explaining an individual's grief.

*Discuss:* How can confession and grief exist together without one cancelling the other?

### Lamentations 1:7 — Remembering Pleasant Things

"Jerusalem remembered in the days of her affliction and of her miseries all her pleasant things that she had in the days of old." Memory hurts here. She remembers what she lost "when her people fell into the hand of the enemy, and none did help her." "The adversaries saw her, and did mock at her sabbaths." Enemies mocked her sacred days, or perhaps her ending. Compare exiles remembering Zion by Babylon's rivers ([Psalm 137:1](https://www.churchofjesuschrist.org/study/scriptures/ot/ps/137?lang=eng&id=p1#p1)).

*Discuss:* When does remembering good times bring comfort, and when does it deepen grief?

### Lamentations 1:8–9 — Shame Imagery and Zion's First Cry

"Jerusalem hath grievously sinned; therefore she is removed: all that honoured her despise her, because they have seen her nakedness." Verse 9 continues: "Her filthiness is in her skirts; she remembereth not her last end" (compare [Deuteronomy 32:29](https://www.churchofjesuschrist.org/study/scriptures/ot/deut/32?lang=eng&id=p29#p29)). She "came down wonderfully," shockingly, "she had no comforter."

Then Zion speaks for the first time: "O LORD, behold my affliction: for the enemy hath magnified himself."

These images of exposure use ancient metaphors for a city's public disgrace. They must not be read as justifying sexual violence or as implying that women are morally impure. The violence of war is atrocity. Zion's own cry gives her a voice and dignity in the poem.

*Discuss:* How can we read scripture's shame metaphors without passing shame onto real people?

### Lamentations 1:10–11 — Sanctuary Entered, Treasures Traded for Bread

"The adversary hath spread out his hand upon all her pleasant things," likely including temple treasures ([2 Kings 25:13–15](https://www.churchofjesuschrist.org/study/scriptures/ot/2-kgs/25?lang=eng&id=p13-p15#p13)). "She hath seen that the heathen entered into her sanctuary, whom thou didst command that they should not enter into thy congregation" (compare [Deuteronomy 23:3](https://www.churchofjesuschrist.org/study/scriptures/ot/deut/23?lang=eng&id=p3#p3)).

This reflects ancient rules about who could enter holy space, here violated by invading soldiers. It says nothing about any race being inferior. Isaiah foresaw God's house as "an house of prayer for all people" ([Isaiah 56:7](https://www.churchofjesuschrist.org/study/scriptures/ot/isa/56?lang=eng&id=p7#p7)).

Verse 11: "All her people sigh, they seek bread; they have given their pleasant things for meat to relieve the soul." They traded treasures for food to stay alive. Zion cries again: "see, O LORD, and consider; for I am become vile."

*Discuss:* What does it say that Zion keeps asking God to "see"?

### Lamentations 1:12 — "Is It Nothing to You, All Ye That Pass By?"

Zion turns to passersby: "Is it nothing to you, all ye that pass by? behold, and see if there be any sorrow like unto my sorrow, which is done unto me, wherewith the LORD hath afflicted me in the day of his fierce anger."

Originally this is the ruined city's complaint. As devotional application, Christians have long applied these words to Christ's suffering, adapting them in hymns and in Handel's *Messiah*. That use is meaningful, but it is a later reading.

The verse also challenges bystanders. Jesus' parable condemns those who "passed by on the other side" ([Luke 10:31](https://www.churchofjesuschrist.org/study/scriptures/nt/luke/10?lang=eng&id=p31#p31)).

*Discuss:* Whose sorrow are we tempted to pass by?

### Lamentations 1:13–15 — Fire, Net, Yoke, Winepress

Zion piles up hard images. God "hath sent fire into my bones." "He hath spread a net for my feet, he hath turned me back." "The yoke of my transgressions is bound by his hand: they are wreathed" (knotted together) "and come up upon my neck." This recalls Jeremiah's yoke sign ([Jeremiah 27–28](https://www.churchofjesuschrist.org/study/scriptures/ot/jer/28?lang=eng&id=p13-p14#p13)).

Verse 15 goes further: "The Lord hath trodden under foot all my mighty men." He "hath called an assembly against me to crush my young men." The word behind "assembly" is related to the word for appointed festivals in verse 4, a bitter reversal. "The Lord hath trodden the virgin, the daughter of Judah, as in a winepress."

The poet attributes the violence to God and does not soften it. Christian and Latter-day Saint readers may recall that the Redeemer Himself is pictured treading the winepress alone ([Isaiah 63:3](https://www.churchofjesuschrist.org/study/scriptures/ot/isa/63?lang=eng&id=p3#p3); [D&C 133:50](https://www.churchofjesuschrist.org/study/scriptures/dc-testament/dc/133?lang=eng&id=p50#p50)). That later imagery is theirs, not the poet's.

*Discuss:* Why might scripture keep such raw images instead of polishing them?

### Lamentations 1:16–17 — Eyes Running and Hands Spread Out

Zion: "For these things I weep; mine eye, mine eye runneth down with water, because the comforter that should relieve my soul is far from me: my children are desolate."

Verse 17 returns to the narrator: "Zion spreadeth forth her hands," reaching out or praying, "and there is none to comfort her." The Lord surrounded Jacob with adversaries, and "Jerusalem is as a menstruous woman among them." Under the law of Moses, menstruation brought temporary ritual impurity ([Leviticus 15:19](https://www.churchofjesuschrist.org/study/scriptures/ot/lev/15?lang=eng&id=p19#p19)). Here it serves as an image of a city shunned by its neighbors.

Scripture Helps clarifies that the symbolism reflects ancient purity laws and does not mean menstruation is sinful or shameful. Ritual purity status is not moral sin, and women are not morally inferior. Jesus healed and honored a woman with a twelve-year flow of blood ([Mark 5:25–34](https://www.churchofjesuschrist.org/study/scriptures/nt/mark/5?lang=eng&id=p25-p34#p25)).

*Discuss:* Why is it important to separate ancient purity categories from moral judgments?

### Lamentations 1:18–19 — "The LORD Is Righteous" and a Call to All Peoples

Zion confesses: "The LORD is righteous; for I have rebelled against his commandment." In the same breath she appeals: "hear, I pray you, all people, and behold my sorrow: my virgins and my young men are gone into captivity." Confession and complaint sit together in one verse. Neither is false.

Verse 19: "I called for my lovers, but they deceived me." Allies failed her. "My priests and mine elders gave up the ghost in the city, while they sought their meat to relieve their souls." Even religious leaders starved.

*Discuss:* Can someone admit wrongdoing and still cry out about the suffering they face? Why does that matter?

### Lamentations 1:20–22 — Distress Within, Sword Without, and a Plea for Justice

"Behold, O LORD; for I am in distress: my bowels are troubled; mine heart is turned within me." That is the same idiom Hosea uses for God's anguish ([Hosea 11:8](https://www.churchofjesuschrist.org/study/scriptures/ot/hosea/11?lang=eng&id=p8#p8)) and close to Jeremiah 31:20. The God whose heart turns over Ephraim is addressed by a people whose hearts are turned.

"Abroad the sword bereaveth, at home there is as death." There is war outside and famine or plague inside.

Verse 21: enemies "are glad that thou hast done it." Yet Zion trusts: "thou wilt bring the day that thou hast called, and they shall be like unto me." Verse 22: "Let all their wickedness come before thee; and do unto them, as thou hast done unto me."

This is a plea for equal justice placed in God's hands, not a model for personal revenge (compare [Romans 12:19](https://www.churchofjesuschrist.org/study/scriptures/nt/rom/12?lang=eng&id=p19#p19)). Jesus later taught, "Love your enemies" ([Matthew 5:44](https://www.churchofjesuschrist.org/study/scriptures/nt/matt/5?lang=eng&id=p44#p44)). The poet does not say that.

The poem ends with no comfort: "my sighs are many, and my heart is faint."

*Discuss:* What do we gain from scripture that ends with a faint heart rather than a resolution?

## Lamentations 3: New Every Morning

The speaker begins, "I am the man that hath seen affliction." He is an individual sufferer who represents his people. Tradition links him with Jeremiah, but the poem does not clearly identify him, and it should not be read as certain autobiography.

The chapter moves through several stages:

- **Verses 1–20:** relentless complaint.
- **Verses 21–39:** hope and wisdom.
- **Verses 40–47:** communal confession and protest.
- **Verses 48–51:** weeping.
- **Verses 52–58:** the pit and remembered rescue.
- **Verses 59–66:** a fierce plea for justice.

Each three-verse stanza below begins with the same Hebrew letter. The official lesson points readers to verses 20–33 for hope in Christ, alongside [Matthew 5:4](https://www.churchofjesuschrist.org/study/scriptures/nt/matt/5?lang=eng&id=p4#p4), [James 4:8–10](https://www.churchofjesuschrist.org/study/scriptures/nt/james/4?lang=eng&id=p8-p10#p8), and [Alma 36:17–20](https://www.churchofjesuschrist.org/study/scriptures/bofm/alma/36?lang=eng&id=p17-p20#p17).

### Lamentations 3:1–3 — The Rod, the Darkness, the Turned Hand

"I am the man that hath seen affliction by the rod of his wrath." God is not named until verse 18, but "he" is clearly God. "He hath led me, and brought me into darkness, but not into light." This reverses the shepherd's leading in Psalm 23. "Surely against me is he turned; he turneth his hand against me all the day." The suffering feels personal and constant.

*Discuss:* Why might it help some sufferers to know that scripture permits this kind of honesty?

### Lamentations 3:4–6 — Old Skin, Broken Bones, Dark Places

"My flesh and my skin hath he made old; he hath broken my bones." The pain is physical. "He hath builded against me, and compassed me with gall and travail." That is siege language, bitterness and hardship walled around him like ramps around a city. "He hath set me in dark places, as they that be dead of old." He feels as forgotten as people long buried.

The images describe distress without diagnosing any illness; grief and despair are human, not shameful.

*Discuss:* How can a community support someone who feels "as they that be dead of old"?

### Lamentations 3:7–9 — Hedged In, Chained, Unheard

"He hath hedged me about, that I cannot get out: he hath made my chain heavy." "Also when I cry and shout, he shutteth out my prayer." This is unanswered prayer, stated plainly. "He hath inclosed my ways with hewn stone, he hath made my paths crooked."

Compare Joseph Smith in Liberty Jail: "O God, where art thou?" ([D&C 121:1](https://www.churchofjesuschrist.org/study/scriptures/dc-testament/dc/121?lang=eng&id=p1#p1)). As a Christian connection, compare Jesus' cry from the cross ([Mark 15:34](https://www.churchofjesuschrist.org/study/scriptures/nt/mark/15?lang=eng&id=p34#p34)).

*Discuss:* What do you do with seasons when prayer feels shut out?

### Lamentations 3:10–12 — Bear, Lion, Archer

"He was unto me as a bear lying in wait, and as a lion in secret places." "He hath turned aside my ways, and pulled me in pieces: he hath made me desolate." "He hath bent his bow, and set me as a mark for the arrow." The speaker experiences God as a predator and an enemy archer. Scripture does not censor these images.

Keep in mind that this is a sufferer's honest perception, voiced within a covenant crisis. It is not a full definition of God's character, which the poem itself will soon complicate (verses 31–33).

*Discuss:* Why is it important that scripture includes perceptions that feel at odds with God's goodness?

### Lamentations 3:13–15 — Arrows, Mocking Songs, Wormwood

"He hath caused the arrows of his quiver to enter into my reins." The "reins" are the kidneys, the ancient seat of the deepest emotions. "I was a derision to all my people; and their song all the day." He is mocked by his own people. "He hath filled me with bitterness, he hath made me drunken with wormwood," a bitter plant.

As a Christian connection, compare the mockery of Jesus ([Matthew 27:39](https://www.churchofjesuschrist.org/study/scriptures/nt/matt/27?lang=eng&id=p39#p39)) and the gall offered to Him ([Matthew 27:34](https://www.churchofjesuschrist.org/study/scriptures/nt/matt/27?lang=eng&id=p34#p34)). This is typological resonance, not prediction.

*Discuss:* How might being mocked by one's own community add to suffering?

### Lamentations 3:16–18 — Gravel, Ashes, and Perished Hope

"He hath also broken my teeth with gravel stones, he hath covered me with ashes." The images are of eating grit and being pressed down into the dust. The address shifts to "thou": "thou hast removed my soul far off from peace: I forgat prosperity." He can no longer remember what good felt like.

Then the lowest point: "My strength and my hope is perished from the LORD." This is the first time God is named, and it is to say that hope from Him is gone.

*Discuss:* Why might the poet name the Lord for the first time in a statement of despair?

### Lamentations 3:19–21 — Remembering, and Then Recalling

"Remembering mine affliction and my misery, the wormwood and the gall." Some translations read this as a request: "Remember my affliction." "My soul hath them still in remembrance, and is humbled in me." The grief is still present.

Then the turn: "This I recall to my mind, therefore have I hope." Hope does not arrive by forgetting. It comes from inside remembered pain, through deliberately calling something else to mind.

*Discuss:* What truths do you deliberately "recall to mind" when grief is heavy?

### Lamentations 3:22–24 — New Every Morning

"It is of the LORD's mercies that we are not consumed, because his compassions fail not." "Mercies" is *ḥesed*, covenant loyalty, which Shannon lists among his key passages. "They are new every morning: great is thy faithfulness." "The LORD is my portion, saith my soul; therefore will I hope in him." A "portion" is an inheritance share, like the land given to tribes; the priests had the Lord Himself instead of land ([Numbers 18:20](https://www.churchofjesuschrist.org/study/scriptures/ot/num/18?lang=eng&id=p20#p20)).

Claybaugh's 2022 aid also highlights this hope. Connect it to Jeremiah 33:20, where day and night themselves witness covenant faithfulness.

This turning point does not instantly end the grief. Complaint will return.

*Discuss:* What does "new every morning" mean to you on a hard day?

### Lamentations 3:25–27 — Good to Wait, Good to Bear

All three verses begin with the same Hebrew word for "good." "The LORD is good unto them that wait for him, to the soul that seeketh him." "It is good that a man should both hope and quietly wait for the salvation of the LORD." "It is good for a man that he bear the yoke in his youth." Learning endurance early can build strength.

This is wisdom spoken in third person, a way of thinking that steadies the grieving speaker. It does not command anyone to accept mistreatment.

*Discuss:* How is quiet waiting different from passive resignation?

### Lamentations 3:28–30 — Silence, Dust, and the Offered Cheek

"He sitteth alone and keepeth silence, because he hath borne it upon him." "He putteth his mouth in the dust; if so be there may be hope." Note the tentativeness: "if so be." "He giveth his cheek to him that smiteth him: he is filled full with reproach." Compare [Isaiah 50:6](https://www.churchofjesuschrist.org/study/scriptures/ot/isa/50?lang=eng&id=p6#p6) and Jesus' teaching in [Matthew 5:39](https://www.churchofjesuschrist.org/study/scriptures/nt/matt/5?lang=eng&id=p39#p39).

These verses can illuminate patient endurance. They must never be used to require anyone to stay in abuse, to forbid seeking help or safety, or to silence lament. This very chapter cries out, appeals, and asks for justice.

*Discuss:* How can patience and protecting oneself from harm both be faithful?

### Lamentations 3:31–33 — Not Forever, Not from His Heart

"For the Lord will not cast off for ever." "But though he cause grief, yet will he have compassion according to the multitude of his mercies." "For he doth not afflict willingly nor grieve the children of men." "Willingly" can be read as "from his heart." Affliction is not what God desires.

These lines stand against the harsh images of verses 1–16 without erasing them. They are not a timetable, and they are not a claim that God personally causes every abuse.

Latter-day Saints may think of God weeping over His children ([Moses 7:28–33](https://www.churchofjesuschrist.org/study/scriptures/pgp/moses/7?lang=eng&id=p28-p33#p28)). Christ took on "pains and afflictions" so He would know how to help ([Alma 7:11–12](https://www.churchofjesuschrist.org/study/scriptures/bofm/alma/7?lang=eng&id=p11-p12#p11)).

*Discuss:* How does "he doth not afflict willingly" change how you read the chapter's earlier verses?

### Lamentations 3:34–36 — What the Lord Does Not Approve

"To crush under his feet all the prisoners of the earth, to turn aside the right of a man before the face of the most High, to subvert a man in his cause, the Lord approveth not." Crushing prisoners, denying people their rights, and twisting justice in court are all condemned.

This stanza makes clear that oppression is not God's will. It also gives sufferers language for naming injustice as injustice.

*Discuss:* How can these verses guide how we treat prisoners, people in court, or anyone vulnerable to an unfair system?

### Lamentations 3:37–39 — Sovereignty and Self-Examination

"Who is he that saith, and it cometh to pass, when the Lord commandeth it not?" "Out of the mouth of the most High proceedeth not evil and good?" Here "evil" means calamity, not moral wickedness. "Wherefore doth a living man complain, a man for the punishment of his sins?"

In context, the poet is affirming that God remains sovereign even over disaster, and calling his community to self-examination. These lines do not cancel the condemnation of oppressors in verses 34–36. They do not cancel the lament before and after them. And they do not license judging any present sufferer's guilt.

*Discuss:* How do you hold together God's sovereignty and the real responsibility of people who do wrong?

### Lamentations 3:40–42 — Search, Lift, Confess

The voice becomes "we." "Let us search and try our ways, and turn again to the LORD." "Let us lift up our heart with our hands unto God in the heavens" — outward gesture and inward devotion together. Compare [James 4:8](https://www.churchofjesuschrist.org/study/scriptures/nt/james/4?lang=eng&id=p8#p8): "Draw nigh to God, and he will draw nigh to you." Claybaugh's aid also points to these verses.

Then: "We have transgressed and have rebelled: thou hast not pardoned." Confession is spoken, yet relief has not come. The poem holds both truths.

*Discuss:* What helps you "search and try" your ways without despair?

### Lamentations 3:43–45 — Covered by Anger, Blocked by a Cloud

"Thou hast covered with anger, and persecuted us: thou hast slain, thou hast not pitied." "Thou hast covered thyself with a cloud, that our prayer should not pass through." The cloud that once showed God's presence ([Exodus 40:34](https://www.churchofjesuschrist.org/study/scriptures/ot/ex/40?lang=eng&id=p34#p34)) now feels like a wall. "Thou hast made us as the offscouring and refuse in the midst of the people," treated as trash.

Right after the hope of verses 22–33, the darkness returns. Healing is rarely a straight line.

*Discuss:* Why might the poem return to complaint after hope?

### Lamentations 3:46–48 — Open Mouths, Snares, and Rivers of Tears

"All our enemies have opened their mouths against us." "Fear and a snare is come upon us, desolation and destruction." The words are paired by sound in the original, and the pairs pile up.

Verse 48 shifts back to "I": "Mine eye runneth down with rivers of water for the destruction of the daughter of my people." The speaker turns from describing communal disaster to his own weeping for it. Compare Jeremiah's longing to weep "day and night for the slain" ([Jeremiah 9:1](https://www.churchofjesuschrist.org/study/scriptures/ot/jer/9?lang=eng&id=p1#p1)).

*Discuss:* What does it mean to weep for others' destruction and not just your own?

### Lamentations 3:49–51 — Till the LORD Look Down

"Mine eye trickleth down, and ceaseth not, without any intermission, till the LORD look down, and behold from heaven." The tears continue until God sees. Weeping becomes a form of prayer. "Mine eye affecteth mine heart because of all the daughters of my city."

Hilton's slides pair Jeremiah's tears with Jesus weeping over Jerusalem ([Luke 19:41](https://www.churchofjesuschrist.org/study/scriptures/nt/luke/19?lang=eng&id=p41#p41)). That is a meaningful typological link.

*Discuss:* Can tears themselves be a way of asking God to look down?

### Lamentations 3:52–54 — Hunted, Thrown in a Pit, Overwhelmed

"Mine enemies chased me sore, like a bird, without cause." He says he did nothing to deserve it. "They have cut off my life in the dungeon," or pit, "and cast a stone upon me." "Waters flowed over mine head; then I said, I am cut off."

Readers often think of Jeremiah's cistern (Jeremiah 38:6), though here the pit has water. As devotional resonance, some Christians think of the stone sealing Jesus' tomb ([Matthew 27:60](https://www.churchofjesuschrist.org/study/scriptures/nt/matt/27?lang=eng&id=p60#p60)).

*Discuss:* Why might it matter that the speaker insists he was hunted "without cause"?

### Lamentations 3:55–57 — Fear Not

"I called upon thy name, O LORD, out of the low dungeon." "Thou hast heard my voice: hide not thine ear at my breathing, at my cry." "Breathing" may suggest a gasp for relief. "Thou drewest near in the day that I called upon thee: thou saidst, Fear not."

Some translators read these verbs as pleas ("hear!") rather than reports. Either way, the speaker clings to an intimate memory or hope: God drawing near and speaking "Fear not." Compare [Psalm 40:1–2](https://www.churchofjesuschrist.org/study/scriptures/ot/ps/40?lang=eng&id=p1-p2#p1).

*Discuss:* When has God drawn near to you with "Fear not"?

### Lamentations 3:58–60 — Pleaded Cause, Redeemed Life

"O Lord, thou hast pleaded the causes of my soul; thou hast redeemed my life." God is pictured as an advocate in court and as a redeemer. Compare the kinsman-redeemer of Jeremiah 32:7, and, for Christians, Christ as Advocate ([1 John 2:1](https://www.churchofjesuschrist.org/study/scriptures/nt/1-jn/2?lang=eng&id=p1#p1)).

"O LORD, thou hast seen my wrong: judge thou my cause." "Thou hast seen all their vengeance and all their imaginations against me." He asks God to judge rather than taking revenge himself.

*Discuss:* What changes when you picture Christ as the advocate for your cause?

### Lamentations 3:61–63 — Heard Reproach, Mocking Music

"Thou hast heard their reproach, O LORD, and all their imaginations against me." "The lips of those that rose up against me, and their device against me all the day." "Behold their sitting down, and their rising up; I am their musick." From morning to night, he is the subject of their mocking songs (compare verse 14).

The plea is for God to see clearly what is being done. Naming mistreatment honestly is part of faithful lament.

*Discuss:* How can bringing specific wrongs before God be healthier than either silence or revenge?

### Lamentations 3:64–66 — The Final Plea for Justice

"Render unto them a recompence, O LORD, according to the work of their hands." "Give them sorrow of heart, thy curse unto them." "Persecute and destroy them in anger from under the heavens of the LORD."

The chapter does not end in calm. It ends with a fierce plea for judgment on persecutors. It should not be skipped, and it should not be dressed up as triumph.

The Gospel later teaches love for enemies ([Matthew 5:44](https://www.churchofjesuschrist.org/study/scriptures/nt/matt/5?lang=eng&id=p44#p44)), and Christ prayed "Father, forgive them" ([Luke 23:34](https://www.churchofjesuschrist.org/study/scriptures/nt/luke/23?lang=eng&id=p34#p34)). The poet does not say that. What he does is hand justice to God instead of taking it himself.

The chapter's hope (verses 21–33) is real. So is its unresolved ending. The faithful response to people living in such an ending is often to stay present rather than to explain. "Blessed are they that mourn: for they shall be comforted" ([Matthew 5:4](https://www.churchofjesuschrist.org/study/scriptures/nt/matt/5?lang=eng&id=p4#p4)).

*Discuss:* How can we stay with people whose stories have not yet resolved?

## Weekly Study and Discussion Plan

**Monday, October 26 — Jeremiah 31.**

- **Read:** the whole chapter.
- **Mark:** every place Israel/Ephraim and Judah are named.
- **Ponder:** 31:13 and 31:33.
- **Write:** one phrase of comfort and one covenant change you hope God will write in your heart.

**Tuesday, October 27 — Jeremiah 32.**

- **Trace:** the steps of the land purchase: word, visit, payment, deed, witnesses, jar, prayer, answer.
- **Try:** writing a "deed of hope." Record one promise of God you are trusting, date it, and keep it somewhere safe.

**Wednesday, October 28 — Jeremiah 33.**

- **Read:** verses 10–11 aloud, and listen for the returning voices.
- **Notice:** tomorrow's sunrise, and connect it with 33:20 and Lamentations 3:23.

**Thursday, October 29 — Jeremiah 36.**

- **List:** each person and how they responded to the scroll — the Lord, Jeremiah, Baruch, Michaiah, the princes, Jehudi, Jehoiakim, and the three intercessors.
- **Ask:** which response most resembles yours to a hard teaching?

**Friday, October 30 — Jeremiah 37–38.**

- **Make two columns:** Zedekiah's actions that show belief and his actions that show fear or unbelief.
- **Study:** Ebed-melech's actions closely: what he said, where he said it, and what he took with him.

**Saturday, October 31 — Jeremiah 39 and Lamentations 1.**

- **Read:** the fall slowly.
- **Track the voices:** in Lamentations 1, mark where the narrator speaks and where Zion speaks, and circle each "none to comfort."
- **Pray:** for someone who feels uncomforted.

**Sunday, November 1 — Lamentations 3 and discussion.**

- **Read:** the chapter in three-verse stanzas.
- **Mark:** the turn at verse 21, the "we" of verses 40–47, and the unresolved ending.

**A 45-minute class or family discussion outline:**

1. **Opening (5 minutes):** Read Jeremiah 1:10 and 31:28. Ask what it means that the same God who tears down also builds and plants.
2. **The field (10 minutes):** Act out Jeremiah 32:6–15 with simple props: a paper deed, a string seal, and a jar. Discuss what concrete acts of hope look like.
3. **The scroll and the king (10 minutes):** Read 36:22–25 and 37:17. Compare burning the word with asking for it but not obeying.
4. **The courtier (10 minutes):** Read 38:7–13 and 39:15–18. Discuss courage that coexists with fear, and practical gentleness.
5. **The lament (10 minutes):** Read Lamentations 3:19–26 and 3:64–66 together. Discuss how scripture makes room for both hope and unresolved grief.

**For families with children:** Let children lower a "rope" padded with a soft cloth to rescue a stuffed animal from a box, then read Jeremiah 38:11–13. Ask why the rags mattered. Read Lamentations 3:22–23 at breakfast and talk about what "new every morning" means.

**A note for teachers:** Some people in any group carry recent grief, family pain, or experiences of abuse.

- **Introduce Lamentations gently.** It gives permission to grieve.
- **Don't treat the national judgment passages as explanations for anyone's present suffering.**
- **Present Lamentations 3:27–30** as wisdom about endurance, never as a reason to stay in harm's way.
